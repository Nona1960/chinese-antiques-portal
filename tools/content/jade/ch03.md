# The History of Jade Working

## From which deposits was nephrite mined in antiquity?

Two main types of geological source of Chinese nephrite are known: sources associated with metamorphosed dolomitic marbles, and sources associated with serpentinites.

Research using scientific methods could resolve questions concerning the geological origin of nephrite in ancient China, as well as its mining and use. Analysis of early Chinese jades by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy indicates that the geological sources of the material used for these ancient jades were most probably associated with dolomitic marbles.

The geological sources of the nephrite used in the early cultures of China are at present unknown to scholars. Such sources may have been exhausted already in antiquity, since nephrite can occur in small, localised deposits.

Of particular interest is the content of iron and magnesium oxides in nephrite objects of the Neolithic period (the Hongshan, Liangzhu and Longshan cultures). Perhaps the geological sources for each culture were one or several related deposits. An unusually high content of iron (0.35–17.95%) and magnesium (0.02–0.89%) has been found in ancient jades of the Longshan culture. This suggests that the source of these jades was particularly rich in iron and manganese.

The nephrites used for carving in the Shang dynasty and the Western Zhou have a wide range of compositions and presumably came from different sources. Most of these early Chinese jades consist of fine-grained massive tremolite-actinolite.

In antiquity nephrite was quarried in the mountains by the primitive method of splitting hard rock with fire and drenching it with cold water.

Professor Yang Boda, who devoted several special studies to the "culture of jade implements", proposed that the "period of true jade" be counted from the beginning of the use of raw material from Khotan.

The scholar Edward Schafer likewise held that Khotan, and later Yarkand, were the sources of all the nephrite used in China, though he did not entirely exclude the possibility that local resources were used.

## Which scientific methods make it possible to study the working of archaic jades?

In China, more than 500 excavated jades from a wide variety of burials from the Neolithic to the Han dynasty have been analysed at the Chinese Academy of Geological Sciences. In the last decade such scientific methods as X-ray diffraction and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) have been used in these studies.

## How long ago did people begin to mine and work nephrite in China?

The Chinese have been working nephrite for more than 5,000 years — that is, long before they began to use metals. Some researchers believe that nephrite was used in China to make weapons, ornaments and ritual objects as early as about 7,000 years ago.

The earliest nephrite objects were discovered by archaeologists in the area of the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River and have been dated to the Neolithic (4000–2000 BC).

These are mainly cult objects, and they include the earliest known examples of ritual objects. Archaeologists have found many ceremonial axes and knives, various decorative circular and arc-shaped jade pendants, bracelets and necklaces, as well as a number of masks.

## What remarkable objects are found in the study of the Sanxingdui culture?

The most striking are the large bronze masks and heads with angular features, huge almond-shaped eyes, faceted noses and enormous ears — features that do not reflect an Asian appearance. The three largest masks have the most uncanny features of all the Sanxingdui artefacts: animal-like ears, monstrously protruding pupils and richly decorated bodies. The eyes of the ruler imply powerful sight; the large, butterfly-shaped ears, powerful hearing. Most of the carved stone objects of this culture are made of white Khotan nephrite.

Radiocarbon analysis has dated the artefacts to the twelfth to eleventh centuries BC. The ancient artefacts of Sanxingdui date from the end of the second millennium BC — the period of the Shang dynasty, which flourished in the Yellow River valley in northern China, thousands of miles from Sichuan province.

Most Sanxingdui artefacts are made of nephrite and are distinguished by skilful carving. The figures have bulging eyes and widely spaced ears. Some collectors and researchers have even regarded the Sanxingdui culture as derived from extraterrestrial civilisations.

### The history of the discovery of the Sanxingdui culture

Sanxingdui is an ancient settlement in the Yangtze basin near Chengdu, Sichuan province. It belongs to the late Neolithic and early Bronze Age (2800–800 BC). The settlement was enclosed by ditches and earthen ramparts. Its creators possessed an advanced technology of bronze casting and also built irrigation works.

In 1929 a local farmer digging a well discovered a pit containing jade objects. By 1986 about 2,000 copper and jade objects had been excavated. This event later led to the discovery of a mysterious ancient kingdom. Generations of Chinese archaeologists searched the area without success until 1986, when workers accidentally dug up pits containing thousands of artefacts. The objects had been broken, burned and then carefully buried.

The sacrificial pits are thought to have been the place where the ancient people of Shu made offerings to Heaven, Earth, the mountains, rivers and other nature gods. The human-like figures and bronze animal masks with bulging eyes may have been images of the nature gods worshipped by the Shu people. Particularly outstanding is the bronze figure of a standing man 2.62 m tall — the largest and best preserved in the world.

Sanxingdui covered an area of about three square kilometres. It was a large settlement for its time, with developed agriculture, including wine-making. The production of ceramics and sacrificial implements and the extraction of minerals were common activities. According to archaeologists, the inhabitants suddenly abandoned Sanxingdui for unknown reasons around 1000 BC, and this mysterious culture declined.

Among the Sanxingdui artefacts are gold and bronze masks and a gold staff bearing images of human heads, evidently a regalia of power. Many hundreds of cast bronze objects have also been found, including statues of people life-size and even larger, and large-scale sculptured human heads with various headdresses (evidence of a stable social stratification). Sanxingdui already had communities whose form of organisation went beyond the tribe.

## How are the working techniques of ancient artefacts studied in scientific laboratories?

Silicone moulds are taken from the ancient objects so as to avoid any risk of damage to the objects themselves, which will be studied under the microscope in high vacuum. The moulds are mounted on aluminium stubs and coated with a thin layer of gold to facilitate their examination by secondary-electron imaging in the high-vacuum chamber of the electron microscope.

Careful microscopic study makes it possible to reveal the features of the grinding and polishing of ancient jade artefacts.

The study of archaic jades by optical microscopy has proved that a rotary rigid saw (a disc saw whose cutting disc moves around a centre), pointed tools, tubular drills and lathes for shaping were used.

## What are the basic principles of jade carving?

Since antiquity the following sequence of technological operations has been traditional:

1. Unwanted material was removed by intersecting saw cuts.
2. In the course of roughing out, all the planes of the cuts were smoothed.
3. The sculpture was gradually given rounded surfaces.
4. The ornament was applied.
5. The sculpture was polished.

Excess material was removed during carving with abrasives. In rough shaping, the maximum amount of excess material was removed with a powerful saw.

Since carved objects always have hollows, many rotating tools, abrasive heads and the like were used to give them their final form. With these, surfaces of any curvature were created, and their small size made it possible to cut and finish the necessary hollows.

Having removed the maximum of unwanted material with coarse abrasive powder, the carver went on to work the object with fine-grained powder, bringing out finer details.

The final stage in making a carved object was polishing. Surfaces were levelled with wooden and leather wheels using loose abrasive, and polishing was done on similar wheels using polishing powders.

## What were the stages of jade working in ancient China?

**Stage 1. Pounding.** Abrasive sand (powder) was produced by this method. The sand could be black, red or yellow depending on the composition of the minerals crushed, and the type of sand was identified by its colour.

**Stage 2. Preparing the grinding slurry,** by mixing the abrasive powder with water.

**Stage 3. Opening the jade boulder.** The rock was cut with a frame saw while a slurry of black powder ran into the cut from a pot. The cut was made by friction. The rock itself was cut into slabs.

**Stage 4. Cutting.** The jade slabs were cut into cubes and rectangles with a sharp metal disc, the process being accompanied by continuous wetting with a slurry based on red powder.

**Stage 5. Preliminary grinding.** A steel rim (2–3 cm or 3–5 cm thick) and a red-powder slurry were used. At this stage only the edges of the jade blanks were ground.

**Stage 6. Grinding.** For subsequent grinding, six or seven kinds of steel discs were used, each 2–3 centimetres thick.

**Stage 7. Hollowing.** Special metal core drills were used to cut out the interior of the jade blank. The jade core was left in place, since its removal was later entrusted only to the most experienced master, using a special chisel. When vessels with a narrow neck and wide body were made, special core drills with a thickened end were used, and the interior was then widened with a special abrasive.

**Stage 8. Marking the design on the jade surface,** done with metal discs.

**Stage 9. Carving.** Openwork interior carving was done with a metal drill driven by the back-and-forth motion of a bow.

**Stage 10. Applying the pattern.** The jade object was fixed in a vertical or horizontal vice, and the pattern previously drawn on it was engraved with a bow strung with metal wire, while sand slurry was poured on at the same time.

**Stage 11. Drilling holes.** The jade blank was fixed in a board of suitable size and placed in a cup of water made from a section of bamboo. A metal drill was rotated by the back-and-forth motion of a bow to make the holes.

**Stage 12. Final grinding.** Large jade objects were ground until the characteristic lustre appeared, using a wooden disc impregnated with a water slurry of topaz sand or of other harder minerals. Small objects were ground with a dried bottle gourd impregnated with a similar slurry.

**Stage 13. Polishing** was done on a polishing disc covered with leather and impregnated with a water slurry of topaz powder.

## Which tool marks on the surface can be regarded as features of the primitive technology characteristic of antiquity?

In assessing the features of working, attention should be paid to:

1. **Sawing.** If the thickness of flat plates is absolutely uniform, this may indicate the use of modern diamond saws.
2. **How cleanly and neatly the small details are worked,** and whether there is any "rough carelessness" in the work. In antiquity nephrite was a very expensive material, and the ancient carver worked every detail with care.
3. **The surface.** In antiquity, careful grinding at slow speed with soft abrasives created a "soft, satiny" surface, which differs from modern polished surfaces with a strong gloss. The object must be studied carefully to establish whether there is evidence of the use of high-speed lapidary machines and diamond abrasives.
4. **Drilled holes.** This is an important diagnostic feature. Each period in the development of jade working has its own drilling characteristics.

## What is the algorithm for attributing ancient jades?

The attribution of archaic Chinese carvings must begin with a visual examination of the external appearance for stylistic analysis.

The next stage is the analysis of the features of working, in which the tool marks left on the surface of the carving are studied.

Visual diagnosis of primitive technologies includes the study of the slight "orange peel" effect on the surface of ancient jades polished with soft natural abrasives. In expert examination it must be remembered that a jade object may perfectly well be finished by hand with natural abrasives today.

## What symbolic meaning do jade carvings carry?

Nephrite has a special significance in Chinese culture. The Chinese call it the "stone of heaven" and believe that it is a symbol of good luck, health and happiness and that it drives away evil spirits. It guarantees a long life if carved in the form of a stork or a bat. Nephrite was used for opium pipes and mouthpieces, because the Chinese believed that its power prolonged the smoker's life.

Poets held that the sound of a struck nephrite pebble resembled in its melodiousness the voice of the beloved, and with characteristic Eastern floridity they called nephrite a "concentration of love". The ability of nephrite to produce pleasant sounds when struck was used in making a great number of lithophones (stone musical instruments). From 12 to 24 plates of different lengths were made into a kind of lithophone producing a particular melodic series; the pitch depended on the thickness of the plates. Such lithophones were used in ritual and religious ceremonies.

## What did the jade axe and knife symbolise in the Neolithic?

As the specialist in symbolism Bronislav Vinogrodsky explains: "In antiquity jade axes and knives appeared which were not used functionally but served to change and recut the situational structure of time. That is, if you feel that a situation is going the wrong way, that an undesirable change in the pattern of circumstances is taking place, you use such a knife or axe to take the situation apart."

A specific image was carved on ceremonial jade axes: a small face with two round eyes and a strongly curved nose or mouth, above which are two rows of incised lines. Such images are also found on ancient pottery.

Some scholars have suggested that these decorative motifs may have influenced the design of the *taotie* mask, which was popular on sacrificial bronzes of the Shang dynasty.

## Which Neolithic culture of ancient China is surrounded by an aura of mystery?

The Hongshan culture takes its name from Mount Hongshan; the name means "Red Mountain". The culture vanished thousands of years ago but left behind a number of quite astonishing jade figures that we can admire today.

The people of the Neolithic Hongshan culture inhabited the lands of Inner Mongolia between Liaoning and Hebei provinces about 6,500 years ago.

For many years Chinese scholars tried to determine which animals the Hongshan stone carvers depicted, and came to the conclusion that most of them are stylised pigs.

Like the members of many other ancient cultures, the Hongshan people built pyramids. In 2001 Chinese archaeologists discovered a pyramid-like structure built more than 5,000 years ago in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region in northern China. Located on a mountain ridge, it is a three-tiered stone structure more than 30 metres long and 15 metres wide.

The jade objects depict mainly birds and animals. The emphasis of the artefacts is on form: the design is simple and majestic. The double perforations ("holes") are as a rule easily recognisable. It is generally accepted that the dragon and phoenix motifs of the Hongshan culture are the prototypes of those of later periods in the history of Chinese jade carving.

The best-known objects of this period are the pig-dragon embryo, a symbol of fertility, and the eagle, regarded as the messenger of the will of heaven to people.

Jade artefacts with the appearance of an alien being deserve attention: a dragon's muzzle on a human body. Its four horns and two ears resemble the rays of the sun, which is why it is also known as the **Sun God**.

A cult of childbirth existed in the Hongshan culture, as the artefacts found also testify.

## What kind of nephrite was used in the Neolithic?

For the Chinese Neolithic as a whole, the use of Khotan nephrite is a rare exception. The cultures in which jade working reached its greatest development (Liangzhu, Hongshan, Longshan) turned mainly to local sources of raw material.

Besides nephrite proper, substitutes were often used, as well as so-called semi-nephrites — specimens containing both the nephrite-forming tremolite and actinolite and pseudo-nephrite minerals.

## What did the *bi* disc symbolise in ancient China?

The *bi* disc is a round disc of very various sizes with a central hole. It was regarded as an amulet and also as a symbol of Heaven and of the status and power of the ruling class.

The importance of owning *bi* discs was so great that in war the vanquished had to hand *bi* discs to the victor as a sign of submission.

The oldest *bi* discs, dating from the Neolithic (Hongshan and Liangzhu), are small and not carved at all; both sides are smooth.

Later, *bi* discs came to be decorated with ornament, cicadas, waves and images of various mythical animals.

In the Zhou dynasty the *bi* disc was perceived as a symbol of Heaven and the Sun.

It has not been proved whether the *bi* discs of later periods symbolise the Sun and/or Heaven, or eternal life (a circle without beginning or end).

The black *bi* disc was used as one of the most important funerary objects and accompanied the deceased to the grave, since only with the help of a *bi* could a connection be established between Earth and Heaven. This rite, however, was performed only for aristocrats. All the openings of the dead person's body were closed with *bi* discs of various sizes, which were placed on the back, on the chest, on the head, beneath the body, and to the right and left of it.

## What is the ritual object *cong*?

The *cong* is an unusual jade object consisting of a round tube with projecting square corners. It is made up of short segments with decorative profiles along its whole length.

*Cong* have been found in the tombs of people who held important positions in society. They were first found in graves of the Liangzhu culture in eastern Jiangsu province near present-day Shanghai. The *cong* was used for offerings to the ancestors.

The *cong* is a cylindrical tube enclosed in rectangular blocks symbolising *yin* (earth, woman) and *yang* (heaven, man). This object may be connected with shamanic rituals.

## When did ritual wine vessels first appear?

Ritual wine vessels were used in ritual ceremonies from the late Shang period to the middle of the Western Zhou dynasty. In form they resemble a sauce-boat with a curved lid and a strong handle. The whole vessel is decorated with carved images of animals.

## When did Khotan nephrite come into wide use?

The tradition of using imported Khotan nephrite continued in the Qijia culture. It was perhaps through the Qijia culture that nephrite from Xinjiang reached the territory of the Shang-Yin state.

Special expeditions for nephrite and its receipt as tribute are mentioned in Yin inscriptions on oracle bones.

According to specialists, a considerable part (about 300) of the objects of the richest jade collection, found in the tomb of Fu Hao, are made of Khotan nephrite.

From the Yin period onwards, Xinjiang nephrite became the main material for ritual utensils, above all for the imperial court. This tendency — with two as yet not well-understood interruptions (the Western Zhou, and the Wei, Jin and Northern and Southern Dynasties, whose monuments include practically no Xinjiang nephrite) — persisted for millennia, up to and including the last imperial dynasty, the Qing.

Not only Chinese rulers but also numerous conquerors — Khitans, Jurchens, Mongols and Manchus — were distinguished by their love of Khotan nephrite. It is known, for example, with what interest the Manchu Qianlong emperor (r. 1736–1795) regarded his collection of Khotan nephrite; he initiated the cataloguing of the imperial storehouses, thereby making a notable contribution to the historiography of the subject.

Nephrite arriving from Eastern Turkestan did not entirely displace local raw material, from which ornaments continued to be made for sale in city markets, but it took the dominant position among the ornaments of the imperial apartments. Only in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries was it somewhat displaced by objects of jadeite, which began to arrive from South-East Asia (Burma).

## Was jade working developed in Khotan itself?

In Eastern Turkestan itself, despite its very rich raw-material base, local production of nephrite objects did not develop to any significant degree in antiquity.

In the Qin (221–207 BC) and Han (206 BC – AD 220) dynasties there were as yet no locally made jade objects in Khotan, and most of the nephrite was unworked. According to the historical records of the *New Book of Tang*, the working of jade objects had appeared in Khotan by the last years of the Tang dynasty. Under the Ming dynasty the craft made great progress, and under the Qing it flourished; but owing to backward technology production deteriorated year by year and finally collapsed altogether.

## Which technological features of the working marks should be noted to identify signs of antiquity in jades of the Shang-Yin and the first half of the Western Zhou?

It must be borne in mind that after the appearance of bronze and iron tools, jade carvers were able to achieve precise lines and relatively smooth surfaces. But examination of carved ornament under a loupe shows unambiguously that the engraved lines on curved sections are discontinuous and lack smooth rounding — unlike modern engraved designs made with diamond burrs.

The sequence of making pierced openwork can also be traced. On ancient objects several holes were first drilled and then joined by relatively straight saw cuts.

This feature of the technology influences the overall style of the objects, which have rectilinear, chopped forms and lines, slightly angular rounded edges and a soft, satiny glow typical of primitive working of nephrite with sand. In other words, technology determines style.

## What working marks do modern nephrite objects show?

In diagnosing antiquity, the tool marks on the surface of the carving (the presence of irregularities on the ground surface) and the features of the carving methods (drilling of holes, incisions) are studied. Ancient tools and modern saws and discs leave different marks. It is therefore wrong to think that a copy of a jade artefact is easy to make with modern automatic means.

At the beginning of the twentieth century electrically driven machines for cutting and polishing hard stones appeared, making the working of nephrite easy and fast. Modern carvings as a rule have a glossy lustre and sharp edges and facets, and the ornament is applied to the surface in one continuous line.

Because of the special nature of nephrite working, a carving made with hand tools gives a better result than one made with modern high-speed automatic tools. The skill and patience of the workers also affect the quality of the object. The form and size of the original nephrite pebble suggest to the master what can be made from it.

In ancient times a jade carving took months or years; today it takes weeks or even days.

The jade carvers were slaves who worked with complete responsibility and care so as not to fail and to avoid punishment. Therefore even if one tries today to use hand tools to carve modern copies, they will certainly differ from ancient jades — not to mention that some ancient working methods cannot be copied with modern tools.

The combination in nephrite of moderate hardness and exceptional toughness makes it difficult to carve, grind and polish. This is all the more true of ancient carvers, who used only natural abrasive materials and a foot drive. A high quality of carving and of the artistic finishing of individual elements of the object is therefore evidence of antiquity and nothing else. There are, however, jades with simple and coarse carving: these are burial objects, carved in a short time solely to serve the dead body. They differ greatly from the jades made during the owner's lifetime.

## What features can be observed in genuine nephrite objects of the Shang and Zhou dynasties?

According to an old Chinese legend, the ancestor of the Shang was born of a phoenix sent from heaven. The phoenix therefore became the main motif of Shang artefacts.

The jade artefacts represent a kneeling man, pigs, sheep and mythical animals. Vessels and jade plaques are also found.

In the Shang dynasty a new range of jade objects appeared. Objects became three-dimensional and were decorated with incised ornament. Ceremonial weapons appeared, as did personal ornaments that decorated clothing, and other adornments. A whole series of small sculptures, such as birds and animals, also appeared.

It should be noted that Shang artefacts are made not only of high-quality nephrite but with extraordinary skill. Low relief, flowing lines and a carefully cut-back ground are typical of jades of this period.

The carving style of the Zhou dynasty is characterised by single and double slanting cuts and is therefore called the "forty-five-degree bevel". This technical feature disappeared in the middle of the Han dynasty.

Jade of the late Zhou is characterised by more detailed carving of individual elements, flowing design and exquisite quality. The "auspicious cloud" is a typical motif of this period. Most subjects are dragons and phoenixes. On objects in low relief, abstract and simplified motifs occur on the surface.

In the Zhou dynasty, *bi* discs and other ritual jade forms were popular among aristocrats.

In this period bronze tools were widely used for working nephrite. The carvers imitated the designs of their Shang predecessors, but in the middle of the Zhou period they began to decorate jade objects with zoomorphic *taotie* images, which later gave way to more abstract patterns.

In the Zhou period many objects of decorative art served ritual purposes: bronze mirrors, bells and various objects of the sacred stone, nephrite. Translucent and always cold, nephrite symbolised purity and was regarded as a protection against poison and the evil eye. In the supple interlacing patterns applied to bowls, dishes and trays is reflected the desire to catch and fix the movement of the universe.

Jade artefacts of the Zhou dynasty are in great demand among serious collectors because of their fine workmanship and rarity.

## What does the *taotie* mask mean?

The *taotie* is the image of a monster frequently found in the ornament of bronze and jade objects of the Shang and Zhou dynasties.

*Taotie* masks embody features of real and mythical animals and birds (dragons, bulls, rams, deer, tigers, owls, phoenixes and others). Sometimes these masks are highly stylised, sometimes naturalistic. Whenever the ancient Chinese masters used them to decorate ritual objects, they invariably placed them in the most prominent position.

Art historians consider that such images have the following set of features:

1. symmetry about a central vertical line — the line is sometimes clearly marked, sometimes implied, but vertical symmetry is an indispensable attribute of every *taotie* image;
2. three vertical and three horizontal registers in the image, the middle vertical register often being a continuous line.

## What jade objects were carved in the Qin dynasty?

The Qin dynasty ruled the whole of China between the Zhou and Han dynasties, in the period of the Qin Empire (221–207 BC).

At this time jade objects were increasingly decorated with images of animals and various decorative designs. Images of warriors are of interest, their faces and clothing executed with great care.

## In which period of history did the art of jade carving flourish?

Jade-working technology developed further in the Han era. The Han dynasty (206 BC – AD 220) is the period of Chinese history after the Qin Empire and before the Three Kingdoms.

The Han era in Chinese history was marked by a new flowering of culture and art and by the development of science. Historical scholarship was born. Chinese scholars made great efforts to copy ancient writings from the decaying bamboo strips that served as books onto silk scrolls. The most important discovery was the invention of paper in the first century BC.

Caravan routes linked China with other countries. Along the Great Silk Road, for example, the Chinese carried westward silk and the finest hand embroidery, famous throughout the world. Written sources preserve information about the lively trade of the Han empire with India and with distant Rome, where China had long been called the Land of Silk.

The country was divided into administrative units governed by officials appointed by the emperor. These were paid a salary, and promotion was based not on nobility but on education and success in a series of examinations. Owning carved jade objects with images of dragons was very prestigious. Aristocrats ordered jade objects to decorate their houses; animals were often depicted on them. The carving of chains from a single piece of nephrite reached great mastery.

Noble people were buried in spacious tombs. By custom, various ritual objects (*bi* discs) and jade objects were placed in the burials. All the images carved in jade are very realistic.

The most unusual jade work of the Han period was the "jade suit" exhibited in the National Museum in Beijing. It was made for a deceased nobleman to ward off evil spirits in the afterlife. This astonishing ensemble is made of more than 2,000 jade plaques sewn together with gold thread.

Many carvings of this period are made of pale-yellow and white nephrite, considered the most resistant to wear.

Carvings made in China in the Han dynasty are classified on the international antiques market as "white jade" or "old jade" and bear the characteristic name **"mutton fat"**. It must be remembered that such white jades were the exclusive privilege of the families of the ancient emperors.

The objects of this time were still rather primitive in design but already fully three-dimensional. Interesting sawing techniques were used in their carving, executed simply, clearly, skilfully and smoothly.

Artefacts of this period characteristically have small yellow-brown spots that penetrate deep into the body of the nephrite. Among art dealers such penetration is regarded as a sign of "true authenticity".

## What features can be observed in genuine nephrite objects of the Han dynasty?

Han jade carving is known for its straight lines. Objects tend to become larger owing to an ample supply of white nephrite. The main motifs are auspicious creatures: dragon, phoenix, tiger, snake, tortoise and eagle. The images are rendered with greater expressiveness, elegance and lightness. In the treatment of their fancifully curved bodies a closeness to the Scythian "animal style" can be seen.

Buckles, rings and figures of people and animals are distinguished by the softness of modelling of the smoothly ground surface of the stone and by the finest patterns.

## The study of the working of ancient jades

In ancient China nephrite was worked by abrasion with fine mineral powders, without metal tools.

In 1997 the remains of a jade workshop were discovered during excavations north of Nanjing. Nephrite pebbles can still be found along the nearby river close to the archaeological site; this material may have been the source of nephrite for the craftsmen of the Neolithic. Many stone tools used for cutting, drilling, grinding and polishing nephrite were also found. Study of the marks left by these primitive tools helps to reveal a great number of forgeries produced in modern times.

## Which features of working must be noted in the attribution of antique nephrite objects?

1. **Sawing.** If the thickness of flat plates is absolutely uniform, this may indicate the use of modern diamond saws.
2. **Evidence of the use of high-speed lapidary machines and diamond abrasives.**
3. **The cleanness and neatness of the working of small details.** In antiquity nephrite was a very expensive material, and every fragment and detail of decoration was worked carefully. The researcher must therefore note whether there are any poorly worked surfaces with sharp edges and chips. Careful microscopic study should help to reveal the features of grinding and polishing of ancient jade artefacts.
4. **Drilled holes.** This is an important diagnostic feature. The holes of genuine jade artefacts differ from period to period, because the ancient tools differed. It has been firmly established, for example, that the holes in objects of the Hongshan and Liangzhu cultures are double (drilled from both sides), with an irregular inner surface or a spiral texture on one side.
5. **A "soft, satiny" surface.** In old times, careful grinding at slow speed with soft abrasives created a slightly undulating surface with a characteristic greasy lustre, which differs from modern polished surfaces with a strong gloss.
6. **Selective zonal iron-staining of the surface with a "Liesegang ring" effect** can also serve as a tool for verifying the authenticity of jade artefacts.

## Was waxing of jade objects practised in antiquity?

It is known that the ancient Chinese took steps to preserve the beauty of nephrite. The jade surface was treated with hot wax and/or dipped in boiling fat or wax. Depending on the aggressiveness of the soil, these traces of wax or the layer of fat are sometimes preserved on the artefacts as heavy runs on the surface. Designs, cracks and hollows sealed with wax sometimes look new even today.

## Was nephrite dyed in antiquity?

Since nephrite very rarely occurs in the desired range of colours, attempts have been made for thousands of years, since antiquity, to colour it artificially. In the Han and Tang dynasties, for example, reddish and reddish-brown nephrites were particularly prized. Dyeing was done in boiling oil with suitable additives and ingredients, such as iron compounds, which gave light nephrites a beautiful brown colour.

## How do antique dealers describe in words the diagnostic features of ancient white jades?

- "Old jade is denser and is characterised by brilliant, smooth, heavy surfaces and distinctive sounds when tapped."
- "Its colour is like the tail fat of a sheep, and it has a greasy, radiant lustre."
- "Its internal structure resembles half-cooked rice."
- "Misty and translucent because of its greasy lustre, the jade looks different under different lighting."
- "The oily white radiant lustre has a slight yellowish tinge."
- "Oily and hard, translucent and pure, white and without cracks — just like congealed mutton fat."
- "Even if archaic white 'mutton fat' jade has been buried in the ground for more than 2,000 years, its surface is still smooth, lustrous and oily as before, because of its hard and dense quality. This is a very valuable quality that will never be impaired over thousands of years, and thanks to it this jade is classed as a precious stone."

## What features can be observed in genuine nephrite objects of the Sui, Tang, Song and Yuan dynasties?

The design of jade artefacts of these dynasties is characterised by its connection with everyday life. These artefacts reflect the clear influence of Buddhism; many Buddhist figures in jade were made in this period.

## What did objects for the scholar's desk look like in later times?

Later, nephrite became the favourite material of the Chinese scholar. It was used particularly often for such objects as holders for the brushes used in calligraphy, and even for the mouthpieces of opium pipes, owing to the widespread belief that they granted the smoker longevity.

## Which mineralogical features of nephrite affect the quality of the worked surface?

After working, fibrous nephrite has an uneven surface caused by differences in the direction of the fibres.

John Sinkankas, in his book *Gem Cutting: A Lapidary's Manual*, drew attention to the unevenness of the working and polishing of nephrite: "Nephrite varies notably in structure, as a result of which in certain directions it is not as hard as one would wish; this causes great difficulty in grinding and polishing. With prolonged grinding and polishing the differences in hardness lead to an uneven surface, and the surface of the stone comes to resemble a 'lemon peel'. This is because its fibrous structure resembles that of wood. Like wood, nephrite seems harder when it is sawn across the grain. It also takes a polish unequally in different directions. Modern diamond materials and high working speeds make it possible to achieve a smooth ground surface."

## What is particular about the working of nephrite today?

Nephrite consists of a felted-fibrous mass of extremely fine crystals, which gives it extraordinary toughness and strength. It roughs out easily, but a smooth surface is difficult to obtain in grinding and polishing. The key to success is careful grinding; the specific method depends on the material used. Sometimes a smooth surface is obtained by wet grinding, sometimes by dry. If undercutting (plucking) is observed, a change of technique is recommended. At present the most reliable results are obtained with diamond pastes on wood. Formerly, aluminium oxide or chromium oxide on leather was used for polishing.
