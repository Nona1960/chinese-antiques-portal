# Signs of Antiquity in Jade

## What does the term "artefact" mean?

The word "artefact" derives from the Latin *arte factum*, "made by art", that is, artificially made. Originally the term was used of a phenomenon, property, factor, process or object that could not have been created, or could not exist at all, under normal natural conditions. It implied a purposeful intervention, or an unspecified factor that led to the appearance of the artefact.

Most often we meet the term in connection with archaeological finds, where it denotes a man-made object studied by archaeologists. Today, however, the term is often used not only of an ancient object made by man but also of an object of obscure or unproven origin, sometimes even one frankly not recognised by official science.

## How does the study of the mineral composition of nephrite help to solve the problem of authenticating ancient artefacts?

For the last several decades the scientific analysis of early Chinese jades has been directed at determining their mineral composition and the features of secondary alteration of their surface. This is because the physical and chemical changes of the nephrite surface are formed by nature over centuries and even millennia and cannot be produced artificially.

The study of well-documented objects from archaeological excavations helps to determine the origin of an artefact by analogy of its characteristics and contributes greatly to the authentication of ancient jade objects.

## What explains the selective rust-coloured staining of ancient jade artefacts?

The increased sorption activity of the surface of tremolite nephrite is explained by enhanced diffusion of iron hydroxides from surface waters into precisely those surfaces that were sawn, ground and polished. In other words, the working of the surface produced structures with a specific surface area 30–40 times greater than that of nephrites that were not carved or ground. The selective zoning of the distribution of haematite accumulations on the nephrite surface is explained by the orientation of the highly porous cross-sections of the fibres, which have a well-developed internal specific surface.

Manganese and iron are constant components of natural fresh waters, and their content often exceeds the levels of the main macro-elements. The soluble forms of manganese and the complex organic compounds of iron present in surface waters are resistant to chemical oxidation by dissolved oxygen. The cycle of iron and manganese consists in their compounds being carried from the catchment area into the ancient burial, where they are oxidised and deposited on the artefacts. The migration of iron and manganese in surface waters depends to a great extent on the activity of micro-organisms. The biological transformation of both manganese and iron can take place through physico-chemical processes and with the participation of oxidising micro-organisms.

## What is an important sign of antiquity in the study of old jades?

The most important point in establishing the antiquity of carved figures is the study of the secondary alteration of the worked surface of the object.

The collector must pay particular attention to analysing the causes of formation of the characteristic **ring-shaped iron-rich formations** — crystallised haematite and lepidocrocite (ruby mica, a mineral of the hydroxide class, a layered iron oxyhydroxide) — on the surface of carved artefacts.

The jade researcher Wilkins (2003) described certain features of the colouring, composition and recrystallisation of natural nephrite specimens that had undergone long-term weathering. Long burial (more than 1,000 years) may be characterised by selective diffusion of brown hydrated iron oxide combined with whitish films. Such surface bleaching may be caused by the dissolution of nephrite in the alkaline environment of burials.

The reaction develops on the surface of polished nephrite, which is porous and fissured and has many defects, including lone electron pairs not involved in bonding. Absorption takes place especially in the zones where the fibres lie perpendicular to the worked plane. Molecules of iron oxides readily interact with these electrons and, thanks to the bonds formed, are firmly held on the nephrite surface.

This transformation takes place over a long time, and different stages of the complex process can be seen under the microscope. It has been established that the behaviour of the iron oxides is accompanied by changes in their primary and secondary structures. Iron released during the serpentinisation of nephrite is fixed in the form of veinlets and accumulations forming a pseudo-reticulate (mesh-like) structure.

## What should be noted in the careful study of ancient jades?

There are some fundamental points that may be useful in diagnosing the antiquity of jades:

1. Ancient jades could not have been made with modern tools and polishing pastes.
2. The natural mineral abrasives used in antiquity are still available today, so their presence on an object in microscopic quantities proves nothing.
3. The presence of soil encrustations and carbonate accretions does not confirm antiquity. A clever forger intending to imitate a burial appearance and/or great age can easily add such encrustations to the surface of an imitation.
4. The age-related alteration of the surface of ancient jades is explained by the development of secondary minerals on it. These minerals differ from the original nephrite in their high degree of dispersion and are responsible for the growth of lamellar pure iron hydroxides: the solid phase of iron hydroxide precipitates on an oxidation barrier as a colloidal micro-film, on which iron hydroxide formations begin to be deposited in the form of the mineral lepidocrocite, which has a layered structure.

Some scholars maintain that nephrite with a weathered surface is at least a thousand years old, since the nephrite surface resists corrosion thanks to its structure and composition.

Any scientific attribution study must take into account many factors, such as the porosity of the nephrite surface, the presence of harmful substances in the burial environment, and the water content and acidity of the soil.

## Can a white film be induced artificially on a nephrite figure?

The presence of a white film as "traces of ancient weathering" is a disputed proof. A particular type of alteration (a white film) is known to occur on ancient Chinese jades from burials. The process consists of selective dissolution (leaching) on a microscopic scale along the grain boundaries of the mineral under the influence of highly alkaline solutions (pH > 9). Such an environment can arise during the decomposition of the body or bodies with which the jade objects were buried.

One study modelling ancient chemical reactions sets out the conditions under which "ancient white film" arises. Researchers placed a piece of nephrite in a bath at pH 10 for three months, after which alterations of the same type as those characteristic of jades from ancient burials were observed. It is therefore quite possible that this type of alteration can easily be achieved within a few months by various artificial chemical processes.

Factors acting on jade in a burial include natural soils, chemical substances (such as red ochre, a mixture often used in large quantities around the grave), groundwater, the corrosion products of iron and bronze objects in the burial, the putrefaction of organic burial materials, and high temperatures from burial fires.

## What is the history of the forgery of archaic jades?

In Chinese decorative art, "archaic" usually means objects made before the beginning of the Han dynasty (about 200 BC).

The forgery of ancient jades has a long history: copying began under the Song (960–1279) and Ming (1368–1644) dynasties. In China, copies were made not to deceive buyers but in homage to ancient Chinese culture. Exact copies of ancient jades were produced to imperial order, exclusively from high-quality raw material; the reasons for making "forged" ancient jades were thus not commercial. According to historical documents, collectors of the imperial family preferred objects of the Warring States period (from the fifth century BC to the unification of China in 221 BC) and of the Han dynasty (206 BC – AD 220).

Today, by contrast, fake jades are made purely for commercial purposes. They include modern copies, most often made from low-quality raw material with many various inclusions.

## What questions does a collector intending to acquire an ancient carved Chinese jade usually ask?

1. Is it nephrite?
2. Is it Chinese nephrite?
3. Is it ancient Chinese nephrite?

Nephrite as a mineral is identified by visual and instrumental gemmological methods. Colour, lustre, translucency, hardness and refractive index are determined, and the absence or presence of luminescence and the features of inclusions of other minerals are studied. The study of physical and optical properties helps to reveal imitations (serpentine, bowenite, white chalcedony, aventurine and even "Peking glass"). Serpentine (which, unlike nephrite, has a sickly, dull yellow-green colour) and chalcedony, distinguished by a heavy opaque white colour, are most often used to imitate ancient artefacts.

In the attribution of archaic jades the following questions are asked:

- is the carved motif characteristic of the supposed period of manufacture?
- are the features of the working marks characteristic of the supposed period?
- is there correspondence between the style of surface decoration and the form of the object?

## Is there scientific proof of the signs of antiquity in archaic jades?

The analysis of signs of antiquity in jade artefacts includes the study of oriented mineral overgrowths on the surface, the features of internal cracks, the characteristics of selective weathering, the presence of raised relief, the spread of diffusion staining, a whitish film on the surface, pseudomorphs of mica and talc, and the selective iron-staining of the carved surface. These physical and chemical phenomena are formed by nature over centuries and cannot be produced artificially by man. Verifying these proofs of antiquity is of paramount importance for the whole scientific method of authenticating ancient jades.

## Which secondary alterations of carved objects can serve as proof of the antiquity of nephrite?

It has been scientifically proved that the alteration of nephrite during weathering can produce various effects depending on the volume and chemistry of any liquids or gases that interact with it.

The authentication of nephrite is based on the study of certain physical and chemical characteristics of surface alteration. It is supposed that certain alterations of archaic jade artefacts can provide basic evidence for diagnosing the antiquity of jades that were buried in the soil for long periods, and for distinguishing them from recently made ones.

Such alterations include the following.

**1. Oriented mineral overgrowth on the surface of artefacts (epitaxy; "additive crystal planes").** It occurs when the total energy of the boundary, made up of the substrate–crystal, crystal–medium and substrate–medium areas, is at a minimum. Epitaxial intergrowths proper arise when one mineral grows on the surface of nephrite; if a crystal forms ingrowths, such formations are called endotaxial. Visually, the regularity of the overgrowth results from the coincidence or proximity of particular planes in the crystal lattices of the minerals. The crystals rise above the plane and grow outwards from a centre in various directions, with a natural and regular arrangement.

**2. Internal cleavage ("cleaving veins").** Internal cracks are caused by bulk mechanical stresses of underground processes (high pressure, temperature, plastic deformation). Internal cracks differ from external cracks that reach the surface.

**3. Selective weathering ("differential weathering").** This phenomenon is connected with differences in the degree of alteration of colour, hardness, density and recrystallisation of the surface layer of the jade artefact under the influence of moisture, heat and chemical substances during a long stay underground. Channels and traces caused by the activity of underground plant roots are sometimes noted on jade artefacts; they may also be caused by the interaction of the roots surrounding the jades.

**4. Corrosion of the polished surface ("dissolved pits").** The corroded pitted surface has a form showing natural irregularity and is formed by natural weathering. The form of the mineral crystals cannot be imitated by artificial methods.

**5. Swelling of the surface — the formation of raised crystals on the surface of carved artefacts ("raised relief").** Recognising the natural origin of raised relief can serve as a tool for authenticating jade artefacts.

Raised relief appears on the surface of jade artefacts both in small areas (up to 1.5 mm) and in large areas that may consist entirely of fibrous growths of the altered nephrite surface. The mechanism by which raised relief forms on jade artefacts has been determined: it is connected with the alteration of tremolite (and/or pyroxene) through the secondary growth of hydrous minerals such as clays and/or micas. In some cases the force of crystallisation is enough to lift crystals or nephrite fibres above the surface. This effect is present only on artefacts made before the beginning of the Song dynasty.

It must be borne in mind that artificial raised relief can be made by undercutting and grinding followed by micro-sandblasting. Such work leaves individual crystals standing higher with an unnatural surface; they are then usually polished and look unnatural. Drops of epoxy resin glued to the surface of the object can also be used to create raised relief. But such artificial ageing of the surface is easily detected with an ordinary binocular microscope and a loupe, and in some cases is visible even to the naked eye.

**6. Mica pseudomorphs after nephrite ("exposed crystal plane — mica").** This is a process of hydration and the formation of sheet silicate minerals (clays and micas) connected with the long exposure of jade artefacts to groundwater.

Tremolite is replaced by talc along particular crystallographic directions, sometimes to the point of complete pseudomorphs; more rarely by antigorite or chrysotile, by an aggregate of calcite, mica and quartz, or by scapolite. In ancient objects, replacement of nephrite by tabular tremolite has been noted. In the zone of supergene alteration, replacement of tremolite by nontronite has been established.

Recrystallisation entails an increase in volume or the appearance of irregularity on the surface owing to secondary alteration of both individual crystals and large areas of the surface. Since the growth of silicate minerals such as clay and talc is connected with the restructuring of amphiboles and pyroxenes and requires a great deal of time, the observation of zones of talc alteration on the surface is evidence of the authenticity of the artefact.

**7. Deterioration marks ("cow's hair").** This effect on jade artefacts resembles coloured veinlets and cracks reaching the surface, recalling the hair of a cow. They are sometimes called "age spots", "ant's legs" or "cow's hair". Their formation is connected with the growth of salt crystals formed from waters that penetrate capillary cracks as solutions. The salts contained in the solutions crystallise, pressure increases, and the capillary cracks widen, which breaks down the monolithic integrity of the nephrite. These physical or chemical transformations may be caused by underground heat, pressure and chemical reactions over millennia.

**8. Burial traces ("underground deterioration").** Surface leaching is characterised by areas of alteration that are softer than the unaltered areas of the worked nephrite surface. This type of alteration is a selective dissolution or leaching along mineral grain boundaries at the microscopic level by highly alkaline solutions (pH > 9), not a mineralogical change. Such a high-pH environment can arise from the decomposition of the body with which the jades were buried.

It is quite likely that such alterations of jades take place within a few months immediately after burial, while the body decomposes. The destructive work on the surface of ancient jades is begun by bacteria, which prepare the ground for the appearance of microflora. In the course of their life activity, organisms absorb chemical elements from the rock they destroy, and the organic acids formed during the decomposition of organic masses promote the processes of dissolution and hydrolysis.

**9. Diffusion-pore staining ("diffusive markings").** This is connected with the diffusion of iron solutions along the grain boundaries of nephrite. The solutions diffuse between the grain boundaries and are adsorbed on the surface of the pores, since all nephrite fibres are more active because of their pores. Iron hydroxides are adsorbed on the surface of the pores, which act as centres of activity, and then cause oxidation. The staining penetrates selectively along the grain boundaries. It has a dirty-brown tint, unlike the bright reddish colour induced artificially on imitations under pressure in a suspension of pure iron oxide or dry red-lead powder.

For electrochemical corrosion processes to develop on the surface of worked ancient jades, it is enough for a certain potential difference to arise between individual micro-areas of the nephrite. Such a potential difference may arise:

1. if the microstructure of the worked mineral is inhomogeneous — that is, the mineral consists of individual micro-grains with differently developed microcracks;
2. where micro-impurities of many elements are localised at grain boundaries and at other so-called "active points" of the structure, which may cause local secondary alteration; this also includes the local zones of deformation that arose during cutting and grinding.

## What is the secondary alteration of ancient jades?

The characteristic manifestations of secondary alteration on the surface of ancient jades are the consequence of weathering and/or **hypergenesis** (from the Greek *hyper*, "above", and *genesis*, "origin"). This is a complex process that proceeds over a long time under the influence of many physical, chemical and biological factors. Archaeological finds of carved objects of the Neolithic (the Hongshan culture) prove that nephrite undergoes the destructive action of corrosion over long periods (5,000–6,000 years).

Although nephrite is resistant, with time it undergoes **serpentinisation** (zones of white powdery spots appear on the surface of the object) and **limonitisation** (deposition of iron hydroxide). Serpentinisation results from the weathering of nephrite; the more iron the nephrite contains, the stronger the alteration. On figures of black nephrite a mottled film of shapeless reddish-white spots is often observed.

Serpentinisation is caused by the organic acids of damp soil, especially in ancient burials (3,000 years and more). The white spots of the newly formed mineral are distributed zonally over the worked surface. Microscopic studies have shown that the alteration of the surface is selective. It depends on the orientation of the fibres to the plane of polishing: where they are perpendicular, white spots of serpentine form; where parallel, everything is smooth, there are no spots, and the nephrite is unaltered.

## What do the zones of secondary alteration of ancient jades look like under the microscope?

As the latest methods of X-ray and electron-diffraction study have established, only a few of the secondary minerals that develop on the primary surface of a carving are amorphous. Most of them, despite their high degree of colloidal dispersion, have a distinctly crystalline structure.

Most often the mass of secondary minerals consists of individual particles of ultramicroscopic, colloidal size, which are clearly distinguishable under the microscope. It has been proved that it is precisely well-recrystallised secondary minerals that are a sign of antiquity. These minerals have the form of platelets, unlike the shapeless amorphous masses that appear as a result of etching with acids and alkalis on modern forgeries.

## Can secondary alteration be created artificially on the surface of carvings?

Forgers in China, using modern technology, copy ancient carvings of the Hongshan culture and age their surface artificially with acids. Hydrofluoric acid, for example, etches the nephrite surface. In diagnosing such objects it should be remembered that etching produces a pitted texture and a white film that is easily washed off, whereas on genuine ancient carvings the white spots lie within the body of the nephrite and are not etch pits but the mineral serpentine, whose felted-fibrous structure can be seen under the microscope. An additional diagnostic method is the study of the elemental composition of the white spots of secondary alteration of the nephrite: **the absence of calcium in their composition confirms the transformation of nephrite into serpentine over at least 4,000–6,000 years.**

In detecting imitations it should also be borne in mind that the acid-etched pitted texture covers the whole surface of the object evenly, not selectively. A regularity has been revealed in the study of forgeries: white amorphous films always accumulate in the engraved lines and cut ornament; they are traces of the etching of the nephrite and are connected with the concentration of acid in the hollows.

## What other scientific methods exist for determining signs of antiquity in jade objects?

Another method of diagnosing the antiquity of Hongshan jades is to reveal the features of working. It is well known that in the Neolithic nephrite was worked with quartz sand. A diagnostic sign of ancient primitive working is the stepped, jerky character of sawn lines and holes. Nor are perfectly even worked surfaces (without the "orange peel" effect) found on ancient objects; such surfaces are characteristic of modern objects worked and polished at high speeds with diamond tools.

The study of ancient Chinese stone carving of the Shang and the first half of the Zhou with modern scientific methods also makes it possible to single out genuine ancient objects and to propose methods for detecting copies and forgeries of modern times.

Carved objects with supergene iron-staining of the nephrite are characteristic of the Shang and the first half of the Zhou. Many carvings of this period were made of pale-yellow and white nephrite, considered the most resistant to wear.

Nephrite is an acicular, finely fibrous mineral with many micropores. Over time, iron hydroxide is deposited on the surface of nephrite objects as dark-brown formations. The rust penetrates the surface layer of the nephrite from the enclosing rocks in the form of diffusion rings, since in graves nephrite enters the zone of stagnant water.

The diffusion rings named **Liesegang rings** were discovered at the end of the nineteenth century by the German chemist Raphael Liesegang. The rings are regular in form and each ring is of equal thickness; the rings increase in diameter as they spread from a central point. Liesegang structures form when a solution diffuses through a gel or porous substance in which an insoluble precipitate is periodically deposited from the solution. The result is layers, if diffusion proceeds into the depth of the substance, or rings, if diffusion begins at a centre on the surface of the stone.

A mechanism explaining the formation of Liesegang structures was proposed by the chemist Wilhelm Ostwald in 1897. He held that during the reaction a precipitate is periodically deposited from a supersaturated solution. On ancient jade objects the rusty Liesegang rings are covered with brown, lustrous small crystals of haematite. This confirms the antiquity of the specimens and their burial in the ground for 2,000–3,000 years.

## How are the traces of diffusion-pore staining imitated on fake jades?

On forgeries, imitators try to create the effect of ancient iron-staining. For this, flat blanks are treated under pressure in a suspension of pure iron oxide or dry red-lead powder, the aqueous medium being reinforced with an acid solution. That is why the colour of the peripheral layers is almost red rather than the dirty yellow-brown that results from staining through continuously forming microcracks directly in the multi-component but weak, neutral saline solution of river water or damp soil.

Jade objects treated in iron compounds under pressure, carved and ground with the finest river quartz sand on soaked bamboo sticks, are often passed off as ancient. An example is an artificially aged carved nephrite plaque sold at auction in Beijing as an ancient jade of the Warring States period (475–221 BC).

In such cases the colour of the peripheral layers is almost red, not the yellow-brown that arises from staining through microcracks in the damp soil of the burials of ancient rulers. Sometimes a brown iron-rich "sub-crust layer" is observed on forged objects; this means a modern fake, since the genuine sub-crust layer of ancient objects should be brown, with flakes of black dolomitic skarn and microscopic stained cracks along the nephrite fibres.

## What scientific problems did we ourselves address in the study of ancient jades?

In identifying genuine ancient jades, a topical task is the study of the secondary alteration of the worked surfaces of carvings with the electron microscope, together with the study of the elemental composition of the altered surfaces.

The aim of the research was to study the features of the natural ageing of nephrite in burials and to describe the diagnostic signs of its antiquity. The main attention was paid to analysing the causes of formation of the characteristic ring-shaped iron-rich formations crystallised on the surface of carved artefacts.

Discussion of the results of microscopic studies of mineralogical alteration can lead to an understanding of whether their presence on the surface of nephrite carvings is due to natural weathering or has been induced artificially in our time.

The first step in solving this problem is to analyse the physical and mineralogical alterations present in supposedly archaic artefacts in order to determine the mechanisms of their formation.

The authentication of ancient jades included the study of certain physical and chemical characteristics capable of providing basic evidence for diagnosing the antiquity of jades that were buried in the soil for a long time, and for distinguishing them from recently made objects.

To reveal the signs of antiquity, we carefully studied museum specimens of archaic jade artefacts. In Chinese decorative art, objects made before the beginning of the Han dynasty (about 200 BC) are usually considered archaic.

On many specimens we observed a characteristic picture: reddish-brown accretions on the worked surface. Our task was to determine the composition of the accretions and to reveal the features of the crystallogenesis of these secondary formations.

To develop a method of non-destructive diagnosis of secondary formations on archaic jades, we used a carved nephrite figure of a horse, presumably made in China in the Zhou period.

Samples of the rust-coloured secondary accretions were collected from the surface of the base of the carving with a jeweller's scraper (a steel scraper). The material removed was fixed on the microscope stage with carbon tape. Compositional analyses were carried out by electron-probe microanalysis with a wavelength-dispersive spectrometer (EPMA-WDS). A **Phenom ProX** desktop scanning electron microscope was used; it is entered in the register of measuring instruments of the Russian Federation and can work with both conductive and non-conductive specimens. The resolution of this microscope at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV is 14 nm. The integrated energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS) system of the Phenom ProX makes it possible to carry out elemental analysis of the material at a particular point, along a selected line or over an area.

In the study we took into account that the nephrite particles in the powder could significantly influence one another, which is reflected in the results of the study of secondary accretions on the nephrite surface.

The compositional results of the study of the elemental composition of the secondary accretions revealed several elements. Oxygen (O), magnesium (Mg) and silicon (Si) most probably belong to particles removed from the serpentinised surface. Carbon and nitrogen are traces of organic deposits.

The discovery of minor traces of rare-earth elements was of interest. Their presence in ancient accretions is explained by the fact that the migration of trace elements in suspension is most characteristic of underground river waters. They migrate both in clastic material, where they enter the crystal lattice of minerals, and in clay minerals. Suspensions of iron and manganese hydroxides adsorb from the water and chemically bind many rare and dispersed elements. To this rather complex mineral complex must be added the organic substances carried with the suspension, which retain trace elements.

## What explains the zoning of iron absorption on ancient jade artefacts?

The sectoral redistribution of iron hydroxides testifies to the inhomogeneous arrangement of the nephrite fibres during the formation of the felted-fibrous aggregates.

When such a felted-fibrous structure is cut and ground, the crystal intergrowths are cut in different directions; it has therefore long been known that worked nephrites polished with soft abrasives show a slight "orange peel" effect. Since the outcrops of the cut bundles of hair-like minerals are grouped and arranged in zones, the absorption of iron hydroxides will also be zonal. Its intensity depends on the orientation of the fibres to the plane of polishing.

## What are the features and stages of the formation of staining and alteration of appearance in buried archaic nephrite carvings?

The role of time in the alteration of jade artefacts is an important factor in the occurrence of selective diffusion in nephrite.

The alteration of ancient jades passes through several stages:

1. Recrystallisation of nephrite into serpentine begins, characterised by a decrease in density on the one hand and the simultaneous removal of calcium on the other.
2. The final result of the action of time is selective serpentinisation along the fibrous crystals.
3. Superimposed processes take place on pyrite: it is on pyrite that iron hydroxide (goethite, lepidocrocite) forms, which is very active and hydrophobic, since it has a high specific surface that activates sub-individuals upon itself.
4. Colloidal and organic humic acids are filtered by the active iron hydroxide.
5. Slow settling and precipitation of colloidal particles of iron(III) hydroxide take place.
6. As a result of the crystallisation of the surface iron-bearing solutions, the irregularities of the worked surface are wholly or partly filled with homogeneous or heterogeneous mineral matter, forming secretions. The secretions are filled with crystalline or colloidal mineral matter, crystallisation proceeding from the periphery of the cavity towards the centre. The result is concentrically zoned fillings and the selective settling of minerals on the worked transverse fibres of the nephrite. Absorption is selective because the surface was previously ground and polished and therefore has an increased energy owing to deformations and roughness.
7. Water containing ferrous iron and dissolved oxygen, filtering through the granular layer of the worked (damaged) nephrite surface, releases iron on the surface of the grains, forming a catalytic film of ions and hydroxides of ferrous and ferric iron. The film actively intensifies the oxidation and release of iron compounds from the water.

## What are the signs of antiquity of jade artefacts?

1. The felted-fibrous structure of nephrite, which after working shows anisotropy of absorption depending on the angle of sawing and grinding.
2. A complex composition of colloidal and organic humic acids enriched with active iron hydroxide.
3. Local inhomogeneity of the worked surface of carvings, characterised by roughness and microcracks with increased energy.
4. An age of more than 2,000 years.
5. The action of seasonal fluctuations of stagnant groundwater characteristic of burials.

## How does the quality of the material help to determine the age of an object?

From the Shang dynasty onwards, many artefacts were made of high-quality nephrite. As a rule, most genuine jade objects are made only from high-quality raw material. Conversely, low-quality nephrite, often with various zones, is generally used to make a fake artefact in order to imitate the patina of "antiquity". Thus good quality and a beautiful colour may indicate a genuine object or old Ming-dynasty copies, while lower quality points inevitably to modern forgeries.

## Which signs of contamination of nephrite indicate a forgery?

Genuine ancient jades are covered with a layer of minerals with secondary alterations (talc alteration, serpentinisation), which can be removed only by grinding a deep "window", whereas fake jades, with no alteration of the surface structure of the raw material, are usually covered with dirt or coloured clay that is easily washed off with hot water and a sponge.

If the whole jade is covered with lime to give it a cloudy look, or this film lies in the hollows, it is quite likely to be a forgery.

Epitaxial intergrowths (secondary intergrowths of nephrite with grains of quartz sand) can often be observed on ancient jade artefacts. Such mutual intergrowth proves the long duration of contact between the nephrite and the quartz sand in the burial.

## What is new in the "method of identifying signs of antiquity in Hongshan jades"?

With the spread of all kinds of imitations and forgeries, the development of a method of microscopic and mineralogical analysis of ancient Chinese jade carvings has become of great importance. Of particular scientific interest is the study of the generation of secondary alterations of the surface of archaic jade objects by the microscopic method, together with the elemental analysis of these zones.

We studied the surface of jade objects of the Neolithic/Chalcolithic found in north-eastern China, presumably belonging to the Hongshan culture. All the nephrite figures have characteristic white spots of secondary alteration on the surface. That nephrite undergoes the destructive action of corrosion over a long time (5,000–6,000 years) is proved by archaeological finds of similar carvings of the Neolithic (Hongshan culture) in well-known museum collections.

Microscopic study of the white spots of secondary alteration in reflected light was carried out with a NOOP 32 instrument. It showed that these white spots have a dendritic structure. The most strongly altered areas were identified and their orientation analysed. The intensity of accumulation of zones of secondary alteration with white spots is particularly great in areas where the sheaves of nephrite fibres lie perpendicular to the plane of polishing and show characteristic ingrowths into the depth of the nephrite.

Mineralogical studies were carried out with the most modern methods, including microscopic study with a **Phenom** desktop scanning electron microscope — a unique instrument combining the functions of optical and electron microscopes, with the possibility of analysing chemical composition at a point, over an area of the specimen or along a line. With the built-in EDS system, the elemental composition was determined, roughness was measured, and three-dimensional images were obtained of specimens of the characteristic spots of secondary alteration of ancient jades.

X-ray mapping was also carried out, giving an idea of the distribution of elements over the whole area of the altered surface, which made it possible to assess the elemental composition of the altered zones and the antiquity of the specimens studied.

The studies confirmed that the surface serpentinisation of ancient carved figures is a long and complex process, different stages of which are visible under the microscope.

The study of elemental composition showed that during metasomatism calcium passes into a water-soluble form and is removed. It was also found that the behaviour of the iron oxides is accompanied by changes in their primary and secondary structures. Iron released during the serpentinisation of nephrite is fixed in the form of veinlets and accumulations forming a pseudo-reticulate structure.

An additional diagnostic method is the study of the elemental composition of the white spots of secondary alteration. **The absence of calcium in their composition proves the transformation of nephrite into serpentine over at least 3,000–4,000 years.**

## Has any regularity of serpentinisation been revealed on ancient jades?

The research revealed that the white spots of the newly formed mineral serpentine are distributed zonally over the worked surface. The alteration of the surface is selective; that is, serpentinisation is uneven. The reason for this selective distribution of serpentine spots lies in the structure. Nephrite consists of fine tremolite fibres gathered into bundles which, closely interwoven, form a felted-fibrous aggregate. Each bundle consists of 10–30 sub-parallel fibres measuring from 0.005 × 0.01 to 0.01 × 0.2 mm.

The structures most frequently found in nephrites are fibroblastic (felted-fibrous), felted-parallel-fibrous and porphyroblastic sheaf-like structures combined with a fibroblastic groundmass. The rate of surface corrosion depends on the orientation of the fibres to the plane of polishing.

This explains why the whole surface of ancient carved figures is not uniformly altered. This feature is an important sign of the antiquity of archaic jades; such an effect cannot be created artificially.

The relative mobility of components during the serpentinisation of nephrites was studied by the method of I. V. Davidenko (1975). The method is based on comparing the content of components (rock-forming oxides) in the metasomatites and in the original rocks. If the content of a component is the same in the compared samples, the ratio of their contents equals one, and such a component is the least mobile. Consequently, the recrystallisation of nephrite into serpentine is limited by the duration of the process of hydrothermal metasomatic alteration — which again explains why the whole surface of ancient carved figures is not uniformly altered.

Selective alteration is characteristic. Macroscopically it appears as small light spots, lenses and pockets, recrystallised to varying degrees with the formation of serpentine.

The growing interest in archaic nephrite carvings has stimulated the study of their altered surface, of the composition of corrosion spots and of the conditions of their formation. The results of these studies and the information obtained are of great scientific importance for determining antiquity and detecting forgeries.
