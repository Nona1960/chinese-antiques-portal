# How to Distinguish Nephrite and Jadeite from Imitations

## The diagnosis of nephrite and jadeite

Methods for diagnosing nephrite and jadeite are based on determining the most important physical properties and internal features of these minerals.

The first assumption about the nature of a stone rests on establishing which mineral species it belongs to: whether it is nephrite or jadeite, quartz (chalcedony or dyed quartz), serpentinite and so on. This can easily be done by studying its colour, lustre and general appearance, but one can be sure that the identification is correct only by measuring one or another optical or physical constant.

Differences in the conditions under which minerals form, and in the synthesis of their analogues, are reflected in certain features of their growth and internal structure. These features show themselves in the zoning of crystals and in the distribution of colour and inclusions. The internal features of stones can be studied by the microscopic method of identification.

The resemblance of nephrite to certain varieties of serpentine ("jade") is so great that special methods were developed long ago in the East to distinguish true *yu* from its numerous imitations. Thus in one medieval lapidary, dating from about 1596, we read: "To learn whether there is true jade in a stone, look at it by lamplight; if within it you see a light as of the rising sun, then there is true jade in this stone."

## What are the diagnostic properties of gemstones?

The diagnostic properties of gemstones are used in gemmological examination. They are: transparency, lustre, colour, refraction, birefringence, hardness and density.

### Optical properties of gemstones

A preliminary conclusion about the name of a stone is made on the basis of visual diagnosis — by determining its transparency, lustre and colour.

**Transparency** is the ability of a solid body to transmit rays of light to a greater or lesser degree.

Transparency depends on the structure of the crystals and on the presence of cracks and of solid and gas-liquid inclusions. In fine-grained aggregates consisting of a multitude of tiny, differently oriented particles, light is refracted many times in various directions, scattered and reflected; such aggregates are therefore only slightly transparent, or entirely opaque, compared with single crystals of the same mineral. Translucent or opaque chalcedony, for example, is a cryptocrystalline variety of transparent quartz. The transparency of nephrite and jadeite is determined visually by viewing them against the light.

By degree of transparency, all gemstones are divided into:

- **transparent** — all colourless and lightly coloured stones through a plate of which (3–5 mm thick) an object can be seen clearly (Imperial jadeite);
- **translucent** — through which objects are seen indistinctly (white and grey nephrite, jadeite, opal, chrysoprase);
- **opaque** (black nephrite, marble, serpentine).

**Lustre.** Together with transparency, this is one of the most important diagnostic features. The lustre of a gemstone is determined visually.

Lustre is created by light reflected from the surface of the stone; its intensity — that is, the amount of reflected light — is the greater, the sharper the difference between the speed of light in air and in the stone; in other words, the higher the refractive index, the more intense the lustre.

The following kinds of lustre are distinguished: vitreous (emerald) and greasy or resinous (nephrite). Greasy and resinous lustre belong to the same type: the term "greasy" is applied to light-coloured minerals and "resinous" to dark-coloured ones.

**Colour** is one of the most characteristic distinguishing features of most gemstones. In gemmology the colour of a stone is determined visually against white paper.

## Which precise properties should be measured in the gemmological examination of jades?

For a more precise diagnosis of a gemstone it is recommended to use special gemmological instruments, with which optical and physical constants — refractive index, density, hardness and others — can be measured. The identification constants of jadeite, nephrite and some of their imitations are given in the following table.

| Material | Refractive index | Specific gravity | Hardness (Mohs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jadeite | 1.66–1.68 | 3.30–3.36 | 6.5–7.0 |
| Nephrite | 1.60–1.64 | 2.90–3.02 | 6.5 |
| Hydrogrossular | 1.74–1.75 | 3.60–3.67 | 7.0–7.5 |
| Bowenite | 1.56 | 2.58–2.62 | 4.0–5.0 |
| Verdite | 1.58 | 2.80–2.99 | 3.0 |
| Saussurite | 1.57–1.70 | 3.00–3.40 | 6.5 |
| Prehnite | 1.61–1.64 | 2.88–2.99 | 6.0 |

**Refraction.** The refractive index is an important optical constant of gemstones, directly connected with the phenomenon of the refraction of light. In gemmological practice, refractometers are widely used to determine the refractive indices of gemstones. Their measurement on a refractometer is based on the phenomenon of total internal reflection at the boundary between two media.

**Density** is the ratio of the mass of a substance to the mass of the same volume of water. The density of gemstones has no direct effect on their beauty. The density of precious stones ranges from 1 to 7. Stones with a density of up to 2 are considered light, from 2 to 4 normally heavy, and above 5 heavy.

There are a great many methods of determining density: gravimetric, volumetric, immersion, radiation, refractometric, analytical, X-ray and others. For gemstones the most convenient is perhaps the method of suspension in heavy immersion liquids, since it does not damage the material and is quite rapid. The liquids most often used are bromoform, CHBr₃ (density 2.89 g/cm³; diluted with alcohol), Thoulet's solution, HgI₂ + 2KI (density 3.17 g/cm³; diluted with water) and Clerici solution, CH₂(COOTl)₂ + HCOOTl (density 4.27 g/cm³; diluted with water). The density of the mineral under study is compared with that of the liquid, either undiluted or diluted with the appropriate diluent. Exact values of density are obtained by hydrostatic weighing.

**Hardness** is the ability of a gemstone to resist destruction; it determines the stone's resistance to abrasion during cutting, grinding, polishing and wear. It depends on the nature of the stone and is measured on the Mohs scale, on which the hardness of a mineral is expressed by the number of the reference mineral that scratches it. Most precious gemstones have a hardness above 7 (diamond 10; ruby and sapphire 9; topaz, spinel and alexandrite 8; emerald, aquamarine and beryl 7.5–8; garnet and tourmaline 7–7.5; amethyst 7, and so on).

## What should a diagnostic report contain?

A gemmological report on a carved object of jadeite or nephrite must contain data on the object: its mineralogical name, commercial name, the main colour of the stone, and the dimensions, weight and form of the object.

## Which imitations are sold as nephrite?

Less expensive ornamental stones resembling nephrite are often passed off as it: amazonite, serpentinite, vesuvianite, bowenite, jadeite, chalcedony, aventurine and agalmatolite. Green marble may also be used.

**Agalmatolite** is an opaque decorative ornamental stone, a variety of the mineral pyrophyllite. Various figurines and ornaments are carved from it. The term "agalmatolite" was first introduced into mineralogical nomenclature in 1797. Agalmatolites are white or grey with greenish and yellowish tints; red and even dark-cherry stones occur more rarely. Because of its smooth, "soapy" surface, agalmatolite is called "wax stone" or "soapstone". Different specimens may differ in composition and origin, but all are characterised by low hardness (1–2 or 2–3), a compact texture, and a micro-granular or finely scaly structure of their constituent minerals. The stone is so soft that it is easily worked with a knife or chisel. Besides South-East Asia, deposits of agalmatolite exist in the southern Urals, Uzbekistan, Ukraine, the Czech Republic, Romania and the USA. Extensive deposits exist in Mongolia.

**White chalcedony** is a white cryptocrystalline variety of silica. Chalcedony contains 90–99% silica, the highest content being characteristic of light-coloured and milky-white varieties. It forms compact masses, stalactitic forms, nodules and crusts, and fills cavities.

Chalcedony shows a more or less clearly distinguishable banding parallel to the outer surface of the formation or to the walls of the cavity it fills. This banding is not always visible to the naked eye, but it can be detected under the microscope in thin sections cut perpendicular to the outer surface, or revealed by staining and etching.

The colour of chalcedony is always rather pale. Usually the stones are grey, greyish-blue, milky-blue, pale blue, greyish-green, greenish-blue, yellowish or pale yellowish-brown, as well as greyish-white to light milky-white and almost colourless. Chalcedonies of intense or dark bluish-green or yellow colours, or of pinkish or reddish colour, are much rarer. The blue colour of some chalcedonies may disappear on heating. The colour is for the most part even, or changes only slightly from layer to layer.

Chalcedonies are as a rule translucent to transparent, or cloudy; individual layers in one specimen often differ in transparency.

The hardness of chalcedony varies somewhat; it is lower than that of coarsely crystalline quartz and is usually 6.5. Its density is 2.57–2.64, the variation depending on the quantity and composition of impurities, on porosity and on water content.

There is no cleavage. The fracture is uneven or conchoidal. Chalcedony splits easily across the banding and parallel to the elongation of the fibres, giving an uneven or splintery fracture with a fairly well-marked waxy lustre. A fracture parallel to the layering can also be obtained.

Chalcedony is more or less porous; isolated pores (or, more often, extremely fine tubes and thread-like voids) lie parallel to the fibrous structure. Porosity varies from layer to layer and is least in white or milky-white layers. Some stones stick to the tongue or absorb water rapidly, sometimes with a faint hissing sound or with bubbles forming on the moistened surface. The porosity of chalcedony and its varieties, including agate, allows these stones to be dyed artificially. **Dyed chalcedony is often sold under the trade name "Malaysia jade".**

**Chrysoprase** is a semi-precious ornamental stone, a variety of quartz and chalcedony (a cryptocrystalline form of silicon dioxide containing a small amount of nickel). Its colour is usually apple-green but may range to dark green and bluish-green. Chrysoprase is a cryptocrystalline aggregate of small crystalline grains indistinguishable to the naked eye and visible only under the microscope. Unlike emerald, which owes its colour to chromium, chrysoprase owes its colour to nickel in the form of dispersed microscopic inclusions. Like all forms of chalcedony, chrysoprase has a hardness of 6–7 on the Mohs scale and a conchoidal fracture. **It is often sold under the trade name "Australian jade".**

**Common opal** is an amorphous mineral of various colours consisting of silica and water, containing microcrystals of cristobalite and tridymite. It is a solid natural hydrogel of silicon dioxide (in other words, amorphous quartz) with a variable water content (from a few per cent to a third of the stone's weight). It occurs in zones of relatively moderate temperature and forms from a liquid mass of silica which, as it sets, retains a small amount of water in its composition.

Under the electron microscope opal appears as a fairly dense packing of identical submicroscopic spheres (globules) of silica, comparable in size to the wavelengths of light, arranged in a regular spatial lattice like a honeycomb (with regularly arranged voids between them) in a mass of silica gel. In common opals the packing of the globules is irregular and there is no play of colour.

Opal is brittle and not very hard: 5–6.5 on the Mohs scale; density 1,800–2,250 kg/m³. It dissolves in hydrofluoric acid and readily, almost completely, in alkalis. The fracture is usually conchoidal. Opal is optically isotropic, sometimes with anomalous birefringence. Its refractive indices are 1.44–1.46. Its lustre is vitreous, waxy or pearly. Opals may show a white fluorescence under ultraviolet light.

**Aventurine** is a fine-grained variety of quartz (quartzite). It is a rock consisting of quartz with small scattered inclusions of mica flakes, and sometimes of chlorite or haematite. It is a metamorphic rock formed by the compaction and recrystallisation of sandy-clayey sediments, most often sandstones, into quartzites and crystalline schists.

The colour of aventurine depends on the composition of its inclusions and impurities. It is usually red-brown, brownish, grey-yellow or green. Its characteristic sparkling golden sheen and shimmering lustre are given by inclusions of flakes of mica, goethite and haematite, and by fine cracks filled with iron hydroxides. Its hardness varies somewhat; it is lower than that of coarsely crystalline quartz and is usually 6.5; density 2.57–2.64.

In India green aventurine is found which is called "Indian jade". Aventurine has long been mined in China, where green aventurine was regarded as a sacred stone, was very popular and was called the "imperial stone". In ancient China the emperor's state seal was carved from green aventurine.

Texture and colour depend on the composition of the inclusions, the size of the grains and the evenness of distribution of the constituent minerals. Green aventurines are characterised by a homogeneous structure and even colour and are stronger, while golden-cherry and honey-yellow aventurines have the strongest sparkle. **Green aventurine quartz is called "Indian jade"**, although its spangles of fuchsite immediately distinguish it from true nephrite. To imitate natural green aventurine, glass-makers use chromium-bearing glasses.

**Serpentinite, or serpentine rock.** This is a metamorphic rock consisting mainly of serpentine minerals, which formed at the expense of such iron-magnesium minerals as olivine, orthopyroxenes and partly hornblendes. The primary rocks that underwent serpentinisation were dunites, peridotites, orthopyroxenites and, more rarely, amphibolites or amphibole schists. The purest serpentinite forms from peridotite. Serpentinite may pass very gradually, through a series of intermediate stages of alteration, laterally into the parent rock.

Serpentinites occur as irregular masses and lens-shaped bodies. Large peridotite massifs are often completely serpentinised.

The surface is smooth to the touch. The colour ranges from green to black. Some serpentinites are mottled, with alternating green and white patches, the white being due to branching veinlets of calcite or dolomite. **It is often sold under the trade names "New jade" or "Olive jade".**

In the USA the largest deposits of serpentinite adjoin the coastal ranges in the west of the country. In Great Britain they are widespread in Scotland and the Shetland Islands. In Russia serpentinites are widely represented in the Caucasus, the Urals, the Eastern Sayan, Tuva and other regions.

Polished blocks and slabs of serpentinite are used for interior finishing. Serpentinite with patches of calcite or dolomite (serpentine marble, or ophicalcite) takes a good polish and is used as a decorative stone.

**Bowenite** (tangiwai) is a translucent, nephrite-like variety of serpentine of pale-green colour. In rocks it occurs as veins, bedded deposits and disseminations. It is a typical mineral of hydrothermally altered ultramafic rocks and of contact-altered carbonate-calcium rocks.

It is named after its discoverer, George Bowen, who at first identified his find as nephrite but later established that the mineral was a variety of serpentine. Bowenite differs from nephrite in its much lower hardness but has a similar structure. It was discovered in 1822 and in 1966 became the state mineral of Rhode Island, where it was found. In recent years it has become increasingly popular in America.

Its colour ranges from pale yellow to dark green, grey and bluish. Three large deposits are known today: two in the USA and one in Italy. **It is often sold under the trade names "Bowen jade" or "Bowenite jade".**

**Prehnite** has an olive-yellowish colour and a radiating fibrous structure. It is strongly translucent, sometimes transparent.

**Marble** is a white or lustrous stone, a metamorphic rock consisting solely of calcite, CaCO₃, with admixtures of other minerals and of organic compounds. Impurities affect the quality of marble in different ways, reducing or enhancing its decorative qualities.

Recrystallisation of dolomite, CaMg(CO₃)₂, produces dolomitic marbles. Marble is the result of the process of metamorphism: under particular physico-chemical conditions the structure of limestone (a sedimentary rock of organic origin) changes, and marble is born. Hardness 2.5–3 on the Mohs scale; density 2.3–2.6 g/cm³.

Most coloured marbles have mottled or banded colouring. The pattern is determined not only by the structure of the marble but also by the direction in which the stone is sawn. The colour and pattern of marble are revealed after polishing.

Iron oxide colours marble red (sometimes pink or rust-tinged), finely dispersed iron sulphide blue-black, iron-bearing silicates (especially chlorite and epidote) green, and limonite (iron hydroxides) and iron and manganese carbonates yellow and brown. Grey, bluish and black colours may also be due to admixtures of bitumen or graphite. **Marble imitations of nephrite are sometimes called "Connemara marble".**

**Vesuvianite (californite).** Massive green vesuvianite (a complex natural silicate) is quite often passed off as nephrite. It forms prismatic or dipyramidal crystals and granular massive aggregates. Its colour — light yellow-green, brownish-green, bright green, dark brown to black — depends on composition and impurities. Hardness 6.5; density 3,350–3,450 kg/m³. Vesuvianite sometimes occurs as compact masses not divided into separate crystals; when green, in this form it has sometimes been taken for a variety of jade. **The dark-green varieties bear the trade name "californite".**

**Amazonite** is a bluish-green variety of the feldspar microcline. It is distinguished by its hardness and a vitreous to pearly lustre. It is often called "green moonstone", since moonstone too may be silky to the touch. Its colour varies: most common is apple-green; sometimes the stone is turquoise, and sometimes its tint verges on grey-yellow. The colour is often uneven. Hardness 6–6.5; density 2.55 g/cm³. Cleavage is perfect in two directions. The main diagnostic feature is cross-hatched (grid) twinning, but the bluish colour and the typical feldspar structure are easily recognised with the naked eye.

**Aventurine glass** is sometimes called "aventurine jade" in imitations. It is coloured glass with crystalline inclusions of chromium, iron compounds or copper that look like a multitude of golden sparks, outwardly resembling the natural mineral aventurine. Aventurine glass was first made in Venice at the beginning of the seventeenth century. Its origin is supposedly due to chance (Italian *avventura*): a Murano glassblower once accidentally spilled copper filings into molten glass and obtained an unexpected effect. The name given to the resulting composition was later transferred to the similar mineral, which until then had borne another name. Modern aventurine glass differs from natural aventurine in its lower hardness and the presence of varied inclusions.

## Advice on buying nephrite objects

Among nephrite collectors there are generally accepted recommendations for the rapid diagnosis of the stone. An imitation can be distinguished from nephrite by simple actions. Here are some of them.

1. **Thermal conductivity.** Even in warm surroundings nephrite feels cold in the hand. If you are unsure, hold a piece of nephrite in your closed palm until it warms to about the temperature of your body. Then put it aside for 30 seconds and touch it with the tip of your tongue. Real nephrite does not feel like a warm stone.
2. **Hardness.** In exceptional cases a blunt metal object may be drawn across an inconspicuous inner surface. If the metal easily leaves a scratch, the object is most likely a softer mineral (usually soft serpentine or agalmatolite) or some kind of polymer. Remember that bowenite (a hard variety of serpentine) sometimes "passes" the hardness test. Chalcedony always passes the test and is left unscratched; fortunately, chalcedony imitations are less common than those of softer serpentines. And of course genuine archaic nephrites, whose surface layer has been changing for millennia, cannot be tested in this way.
3. **Translucency and fibrous structure.** Visual examination can reveal a great deal. The translucency of nephrite creates a specific visual effect, unlike most imitations. Nephrite is characterised by a felted-fibrous structure and barely perceptible veining.
4. **Roughness.** Run a fingernail over the polished surface of the object. If it is made of glass, the nail will glide over it and will not feel the very fine roughness characteristic of the surface of genuine archaic nephrite. This sensation is explained by the fact that ancient nephrites were polished with soft abrasives; this specific working produces a slight "orange peel" effect on the surface of the carved object.
5. **Colour.** Although real nephrite comes in various colours, it always lacks the sickly yellow tint so characteristic of serpentines.
6. **A melodious ring.** Real nephrite objects produce a slightly more pleasant, resonant sound than objects made of plastic or glass.

## How to distinguish genuine nephrite objects from glass imitations

Glass is a transparent, lustrous, hard and brittle substance formed by melting sand mixed with soda or potash. In glass there is no regular arrangement of atoms.

Imitations of white nephrite made of an opaque variety of glass with a particularly dense structure, known as "Peking glass", are often sold on the market, frequently under deceptive names such as "Henan jade".

The main diagnostic recommendation is to look for air bubbles with a 10× loupe. Since glass imitations of nephrite contain air bubbles, the object should be examined against the light. Under 10× magnification glass gives itself away: tiny swirls and bubbles can be seen.
