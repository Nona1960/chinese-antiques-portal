# Appraisal of Nephrite and Nephrite Objects

## What are carved stone objects?

Carved stone objects are objects made from natural materials. They include various interior ornaments. Sculpture is also one kind of stone carving. Works of this kind of fine art are three-dimensional and can be viewed from all sides: this is sculpture in the round. **High-relief** sculpture shows figures and objects projecting from the ground to which they are joined. In **low-relief** sculpture the drawing itself is of the greatest importance; low relief is used for cameos. An **intaglio** is, as it were, an inverted low relief in which the image appears pressed into the stone. Indeed, intaglio carvers use a wax impression to check their work: when the wax is removed from the hollow, it bears a raised low-relief image, as on a cameo.

## How are carved stone objects classified?

Carved stone objects are divided into three groups: simple, complex and especially complex.

**Simple works** include table sculpture of everyday subjects (static figures of people and animals with generalised forms) and carving in shallow relief without careful working of texture.

**Complex works** are table sculpture (figures of people and animals in clothing with folds, in dynamic poses, with accessories), portrait and landscape bas-reliefs of fine design (gems, intaglios) with finely worked ornament, and table vases with pierced or raised sculptural plant or geometric ornament.

**Especially complex works** include table sculpture, group or single, in dynamic poses with complex accessories, carved with regard to the stone's pattern, colour and defects; and vases and small architectural objects with especially fine details and designs.

Sculpture in the round is the most labour-intensive and demands exceptional attention, since the object must look good from all sides. High- and low-relief sculptures are less labour-intensive but difficult to execute, since creating an effect of depth in a thin layer of material is a hard task.

Many carved stone objects may be classed as inexpensive commercial mass-produced goods. The signs of such commercial carvings are their crude form, lack of detail and glassy polish. The bright polish is meant to distract attention from the poor quality of the work.

## What is the algorithm for diagnosing and appraising antique nephrite carvings?

In the view of international experts on jade, nephrite artefacts should be dated using analytical and systematic approaches.

The composition of the material can be reliably and scientifically determined with gemmological instruments and devices and verified by the study of certain physico-chemical characteristics.

With a loupe or microscope, the secondary alterations of the surface should be observed and studied: differential weathering, diffusion markings, exposed crystal planes and secondary crystal planes. In addition, to determine the period to which a jade artefact belongs, the carving style, motifs and construction of the object are studied.

## Which colour of nephrite is considered the most valuable in antique objects?

Colour is one of the most important qualities of nephrite as a gem and ornamental stone. The colour descriptions used in practice — "tobacco", "lettuce", "apple" and so on — are subjective and imprecise. The most expensive is white, called **"mutton-fat" nephrite**. It is characterised by a greasy lustre that art dealers call "creamy". The texture of white mutton-fat nephrite is so fine that it is not easily seen with the naked eye (without a loupe). The most valuable nephrite is white with good translucency.

In gemmology the colour of a stone is determined visually against white paper in daylight.

## What is the purpose of treating nephrite?

Both jadeite and nephrite are porous, and to increase their appeal they are sometimes dyed green. Dyed material looks pinkish through a Chelsea filter. The Chelsea colour filter was specially developed to reveal differences between natural and artificially coloured green stones.

## Which factors affect the market value of nephrite and jadeite carvings?

To date, no scientific methods for appraising nephrite and jadeite objects have been established. International practice uses a weighted index of size and quality of the mineral (on 1 to 5 subjective criteria), comprising:

- the particular qualities of the raw material;
- historical significance;
- cultural importance;
- artistic meaning;
- craftsmanship.

Large size increases the value of nephrite.

According to the well-known expert on jade identification and appraisal Dr Daphne Lange Rosenzweig, the value of antique jade can be assessed by the following factors: physical properties (identification and quality of the material); size and condition of the carving (quality of workmanship); school, style and period; aesthetic properties (quality of impression); provenance (history of ownership); and the volatility of the market and of local taste.

In the practice of appraising jade, the market approach plays a considerable role — that is, the assessment of market value by comparing the prices of similar goods sold on the market.

It is recommended that nephrite carvings be appraised in the following sequence:

1. The tone, hue and saturation of the stone's colour. Have the different shades and colours of the material been used effectively to emphasise the theme of the carving?
2. The suitability of the material for the subject.
3. The absence of chips and cracks.
4. The quality of the working of details (expensive carvings have well-worked details and are finished at the front, back and even top).
5. The quality of the stands (fine objects are mounted on well-made, well-fitted stands; harmony between object and base is very important).
6. Whether the object has aesthetic appeal.
7. Whether the material has been treated.

In appraisal, upward coefficients are sometimes applied for the complexity of the technique of manufacture:

| Type of object | Coefficient |
|---|---|
| Objects assembled from flat plates | 1.1 |
| Objects of rotational form (bowls, vases, columns) consisting of two or three volumes | 1.3 |
| Objects of complex form with spherical concave-convex surfaces | 2.0 |
| Objects combining turning and carving with applied three-dimensional decoration in other materials | up to 10.0 |

In appraising works of stone carving with upward coefficients, originality of conception, level of execution and complexity of modelling must be taken into account.

## Features of the attribution of nephrite objects

Nephrite as a mineral is identified by visual and instrumental gemmological methods. Colour, lustre, translucency, hardness and refractive index are determined, and the absence or presence of luminescence and the features of inclusions are studied. The study of physical and optical properties helps to reveal imitations (serpentinite, bowenite, white chalcedony, aventurine and even "Peking glass"). Serpentine (which, unlike nephrite, has a sickly, dull yellow-green colour) and chalcedony, distinguished by a heavy opaque white colour, are most often used to imitate ancient artefacts.

There are certain particularities in determining where a jade artefact was worked. In Chinese decorative art, "archaic" usually means objects made before the beginning of the Han dynasty (about 200 BC). Each period of Chinese carved jade has the "signature and look" of its epoch.

The attribution of archaic Chinese carvings must begin with a visual examination of the external appearance for stylistic analysis. The next stage is the analysis of the features of working, in which the tool marks left on the surface of the carving are studied. Visual diagnosis of primitive technologies includes the study of the slight "orange peel" effect on the surface of ancient jades polished with soft abrasives. The absence of this feature reveals modern objects ground and polished on modern high-speed equipment with diamond pastes.

In the attribution of archaic jades the following questions are asked:

1. Is the carved motif characteristic of the supposed period of manufacture?
2. Are the features of the working marks characteristic of the supposed period?
3. Is there correspondence between the style of surface decoration and the form of the object?

## Which nephrite is more expensive — mined or found in rivers and streams?

In China nephrite was very highly valued. Plaques made of it circulated on a par with money, and nephrite weights served as measures of weight.

There are two kinds of nephrite whose prices differ sharply. One is extracted from mountain workings; the other is found in streams and rivers. The second is valued much more highly, since after millions of years of the action of flowing water what remains is the "heart of the jade" — its hard and homogeneous part.

Judging by the varied colour of the stones found in rivers and streams, various genetic types of nephrite occur there, accumulating in placers both as small pebbles and as large boulders and blocks up to 5 metres across. During transport and working by the water flow, nephrite undergoes natural enrichment through the removal from its surface of fragments and inclusions of less resistant rocks and fissured blocks, and it is therefore of high quality. Pebbles are often covered with layers of coloured nephrite called ***pi*** ("skin"), which are highly prized.

Thanks to its great toughness and resistance, nephrite is carried by water for 200 kilometres or more. Because of nephrite's anomalous resistance to chemical and physical weathering, its primary deposits are usually destroyed only by glacial erosion. The result is polished blocks weighing up to several tens of tonnes, scattered in the deposits of glacial moraines. When moraines are washed out, modern block-and-boulder placers of nephrite accumulate in river valleys.

The price of an unworked pebble ranges from 10 dollars per gram to an absolute maximum of 502 dollars per gram, depending on the thickness and homogeneity of the material. Mutton-fat nephrite is valued more highly.

The presence on the surface of reddish-rust inclusions that can be successfully worked into the design of the carving raises the valuation. Slight zoning of colour, other inclusions, and zones of dull brown colour lower the price of a nephrite pebble.

## How does the quality of jadeite affect the value of a carving?

At present there is no generally accepted system for grading the quality of jadeite. Some gemmologists consider colour the most important quality of jadeite, others translucency, others a combination of various properties, and so on. In the markets of America, Canada and Europe, where jadeite was arriving increasingly, the Eastern traditions of assessing the properties and value of the stone did not suit gemmologists and businessmen at all. Many of them held that a single pure colour is best, but that combinations — green and lavender, red and green, white and green — are also desirable.

In all cases except white, the degree of translucency increases the value; colourless translucent jadeite, however, is cheaper. To achieve the highest price, colour zoning must be minimal.

On the basis of long practice and established traditions, attempts have been made to work out international quality requirements for jadeite while retaining the names and terms in use. The proposed groupings (classifications, characteristics of the stone) concerned mainly the green, almost transparent, most popular and expensive "Imperial" variety.

A very important characteristic of jadeite quality is its tone. Texture and translucency are also integral characteristics of its quality.

The texture of jadeite varies from fine to coarse and can be examined with a 10× loupe. The best texture is smooth and uniform, without cracks or inclusions. These properties are best seen if the jadeite is held against the light or placed over a small lamp to observe how the light passes through.

An American gemmological association has proposed grading the texture and transparency of jadeite, like its tone, on a five-point scale, although the score may change depending on how a particular quality of the stone affects the object.

Translucency, purity of colour and the size of inclusions are of particular importance for stones of jewellery quality, whereas for carvings the last of these plays no essential role. Hong Kong jewellers, for example, use apparent defects of the stone in their work to emphasise the idea of the object (stamens in a flower, eye sockets in faces and so on).

Three grades of jadeite are now distinguished on the international market:

- **"Imperial"** — green jewellery stone;
- **"Commercial"** — jewellery and ornamental stone;
- **"Utility"** — ornamental stone.

The last two grades are to some extent conventional, since they are used mainly in stone carving.

For Imperial jadeite, as for all precious stones, the price rises sharply with the weight of the piece.

## How does treatment affect the value of jadeite?

In diagnosing and appraising jade carvings it is necessary to determine whether the stone has been treated.

In international trade, jadeites are divided into three categories according to the presence of artificial treatment:

- **Type A** — natural, untreated stones. They undergo only the traditional process of polishing with beeswax. They have their "true" colour and have not been subjected to treatment by high temperature or high pressure.
- **Type B** — chemically bleached jadeites subsequently impregnated with polymers. Polymer is introduced into such stones by centrifuge to increase their transparency, and they are given a hard plastic coating. It must be remembered that over time the polymer changes colour and may break down under the action of heat or household detergents.
- **Type C** — chemically bleached and dyed stones. Jadeite has a porous surface, thanks to which it is easily dyed while retaining its appearance. Such artificially dyed jadeites are liable to fade over time through reaction with strong light, body heat or household detergents.

## How can treated and artificially dyed jadeites be identified?

Identification is a problem. At present Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy are used for diagnosis, but these are very expensive methods.

There are five common signs detectable by direct examination:

1. traces of polymer can sometimes be seen;
2. "floating" platelets;
3. a concentration of filling substances can sometimes be seen in cracks and hollows;
4. when an area of the surface is touched with the heated tip of a thermal tester, a specific smell appears if the jadeite is type B;
5. when type B jadeite is struck with a metal rod or another piece of stone, the sound produced is not as clear as that of pure jadeite.

## What is particular about the technology of treating nephrite?

In the USSR a technology was developed for treating variously coloured nephrites from a number of Soviet deposits, and objects made from them — polished and unpolished cabochons of dirty-grey, tobacco-green and tobacco-grey nephrite. Its fundamental difference from known methods of treating other gemstones is that, on the basis of the study of the conditions of nephrite formation, parameters largely close to the natural ones are recreated. The improvement is achieved not by introducing or removing chromophore elements but by redistributing them within the specimen and returning to the stone its original structure and appearance. The specific parameters of the process are naturally adjusted according to the deposit and the character of the initial material, and to the task of obtaining the required colour variety of nephrite.

In the autoclave treatment of nephrites the process is conducted in media of different composition depending on the specific aims of the treatment. To obtain white varieties from apocarbonate yellow and brown nephrite free of carbonate admixture, and light-green varieties from the tobacco-brown nephrites of Ulan-Khodo, the process is conducted in a reducing medium (90% H₂O + 10% H₂), created either by pumping in hydrogen or by the reaction with water of a calculated quantity of metallic aluminium placed in the autoclave, at temperatures of 400–500 °C for 20–60 hours depending on the types and sizes of the specimens.

The colour of Ulan-Khodo nephrite is very uneven. The darker, dirty-green variety acquires a greenish-grey, mottled and cloudy colour after treatment. This reaction to treatment is connected with a fairly considerable quantity of carbonate and serpentine impurities in the original specimen.

Some varieties, however, do not respond to treatment, although they change their colour considerably, taking on greyish, whitish, mottled, non-translucent tones. Thus the quality of the incoming raw material — its "purity" with respect to admixtures of carbonates, serpentine and other minerals — largely determines the results of nephrite treatment.

## Which treatment technologies are used to improve the quality of jadeite?

Natural jadeite is now widely treated: it is dyed, bleached and stabilised with polymers, and it is also used to make doublets and triplets (assembled stones).

A method of treating natural jadeite developed in 1977 by specialists of the All-Union Research Institute for the Synthesis of Mineral Raw Materials consists of dyeing with a solution of Alcian green after preliminary treatment with an aqueous solution of nitric acid mixed with basic nickel carbonate. After an hour in the dye solution at 100 °C, the specimens acquire a bright green colour. They are then washed in hot solution and placed in molten paraffin to fix the colour obtained. After the specimen is removed from the paraffin it is carefully wiped with a soft dry cloth, as a result of which it acquires a mirror lustre and is ready for use.

The bright green colour obtained is close to that of natural green jadeite, is stable at room temperature to various solvents (alcohol, petrol, acetone and so on), and shows high resistance to ultraviolet light over a long period.

## Terms used on the antiques market

**Archaic jade** — a genuine object, that is, one actually carved in the stated period. "Archaic" in Chinese art as a rule means the period before the Han dynasty, that is, before about 200 BC.

**Copy** — a deliberate copy of an archaic jade, sold precisely as a copy, without intent to deceive.

**Fake** — a reproduction sold deceptively as an ancient jade.
