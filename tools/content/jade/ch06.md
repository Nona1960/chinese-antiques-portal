# Scientific Study of Ancient Chinese Bronzes

## How does native copper differ from smelted copper?

Copper is a ductile, pale-pink metal with a melting point of 1083 °C, a density of 8.96 g/cm³ and a face-centred cubic lattice. Its distinctive features are low electrical resistance, high thermal conductivity and good corrosion resistance in many natural environments (the atmosphere, the earth, sea and fresh water). The last property is explained both by the chemical stability of copper itself (copper is more electropositive than hydrogen) and by the stability of the corrosion products that form on it.

Impurities have a considerable effect on the mechanical, technological and physical properties of copper. Soluble impurities (Al, Sn, Zn and others) and their combinations are a kind of diagnostic sign that defines the technology and the period of manufacture of objects.

Native copper began to be used before people learned to smelt copper from ore.

To obtain smelted copper one needs ore; charcoal to create a reducing atmosphere; a high temperature, which depends on the type of furnace and the device for forcing air into it; and fluxes, which make it possible to separate the waste rock from the metallic minerals by forming slags.

The original copper ore in antiquity was most probably an oxidised ore that attracted attention by its colour, such as malachite, but sulphide ores were also used. The presence of sulphide inclusions in the smelted metal supports this view.

## What is bronze?

Bronzes are alloys of copper with tin in various proportions by weight, but with copper predominating.

The colour of bronze changes with its composition: as the percentage of tin increases, the colour passes from pink and red (90–99% copper) to yellow (up to 85% copper), then to white (up to 72% copper) and finally to steel-grey (up to 35% copper).

Tin bronze did not appear at once in most mining and metallurgical centres. It was preceded by arsenical bronze. Alloys of copper with arsenic could be natural: arsenic is present in a number of copper ores and partly passes into the metal during smelting. An admixture of arsenic was considered to impair the quality of bronze.

The presence of metals other than tin in ancient bronzes should be regarded as incidental impurities. Such impurities include zinc, lead, antimony, iron, silver, sometimes nickel, cobalt and gold, and other metals that evidently entered the alloy directly from the copper and tin ores during smelting.

## What is brass?

Brass, or "yellow copper", is an alloy of copper with zinc in various proportions, usually about 32%. Brass is distinguished by a beautiful yellow colour.

## What does the term "Chinese bronze" mean?

In the history of Chinese bronze there are two periods of flowering: the late Shang dynasty and the early Zhou dynasty.

Chinese bronze is a unique phenomenon in world culture, thanks to the quality and scale of its casting and the complexity of its models. At first this aroused heightened interest among collectors, connected with the "closed" nature of Chinese history and culture. The West "discovered" Chinese art, and then became enthusiastic about it — in particular about metalwork — at a time when there were as yet no systematic archaeological excavations in China itself; these began to be carried out regularly only from the mid-twentieth century. The scientific study of Chinese metallurgy, metalworking and Chinese metal objects began only in the second half of the twentieth century.

The forms of the bronze vessels used in ceremonies of worship of Heaven and the ancestors in the Shang period constantly changed, but the vessels for food and wine invariably demonstrated the status of their owner and remained the embodiment of ritual. There are nevertheless differences between Shang and Zhou bronzes: under the Shang, wine vessels predominated; under the Zhou, food vessels.

Among antique dealers and art dealers, the term "Chinese bronze" denotes ancient ritual vessels richly decorated with *taotie* ornament. They often bear decorative inscriptions, especially objects of the late Shang period; these inscriptions gave the clan and name of the owner. At the beginning of the Western Zhou, more extended inscriptions appear on bronzes.

## To which periods does the flowering of art casting in China belong?

Bronze casting on the Central Plain of China probably goes back to the late Neolithic (2500–2000 BC). The Bronze Age in China is defined as 2000–500 BC.

The development of bronze-casting technology led to the appearance of more effective tools, raising the productivity of labour, and to the making of more effective weapons.

Until recently it was believed that the Chinese borrowed bronze casting in the first third of the second millennium BC. Archaeological excavations of the late twentieth century have convincingly shown that centres of Chinese metallurgy arose as early as the third millennium BC.

## How do ancient bronzes differ from modern bronze alloys?

Ancient bronze objects are often very inhomogeneous in different parts of one and the same object; in addition, porosity and other casting defects are often found. Nickel, cobalt, silver, zinc and iron are normal impurities in ancient bronzes, and their authentication usually presents no difficulty.

Modern art bronze differs from ancient bronze in that, besides copper and tin, it contains a considerable amount of zinc (sometimes up to 35–40%), added to make the alloy cheaper.

## The Copper Age and the Bronze Age in China

Copper has been mined in China since the most ancient times. To date, Chinese archaeologists have investigated 252 vertical shafts descending to depths of up to 50 metres, with numerous horizontal adits and crawlways.

The artistic Bronze Age in China begins at the end of the Xia dynasty (early seventeenth century BC) and continues through the Shang (1600–1046 BC), the Western Zhou (1046–771 BC) and the Eastern Zhou (770–221 BC). In this period only the nobility were permitted to use precious bronze vessels. Copper was used mainly for ritual objects.

## When did arsenical bronzes appear?

The age of ancient bronze begins precisely with arsenical bronze.

In the principal cultures of the ancient world known to us (the Mediterranean, the Near East, North Africa), arsenical bronze appeared millennia earlier than tin bronze. Although arsenic is often present in copper ores, there is not very much of it. A useful reference figure here is 0.5% — the arsenic content of bronze regarded by historians of metallurgy as a kind of boundary between natural and artificial bronzes.

Researchers have concluded that up to 0.5% arsenic in smelted bronze could be obtained from its natural impurities in copper ore. To obtain an arsenic content of 0.5% or more, the ancient metallurgist had to add an arsenic-bearing ore — for example arsenopyrite (FeAsS) — to the charge deliberately.

This "boundary" half per cent is very telling, since even the most ancient objects of arsenical bronze contain noticeably more arsenic. This indicates that even at the dawn of metallurgy craftsmen deliberately added arsenical ore to the charge.

The presence of arsenic in copper alloys in concentrations of 0.1% and more is a good diagnostic sign: it testifies to the use of so-called blister copper, non-standard in the modern sense of the word and more characteristic of antiquity. The presence of antimony, silver and gold should be interpreted in the same way (some ancient bronzes contain up to 1% silver and up to 0.1% gold).

Smelting bronze from ore is much simpler than smelting copper. Bronzes can be smelted at a lower temperature, since most impurities (arsenic, antimony, tin and so on) lower the melting point of the alloy (bronze with 20% arsenic, for example, melts almost 400 °C below pure copper). The reduction of metals from oxides on charcoal (by carbon monoxide) can proceed at temperatures below the melting point of copper.

Experimental smelting of copper-arsenic ore imitating primitive technology (on charcoal, with carbon monoxide), carried out by colleagues at the All-Russian Research Institute of Restoration, confirmed this thesis: all the arsenic passed from the ore into the metal, but not all the copper — only a part of it, determined by the temperature of the furnace (that is, corresponding to the melting point of an alloy of copper with the corresponding amount of arsenic).

For the same reason there is a group of rarely encountered high-antimony copper alloys (bronzes) containing more than 8–10% arsenic and antimony, sometimes even up to 30%. Contrary to the view still current among archaeologists that copper was alloyed directly with these elements, we note that in reality such alloys were of course obtained directly by low-temperature smelting of copper ores rich in arsenic and/or antimony. There are absolutely no archaeological data showing that arsenic and antimony were smelted as separate metals in the Early Bronze Age and used to alloy copper.

Greater success in modelling ancient smelting was achieved by the Soviet scholars V. Pazukhin and F. Tavadze, who devoted many years to the study of the most ancient metallurgy. They proved that a reducing atmosphere is undoubtedly possible in an ordinary heap of charcoal, provided enough of it has accumulated in the fire and it is sufficiently compacted by logs laid on top, so that it is protected from being blown through by the wind. In such a fire, without any special devices, they succeeded in smelting pure copper: malachite and chrysocolla "sweated out" pure metal under a thick layer of burnt-out charcoal.

## When did tin bronzes appear?

In the developed Bronze Age another metal, tin, began to be smelted, and tin bronzes appeared. The concentration of tin in copper may vary widely, but it is generally accepted that bronze containing more than 1% tin is an artificially produced alloy, since tin and copper are not, as a rule, associated in nature (which, however, is not strictly true, since copper-tin minerals do occur, though extremely rarely).

It is supposed that the main method of producing bronze in antiquity was cementation, that is, the reduction of cassiterite (tin ore) by charcoal on the surface of molten copper, with simultaneous saturation of the copper with tin.

Another ore — a sulphide in which copper and tin are present together — is stannite. Tin bronze can be obtained directly from stannite.

## Features of the composition of old brasses and bronzes

The expert examination of bronze and brass objects is based on the presence of the requisite concentrations of tin, arsenic, antimony, zinc, cadmium, bismuth, lead, silver and gold.

In antiquity (before the mid-eighteenth century in Europe and the mid-fifteenth century in the East), zinc was introduced into brass not as a metal but through the joint smelting of copper and zinc ores (for example, calamine). In museum literature this process is conventionally called the cementation of copper with zinc, although in metallurgical terms the name does not always correspond to the processes actually taking place.

It is considered established that in cementation the zinc concentration in brass cannot exceed 28%, but this figure seems to us rather a limiting value. It appears that after a single cementation the zinc concentration in copper lies on average in the range of 5–14%.

It has been shown experimentally that in the early periods of its production zinc was apparently regarded as a rare and expensive metal and could well substitute for silver in objects.

A useful criterion for determining the antiquity of brass is the concentration of cadmium. It is considered, for example, that in Chinese brasses cadmium concentrations below 0.5% are characteristic of cementation, used there until the mid-seventeenth century, while higher concentrations are characteristic of the addition to copper of metallic zinc obtained by distillation in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries.

Cadmium is entirely absent from modern brasses: during distillation it is concentrated mainly in the so-called zinc dust, not in the liquid zinc.

## What features characterise the ancient bronze of the Shang era?

The use of bronze in China reached its flowering in the Shang era, as is shown in particular by archaeologists' finds of hundreds of ritual vessels of high artistic quality.

Cast bronze vessels in the Shang dynasty were used in state rituals of communication with the ancestors or gods. The belief that the spirits of the dead had the power to influence events on earth was very important in early Chinese culture. Since the spirits were all-powerful, they had to be propitiated. The most important way of doing so was considered to be periodic offerings of food and drink in bronze vessels, during which the spirits were invited to take part in the ritual meal.

## What lay at the basis of the decoration of bronze vessels in the Shang-Yin period?

The Shang-Yin period of Chinese history (sixteenth to eleventh centuries BC) is named after the tribe that settled the Yellow River valley in the second millennium BC. It was then that the first Chinese state was formed, headed by a ruler, the *wang*, who was at the same time the supreme priest.

At that time considerable changes took place in every sphere of life of the inhabitants of China: silk-weaving, bronze casting and writing in characters were invented, and the foundations of town-planning were laid.

The capital of the state, the great city of Shang, near present-day Anyang, unlike the most ancient settlements, had a clear plan. Among other buildings the ruler's palace stood out, erected on a rectangular platform faced with sea pebbles. The tall thatched roof of the palace rested on three rows of wooden columns, whose bases were bronze discs.

The ancient Chinese saw the will of spirits and gods in all natural phenomena. They deified clouds and rain, wind and thunder, which they represented in the form of dragons, birds and beasts. But people needed reliable protectors, and so another belief arose — the cult of ancestors. It was believed that the spirits of the ancestors cared for their descendants. In turn, the souls of the dead ancestors were also served: their graves were tended and sacrifices accompanied by ritual feasts were made. Sacrifices were offered to the supreme god Shangdi, to local deities, to deceased rulers, their wives and sisters, to high officials and so on. Through sacrifice and consultation of the oracle the supreme ruler established contact with the deity.

The people wrote down their requests and wishes on specially prepared oracle bones — sheep's shoulder blades — or on tortoise shells. They divined about natural phenomena (eclipses, rain, wind, snow). In the practice of ritual divination the most ancient pictographic writing was born — one of the main achievements of Shang civilisation and the prototype of modern Chinese writing in characters.

When the state formed in China, the idea arose of Heaven as the mighty supreme deity of the universe. The ancient Chinese believed that their country lay at the centre of the Earth, which was square and flat, and that the Heaven above China was circular. That is why they called their country *Zhongguo* (the Middle Kingdom) or *Tianxia* (All under Heaven). In different seasons abundant sacrifices were made to Heaven and Earth, and special altars were built for this purpose outside the city: round ones for Heaven, square ones for Earth.

Many works of the decorative crafts intended for ritual ceremonies in honour of the spirits of ancestors and of the deities governing the forces of nature have come down to us. The ritual bronze vessels used for sacrifices are distinguished by their mastery of execution. In these heavy monolithic objects all the ideas of the world formed by that time were united.

The outer surfaces of the vessels were covered with relief. The main place was given to images of birds and dragons, embodying the elements of Heaven and water; of cicadas, which foretold a good harvest; and of bulls and rams, which promised people plenty and well-being. A very frequent motif in the decoration of bronze vessels is the *taotie*, the image of a zoomorphic demon mask.

The vessels were very varied in form. Sacrificial meat was cooked in huge rectangular cauldrons on four legs with two handles (*fang ding*). The tall, slender beaker widening at top and bottom (*gu*) was intended for sacrificial wine. On the surface of these vessels a fine spiral "thunder pattern" (*leiwen*) was usually depicted, against which the main images were executed. Three-dimensional animal masks seem to grow out of the bronze. The vessels themselves often had the form of animals and birds, since they were meant to guard man and protect the crops from evil forces. The surface of such vessels was entirely filled with projections and engraving. The bizarre and fantastic form of ancient Chinese bronze vessels with dragons was ordered by four vertical raised flanges placed at the sides. These flanges oriented the vessels to the cardinal points, emphasising their ritual character.

All the objects, and the patterns decorating statues and bronze utensils, had a magical meaning and were connected by a single symbolism (the *taotie* zoomorphic mask).

## How did Shang bronze vessels differ from the bronzes of the Sanxingdui culture?

Metalworking in the ancient Sanxingdui culture appears in the middle or late Shang period (no later than the thirteenth century BC) and developed in parallel with the Shang tradition, differing from it in many respects.

At Sanxingdui objects of pure copper are found, but various bronze alloys predominate: tin + copper, lead + copper, and tin + lead + copper. Most of the bronzes are of the last alloy. The impurities in the bronze depended on the purpose of the object: in ritual objects, for example, the tin content was fairly low and the lead content high, while in everyday objects it was the reverse.

In Sanxingdui bronzes of copper + tin and tin + lead alloys, phosphorus is also often recorded, a feature of the metallurgy of this culture. Phosphorus increases the ductility of bronze and raises the strength and hardness of the metal.

## What were the technologies of ancient bronze casting?

To identify the signs of antiquity, we studied experimental work modelling ancient smelting technologies. The results confirmed our hypothesis that in some cases ancient founders did not so much select the components of the alloy as use ores with natural inclusions of various minerals.

Of particular interest for analysing the signs of antiquity of bronze vessels are studies of the technology of the earliest smelting of metals, the features of the ore and of the deposits where it was mined. But such studies are difficult because of the scarcity of archaeological evidence (finds of furnaces, crucibles and original slag are rare).

It is well known that special moulds consisting of three or more symmetrical parts were made for casting. These mould sections were dried, fired and then assembled together with an outer casing, leaving a gap equal to the intended thickness of the bronze object. The molten alloy was poured into the mould. When the metal had cooled, the mould was broken and removed.

To give the mould material special properties (such as low shrinkage on drying, high resistance to thermal shock, low thermal conductivity and good venting during pouring of the metal), ancient moulds were made of fine clay mixed with silt, sand and plant ash. Chinese archaeologists have confirmed this in their studies. The plasticity and strength of the moulds could be increased by adding organic binders such as dung and honey.

## How do scholars date the manufacture of bronze vessels?

Ancient Chinese bronze vessels are distinguished by unique artistic and decorative qualities.

Early Chinese bronze can be dated with high reliability only if it comes from professionally documented archaeological excavations.

Much work has therefore been devoted to the study of the stable isotopes of lead, which are the "passports" of ancient artefacts. Lead has four stable isotopes with atomic weights of 204, 206, 207 and 208. After smelting from ore, the isotopic composition of lead from a particular deposit remains constant and does not change with hot or cold working, with corrosion or with alloying with other metals.

Lead isotopes are also used to reveal modern forgeries of ancient bronzes, even the most skilful. But the composition of the bronze and the features of its patina are also important diagnostic signs of antiquity.

## For what purposes were ritual bronze vessels used in ancient China?

Bronzes have been made in China for about 3,700 years. Most bronze objects of 1500–300 BC are ritual vessels bearing the names of deified ancestors. Many were cast specially to commemorate important events in the lives of their owners. Ritual bronze vessels also served as family heirlooms passed down by inheritance.

Of particular interest to museums and collectors are wine vessels, intended for warming wine and for ritual libations. They were placed on altars together with other ritual bronze vessels.

Wine vessels became widespread in the Shang-Yin and early Western Zhou periods. Wine vessels were made in the form of animals and birds, as tripods, square, round and on stands (feet). They usually have symmetrical forms and intricate patterns covering the whole surface. In some cases the decoration is in high relief.

## What is the principal decoration on ancient bronze vessels?

The principal decoration of ritual bronze vessels is the mask of the monster *taotie*. This decoration is a traditional ornament of Chinese bronze and has been used since antiquity. It is believed to depict a man-eating monster defeated by the mythical Yellow Emperor, who decreed that its severed head should henceforth be depicted on bronze vessels as a symbol of his victory. The elongated ears, horns, upper lip and protruding nose of the mask are modelled. The "face" is formed of small stylised *kui* dragons shown in profile, with large, protruding relief eyes.

## From which deposits, and which ore, did ancient metallurgists use to cast sacrificial vessels in ancient China?

It is well known that the ore from each deposit has a specific set of trace impurities peculiar to that source alone. During smelting the composition and quantity of these impurities may change somewhat, but this can be taken into account. In this way certain "tags" can be obtained that characterise the metals of a particular deposit, group of deposits or mining centre.

In antiquity bronze was apparently smelted from easily fusible carbonate-oxide ores of the oxidation zones of sulphides in basaltoids. It is precisely these zones that are characterised by the accumulation of copper, nickel, platinum-group metals, tellurium, lead and zinc. The ease of extraction of such enriched surface ores is also significant. Finds of bronzes of unusual chemical composition point to the possibility of discovering rich sulphide (primary) deposits of copper, nickel, cobalt and platinum-group metals in the area of archaeological finds of ancient bronze. On the basis of our analyses we incline to the view that deposits of this type exist in the basalts of the region.

## Features of the scientific study of ancient bronzes

The most important studies by elemental analysis aim to answer questions about the origin and spread of ancient copper and bronze metallurgy. They have made it possible to move from approximate visual estimates to precise quantitative characteristics of the components of the alloy and to distinguish different types of copper-based alloys.

Linear weathering crusts are of great thickness. This is connected in particular with the fact that the rocks in these more permeable zones were first worked by hydrothermal solutions and only then subjected to weathering.

We set ourselves the task of identifying indicators that would allow a more precise attribution of a bronze vessel. In studying a particular vessel, for example, the question is asked whether the minerals for it came from one ore occurrence or from several. It turned out that this is the standard set of minerals of the linear weathering crusts of deposits or ore occurrences of one type. During weathering, not only are primary minerals destroyed, but still more numerous new, supergene minerals arise. Most clay minerals, numerous sulphates, carbonates, minerals of iron, aluminium, manganese and titanium oxides and many others belong to fault zones or to the contacts of strata of different composition.

The formation of weathering products depends closely on physical and geographical conditions — above all, on the warm and humid climate of the regions of the Shang kingdom in ancient China.

The combination of copper, nickel, antimony and platinum-group metals in the absence of chromium indicates that the ancient miners worked zones of sulphide copper-nickel deposits not in ultramafic rocks. Such ore was mined from the oxidation zones of mafic rocks.

## Which rare impurities in ancient bronzes can serve as a diagnostic sign of antiquity?

Platinum-group metals are a very rare impurity. Their presence means that ore with an admixture of platinum occurred in the weathering crust exploited in this period of history — and platinum defines the type of ore. Since ancient metallurgists used easily fusible ore from the oxidation zones of primary ores of mixed composition with an admixture of platinum-group minerals, it has so far been established beyond doubt that these rare impurities in ancient copper ore are characteristic only of the Shang era.

## Which scientific methods are used to attribute ancient bronze?

The technical examination of metal objects in a modern museum is based, on the one hand, on comparing the composition of the metal (including trace impurities) with known specimens taken as standards for the given time and place of production, and, on the other, on the correspondence of the method of manufacture of the object under study to the reference historical technology.

The method of determining the antiquity of an object used in antiques practice, based solely on the composition and type of patina, in many cases does not work, because of the variety and sophistication of methods of artificial patination. At the same time, knowledge of the main types and structure of the natural patina formed on metals of different compositions is a useful additional sign in expert examination.

Before choosing a method for studying a bronze vessel, we studied modern approaches to the archaeological investigation of ancient bronzes.

Compositional analyses by electron-probe microanalysis (EPMA) of more than 200 bronze objects found in Shaanxi province in China (a border area of the Shang state) yielded several interesting findings, such as original alloys with additions of nickel (copper + arsenic + nickel).

Of particular interest were methodological approaches to studying the compositions, trace elements and microstructures of ancient alloys and their relation to production processes.

Relevant to identifying signs of antiquity are the features of the corrosion of bronze coins, studied by optical microscopy, X-ray diffraction, scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive spectrometry.

As this review has shown, the identification of signs of antiquity in bronze objects must rest on a comprehensive archaeometallurgical study, which allows a fuller understanding of the level of metallurgical production and of the features of prospecting, mining and smelting ore.

## When did imitations of ancient sacrificial vessels begin to be made?

Most of the bronze ritual objects made in ancient China were lost in the tenth to twelfth centuries, which made the performance of ritual ceremonies very difficult. This led to the need to reproduce ancient bronzes, and the newly made objects had to look ancient. Already at that time there was a scholarly treatise describing techniques for producing a complex patina artificially. Artificial patina was obtained by burying the object in earth that was watered with plant juices.

The practice of copying ancient bronzes continued in later periods, under the Ming and Qing. A recipe is known from a manuscript of the Ming dynasty (1368–1644) recommending chlorides, strong vinegar and heating to create a patina — that is, judging by publications, the methods of producing patina were different.

Imitations made to look ancient are of a certain interest to science. In the British Museum and the Victoria and Albert Museum, for example, which have fine collections of Chinese bronze, identified forgeries are included in the displays.

## What is ancient patina?

In identifying signs of antiquity one should distinguish the types of corrosion (patina) on the object under study. Objects of copper and its alloys become covered over time with a protective film called patina. Patina can serve as an indicator of how ancient an object is.

Archaeological finds of bronzes of the Shang and Zhou eras in China prove that ancient bronzes undergo the destructive action of corrosion over a long time (300 to 2,500 years).

The tint of ancient patinas is most often greenish, but other colours occur: blue, black, grey, brown, pink and red. The colour of the patina depends greatly not only on the components of the original alloy but also on where the object lay, and under what conditions and for how long it was kept. Sometimes several tints can be seen combined on one object.

Patina may be matt or glossy. Its thickness may vary; a thin, even layer following the relief of the object is particularly prized.

The conditions under which the metal was smelted have a considerable influence on the patina.

The composition and conditions of formation of patinas on copper and copper alloys define the technology and the period of manufacture of objects.

In composition, patina consists of copper compounds, the most common being malachite and azurite. The patina of bronze objects also contains tin oxides. Accordingly, if other metals are present in the original alloy, their oxides will also enter into the composition of the patina. The patina of bronze objects consists in its main part of the same salts, but with the addition of tin compounds; if the bronze also contains lead and zinc, lead and zinc salts also enter into the composition of the patina.

The formation of the various types of patina depends on the following factors:

- the qualitative and quantitative composition of the alloy;
- the method of making the alloy and the thoroughness with which its components were mixed during melting;
- fine or coarse crystallisation of the metal;
- the place where the object lay and the conditions it was in;
- the length of time during which the artefact was exposed to any influences.

## How does corrosion form on ancient bronzes?

Patina is the result of the corrosion of a copper alloy. Corrosion is the destruction of copper alloys caused by chemical and electrochemical processes developing on the surface of an object as it interacts with its environment. As a result of corrosion, metals pass into stable compounds — oxides or salts — in the form in which they occur in nature.

The environment in which a metal corrodes is called a corrosive or aggressive environment.

It is known that most metals (except silver, copper and gold) occur in nature in an ionic state — as oxides, sulphides, carbonates and so on, usually called metal ores. The ionic state is characterised by a low internal energy. This is evident when metals are obtained from ores, and it is characteristic of corrosion too. The energy absorbed in reducing a metal from its compounds shows that the free metal has a higher energy than the metal compound. As a result, a metal in contact with a corrosive environment tends to pass into an energetically favourable state with a lower store of energy. In other words, the root cause of corrosion is the thermodynamic instability of the system made up of the metal and the components of the surrounding (corrosive) environment. The measure of this instability is the free energy released when the metal interacts with these components.

Free energy in itself, however, does not yet determine the rate of the corrosion process — the quantity most important for assessing the corrosion resistance of a metal. In a number of cases the adsorption or phase layers (films) arising on the metal surface as a result of incipient corrosion form so dense and impermeable a barrier that corrosion stops or is very strongly retarded. In service, therefore, a metal with a great affinity for oxygen may prove not less but more resistant (the free energy of oxide formation of silicon or aluminium, for example, is higher than that of iron, yet they often surpass iron in resistance).

Every corrosion process has several stages:

1. the supply of the corrosive medium or its individual components to the metal surface;
2. the interaction of the medium with the metal;
3. the complete or partial removal of the products from the metal surface (into the volume of liquid, if the medium is liquid).

In studying corrosion we followed the established views on the features of its development on copper and bronze objects. These features depend on the interactions of the cations and anions of the corrosive compounds present in the soil or atmosphere.

The morphology of the malachite corrosion product was studied on the surface of a Chinese bronze vessel of the Shang dynasty.

To understand which zones corrode and form patina, and why, one must consider certain technological characteristics of ancient bronzes.

It is well known that bronze alloys solidify over a range of temperatures: crystallisation begins at one temperature and ends at another. The crystals also differ in composition: those that solidify at the beginning of crystallisation are richer in the metal with the higher melting point, while those that solidify at the end are enriched in the more fusible metal.

Trace impurities in the raw material play the main role in smelting. If impurities are localised at grain boundaries, at certain particular points, it is precisely these places that may become centres of local corrosion damage.

## How does noble patina differ from ignoble patina?

Specialists of the State Hermitage distinguish features of corrosion designated as stable (**noble**) patinas. These patinas grow comparatively slowly. In the formation of a noble malachite patina, a red or black patina of oxides, sulphides and microparticles first arises. This is then gradually transformed into a carbonate compound, producing an enamel-like green layer. Because this transformation takes place very slowly, such a layer conveys all the nuances of the object's surface. Noble patina does not cover the details of the object or distort its form. In the atmosphere noble patina grows to about 80 µm, after which its growth rate decreases.

Noble patinas on bronzes usually have as their first layer (adjacent to the metal) copper oxide — cuprite. The overlying layers are most often basic copper carbonate (malachite, azurite), but hydrated copper sulphate (brochantite) also occurs; and in objects from sites of mass human burial, or from agricultural fields fertilised with phosphates over a long period, hydrated copper phosphates are often found too. In tin bronzes the patina also includes tin dioxide — cassiterite; and where the metal contains a large admixture of lead, certain lead oxides (massicot, occasionally plattnerite).

From the point of view of structural changes in bronzes, it is the solid solution of tin in copper that corrodes first; until all of the solid solution has corroded, the intermetallic phases are only slightly affected.

Unstable, rapidly developing ("wild") patinas as a rule include copper chlorides (atacamite, paratacamite) and bromides. The presence of the latter, incidentally, is a particularly good sign of authenticity, since forgers do not usually use bromine compounds for patination.

## Which mineral layers make up ancient patinas?

The surfaces of ancient bronzes are covered with a two-layer corrosion crust containing greenish-blue minerals, from beneath which reddish-brown spots of cuprite show through in places.

The patina contains many different minerals: quartz, gypsum, calcite, maghemite, butlerite and various other compounds. Sometimes alloys break down into their constituent elements — copper, silver, zinc — and these metals (in pure form) may be concentrated both inside the object and on its surface. In addition, the patina contains oxides and hydroxides, such as cuprite, lead oxide and zinc oxide. Carbonates are also present.

The enamel-like green layer of patina is a stable, slowly growing pseudomorph. The form of the object does not change; corrosion is distributed evenly over the surface without etching the grain boundaries of the alloy. The first layer of the patina (adjacent to the metal) is copper oxide — cuprite. The overlying layers are formed by copper carbonate (malachite and azurite), but hydrated copper sulphate (brochantite) also occurs.

Microscopic studies of corrosion products have shown that noble patinas consist of three layers, the ratio of whose thicknesses may vary from one patina to another.

- **The inner layer** is brownish-red (with an even surface) and firmly attached to the metal base. It consists of cuprous oxide (cuprite) and is the only natural protection of copper against oxidation.
- **The middle layer** consists of copper salts, simple and complex. It is greenish-blue and looser. It contains a surface altered by green copper compounds, with coarse pits and thin and thick lamellar crusts, and in places has a vitreous lustre.
- **The outer layer** consists of light-beige accretions with irregular overgrowths of varying thickness that crumble easily. The corrosion products of this layer include copper sulphides and sulphates (brochantite, bonattite), halides (atacamite, paratacamite) and phosphates (cornetite, hydrated copper phosphates, libethenite, pseudomalachite, sampleite).

## Which minerals are present in the patina of ancient bronzes?

A whole series of minerals forms on ancient bronzes.

**Cuprite** — cuprous oxide. Its colour varies: reddish-brown, violet, lead-grey, black or cherry, sometimes with an adamantine lustre. Cuprite is opaque, hard, brittle and insoluble in water. It characteristically forms dense, fine-grained masses, which may reach several millimetres in thickness. Cuprite often preserves important details of the original surface. It is frequently shot through with cracks or has cavities filled with copper chloride.

**Cassiterite** — tin dioxide. It does not form a layer of its own but lies within the mass of cuprite, forming small powdery accumulations in cracks, and may be mixed with copper salts. On bronzes with a high tin content cassiterite may form a fine-grained, smooth, continuous greenish-grey layer.

**Cerussite** — lead carbonate. It forms a greenish-grey interlayer beneath the outer layer of malachite. Lead is present in bronze as globules; if it corrodes, cuprite forms in its place.

**Malachite.** On the surface of a vessel it is found in both the upper and lower layers. The outer layer of corrosion products consists of basic salts of divalent copper. Accumulations of malachite are localised in the upper parts of the cementation zone and develop as earthy varieties and thin crusts. Under the microscope malachite shows a cryptocrystalline, finely columnar and finely fibrous structure. The fibres are grouped into growth cones, radiating bundles and sub-parallel aggregates. Its usual forms are compact or powdery aggregates, films, smears, botryoidal masses and spherocrystals. Minerals that have dissolved leave pores and voids in the patina, which malachite does not always fill completely, creating a curious spongy, botryoidal surface. The various shades of green of malachite depend on inclusions of other minerals — chrysocolla, asperolite, ehlite and so on. Chrysocolla, for example, gives malachite a bluish tinge.

## How does malachite form in the corrosion of ancient bronzes?

Most researchers regard malachite as a typical mineral of the corrosion zone of ancient copper and bronze objects.

In patina, malachite is deposited through the seepage of copper sulphate solutions and their reaction with carbonate particles of rock and with organic remains. Malachite is usually accompanied by cuprite, chrysocolla, azurite, copper phosphates and other oxidised copper minerals.

Azurite and malachite are often found together; their banded intergrowths are called azurmalachite. Azurite is less stable and in damp air gradually turns green, becoming malachite. It covers even old bronze objects found in archaeological excavations.

Malachite crystals on ancient patinas are usually very thin — from hundredths to tenths of a millimetre — and up to 10 mm long; only rarely, under favourable conditions, can huge multi-tonne flowstone masses form, consisting of a mass of seemingly stuck-together tiny crystals.

## How is patina imitated on bronze objects?

In diagnosing signs of antiquity, the main task is to detect artificial patinas.

Artificial patina is created with certain chemical substances. People learned to fake patina thousands of years ago. In the Renaissance patina was particularly popular: it was applied to monuments and other cultural objects to create an effect of antiquity. For millennia the technique of patination has been applied to copper and bronze objects to achieve particular effects.

To create an artificial patina, special substances — usually containing certain acids and oxidising agents — are applied to the objects. Such a patina may be transparent or matt; it helps to protect the metal from fading and corrosion.

Artificially induced green and blue patinas can be obtained as follows. A greenish-blue two-layer patina (the first layer being copper oxide) can be obtained with a copper nitrate solution of 200 g/l. The solution is applied to the object with a brush and left for 12–16 hours. After rinsing in cold water and drying, the surface is rubbed with a rag and fine pumice powder and then patinated three or four more times. Gradually a greenish-blue patina forms, with a continuous, slightly translucent, dense inner dark-brown layer of copper oxide.

## How can noble ancient patina be distinguished from modern, rapidly induced patina?

It was useful for the methodology of our research to become acquainted with scientific studies of patina whose mechanism of formation indicates the length of its history. The ancient ore consisted of secondary minerals of the oxidation zones of sulphide ores (mixtures of malachite, azurite, cerussite, smithsonite and oxides of antimony and bismuth). Accordingly, in diagnosing patina it is recommended not to carry out elemental analysis but to determine the composition of the minerals that make up the patina. The main diagnostic sign is the intensity, rate and evenness of the penetration of corrosion processes into the depth of the metal. Since corrosion is a rather lengthy process, the formation of natural patina is analogous to the natural processes of mineral formation in the oxidation zones of copper-sulphide deposits.

Unlike artificially induced patinas, which are characterised by uneven penetration along grain boundaries, ancient patinas form slowly, and corrosion on ancient bronze objects spreads evenly over the surface.

## Why have traces of osmium been found in the corrosion products of some ancient bronze vessels?

Osmium belongs to the platinum group of elements. It is highly refractory and heavy and forms natural alloys with iridium and platinum, and may contain other platinum-group elements.

In studying the structure of one vessel we noticed the heterogeneity of the bronze alloy and the presence in it of hard minerals containing rhodium and osmium. These resistant minerals have a very high melting point; consequently they entered the ore as a mechanical admixture of volatile oxide compounds of antimony and of rare resistant platinum-group minerals. The melting point of the ore was lowered by additions to the main carbonate copper ore (malachite, azurite and others).

Inclusions that did not dissolve in the melt, differing in colour and chemical composition, are indicators of the character of the ores from which the metal was smelted.

## What is the method of scientific study of ancient bronzes?

Stylistic analysis and the visual and microscopic study of corrosion products could not serve as a sufficient basis for the expert examination of Chinese bronze. To diagnose signs of antiquity it was necessary to develop a method based on modern techniques that would make it possible to distinguish an object from the range of modern repetitions (copies, forgeries).

The methodological recommendations developed may be useful in the attribution of bronze objects in museum and private collections that lack an unambiguous attribution of place and time of manufacture.

To work out a method of non-destructive diagnosis of the antiquity of copper alloys, we studied a bronze object presumably made in China in the Shang dynasty (made in the style typical of that period and with pronounced characteristics).

A preliminary scientific examination was carried out to attribute the bronze vessel. The object entered a private collection in 1910. Description: a wine vessel in the form of a deer with a lid (ritual wine container, Shang dynasty). The lid is decorated with a bird, and the whole surface is covered with characteristic relief ornament. The state of preservation is high, but the entire inner and outer surface is covered with a layer of corrosion of greenish, bluish, beige, red and black colours. Dimensions: height 35 cm, length 35 cm, width 15 cm.

The next task was to search for and compare this wine vessel with similar and related objects in well-known museum collections.

Since the object entered the private collection as a chance find without proper archaeological context, a similar Shang bronze vessel in the collection of the National Palace Museum in Taipei (Taiwan) served as the basis for our hypothesis and as key evidence for determining its age. On external examination the object was attributed as the earlier of the two.

The hypothesis was based on the foundations of typological study proposed by the archaeologist Augustus Pitt Rivers, which rest on the principle of gradual change: the more complex an object, the more strongly it expresses cultural development and progress; that is, a simpler construction implies greater antiquity.

Following the recommendations of the archaeologist Oscar Montelius on seeking similarities and differences in dating bronzes, the specialists of the Scientific Centre for Certification and Appraisal, in their preliminary scientific examination, assigned this vessel as presumably made in ancient China under the Shang-Yin state, which existed from 1600 to 1027 BC north of the point where the Yellow River emerges onto the Great Plain of China.

The preliminary scientific hypotheses rested on modern research into ancient Chinese metallurgy of the Shang dynasty.

Analysing the results of the latest scientific research in archaeology, materials characterisation and the experimental modelling of ancient metallurgical processes, we concluded that the signs of antiquity include the complex composition of the bronze alloy, its inhomogeneity, the features of corrosion, and unusual impurities.

It is well known that in studying ancient metal objects one must first determine the most suitable method of sampling, so as to damage the surface as little as possible and preserve the artefact. We also took into account that the object has an uneven alloy composition, so turnings and patina were taken from the vessel in different places.

Samples of the alloy, the patina and the breakdown products of the patina were collected by hand from the surface of the bronze vessel with a jeweller's scraper (a steel scraper). Since the surface of the bronze object was covered with corrosion, to obtain more precise results the patina was cleaned off, the metal was ground smooth, and turnings were taken from these cleaned spots — in four different places. The metal turnings removed were fixed on the microscope stage with carbon tape. Measurements were taken at several points (lid, base, rim of the vessel, projecting part of the decoration). For analysis of the elemental composition of the patina and its breakdown products, the samples were crushed.

Compositional analyses were carried out by electron-probe microanalysis with a wavelength-dispersive spectrometer (EPMA-WDS), and also with a **Phenom ProX** desktop scanning electron microscope, entered in the register of measuring instruments of the Russian Federation and capable of working with both conductive and non-conductive specimens.

In the study we took into account that incidental elements in the composition could significantly influence one another, which is reflected in the results of the study of the alloy composition. The turnings were taken from an area previously cleaned of traces of secondary alteration. The points for elemental analysis were chosen with regard to the visible inhomogeneity of the metal.

The compositional results of the study of the elemental composition of the metal alloy, the patina and the breakdown products of the patina of the lid and main body of the vessel were tabulated.

We followed the generally accepted criteria for classifying alloys, in which 2% is taken as the minimum content of an alloying element in a copper alloy (any element exceeding 2% by weight must be named in the designation of the bronze).

Several types of material were identified among the samples studied: six samples of unalloyed copper; nine of copper-tin alloy (Cu–Sn); three of a three-component copper-tin-lead alloy (Cu–Pb–Sn); and six of a two-component copper-lead alloy (Cu–Pb), the last showing an elevated lead content. The tin content did not exceed 4.2% by weight.

The quantities of copper, lead and tin found agree well with the information gathered in the historical review.

Antimony was found in 15 of the samples. Its content is small, ranging from 0.2 to 0.9%. Antimony is a rare alloying addition in bronzes. It is interesting to note that a copper + antimony alloy gives artefacts remarkable colours (such as golden and silvery-white). Vessels of such an alloy may have been important for ceremonial use. The presence of antimony in bronze is a good sign of authenticity. In several samples slight traces of silver, sulphur and silicon were found. A low iron content was found, typical of early and primitive smelting. The rust-coloured spots are explained by the content of iron and arsenopyrite (arsenic). The presence of oxygen in the alloy can be explained by corrosion processes. New information worthy of attention is the discovery in the alloy of traces of the rare elements tellurium and osmium.

## Conclusions of the scientific study of the signs of antiquity of Chinese bronzes

Elemental analysis revealed the complex composition of the alloy from which this bronze vessel was made. The studies confirmed that the object was made with an imperfect technology, so that the alloy is inhomogeneous, multi-component and contains mechanical impurities.

An uneven distribution of copper, lead, tin and antimony is characteristic. The mechanical (unmelted, disseminated) impurities are small grains of minerals of rare elements belonging to the platinum-group metals: platinum, osmium and rhodium. Such a complex composition is a good sign of antiquity.

Thus the signs of antiquity of this vessel are:

- the complex composition of the alloy and its inhomogeneity;
- the local inhomogeneity of the patina in the content of its components;
- the features of corrosion, the composition of the patina and its inhomogeneity;
- the presence of secondary minerals in the patina: cuprite, malachite, azurite and brochantite;
- the presence of areas with an elevated concentration of impurities of rare elements.

Our data show that in the Shang era there were other ore deposits with their own sets of indicator elements. At the same time we consider it possible that bronze was smelted not from primary refractory sulphide ores but from easily fusible minerals of the weathering crust. Here mixtures of carbonates of copper, lead, zinc and antimony, enriched in ore metals, accumulate with an admixture of arsenates of the same metals and a mechanical admixture of platinum-group metals in the fine fraction. Osmium is also known to occur in compounds with sulphur and arsenic: erlichmanite, osmian laurite and osarsite. These minerals are called sulpharsenides. The osmium content of ores as a rule does not exceed 1 × 10⁻⁵. All these minerals accumulate in oxidation zones, are easy to extract and are easily fusible, the metals smelting out at 700–800 °C.

## What other studies can be used to date ancient alloys?

We plan to study the lead isotope composition of our vessel in the near future. If we can establish the isotopic composition of samples of various ores from particular mines and then compare them with samples from our bronze vessel, the source of the metal for our object can be identified precisely.
