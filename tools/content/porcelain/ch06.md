# Symbols in Chinese Porcelain

Most objects of Chinese decorative and applied art carry symbolic meaning. Since the most ancient times symbolism has permeated every sphere of life in China. A so-called "cosmogonic" symbolism took shape, through which the Chinese sought to understand the structure of the universe and the place of man within it. By the middle of the first millennium BC a symbolism of numbers, colours and images had formed.

The decoration of porcelain often serves two purposes: simple decoration of the surface, and encoded auspicious symbols signifying good fortune or luck. It is precisely the combination of symbols of every size in happy proportions that gives Chinese art its inimitable flavour.

When the symbolic content is read, a whole new world opens up to the researcher and the collector, and the objects begin to speak to them. Many symbols can carry several meanings.

When analysing the symbolic content of a porcelain object, one should ask:

- What is the most important thing the master wanted to tell us?
- Which symbols did he encode in the porcelain object?

The most common symbols of Chinese porcelain are listed below.

## Auspicious objects and wedding symbols

**Ruyi** — a ritual sceptre with a curved horizontal handle and a head in the form of a stylised *lingzhi* fungus (a symbol of longevity). In the generally accepted translation *ruyi* means "as you wish, so shall you receive". The *ruyi* sceptre was part of the set of objects for the scholar's study.

**Wedding vases** — large paired vases with characteristic underglaze cobalt painting and the *shuangxi* sign. They came into use at the beginning of the nineteenth century.

**Shuangxi** ("double happiness") — a stylised doubled character *xi* ("joy"). A symbol of conjugal happiness, depicted on objects intended as wedding gifts.

## Mythical creatures

**Dragon** — the most important of the four celestial creatures of Chinese mythology, together with the phoenix, the *qilin* and the tortoise. Dwelling within a house, the dragon brings money into it.

**Flaming pearl.** The dragon is often shown with a magic pearl — a symbol of spiritual and material wealth and of the inseparability of matter and spirit. The flaming pearl is an element of auspicious ornament, depicted in the composition "two dragons playing with a flaming pearl".

**Phoenix.** The Chinese phoenix was a sacred bird of Chinese mythology. It has the head of a pheasant, the beak of a swallow, a long neck, multicoloured legs and the tail of a peacock. It is the messenger of the Daoist immortals, the king of all birds and a symbol of good fortune. It symbolises the sun, fertility, a rich harvest, luck and longevity.

**Dragon and phoenix** — a motif with an auspicious connotation. Vessels decorated with these two most important mythical creatures of the Chinese tradition were, as a rule, made as wedding gifts.

**Qilin** ("unicorn") — a hornless dragon. The *qilin* has a single horn on its forehead, a yellow belly, multicoloured flanks, the hooves of a horse, the body of a deer and the tail of an ox. It was often depicted with a feline head and paws and a forked tail. It symbolises benevolence and honesty, and is a good omen.

**Waves** — the abode of dragons.

**Carp** — a symbol of the noble scholar and of prosperity. Known compositions include "a carp leaping up a waterfall" — a wish to overcome the difficulties of the competitive examinations — and "a boy with a carp" — a wish for "scholarly sons".

**The fish with a dragon's tail** represents the transformation of the carp into a dragon as it passes the waterfall — a symbol of passing the examinations. The carp becomes a dragon when it overcomes the rapids of turbulent rivers. According to an ancient Chinese legend, a carp that manages to leap over a particularly steep river rapid, the "Dragon Gate", turns into a dragon. In Chinese mythology the Dragon Gate lies at the top of a waterfall running down from a legendary mountain. Many carp swim against the strong current, but only a few are capable of the final leap over the falls. Traditional Chinese paintings show a carp arched in mid-leap, its head already transformed into that of a dragon. It has therefore become a symbol of perseverance.

**Coral** — a wish for high office. Coral is one of the "eight ancient treasures". Vase handles were often made in the form of a coral branch and coloured in iron red to imitate it.

## The Twelve Ornaments

The twelve ornaments, or twelve symbols of sovereignty, were in 1759 reserved exclusively for the robes of the emperor. They are:

1. **Sun** (red) — the male principle; imperial sovereignty, brilliance.
2. **Moon** (white) — the elixir of immortality.
3. **Constellation of three stars** — an inexhaustible source of forgiveness and love.
4. **Mountains** — symbols of stability; they represent places of worship.
5. **Dragons** — mythological creatures symbolising strength.
6. **Pheasant** — embodies beauty and good fortune.
7. ***Fu*** — a pattern symbolising good and evil, the imperial power over life and death, and the law.
8. **Axe** — justice, authority, the right to punish.
9. **Two cups together** — respect for one's parents.
10. **Water plants** — purity.
11. **Fire** — intellectual brilliance, a burning spirit.
12. **Rice or millet** — the earth and the country's ability to feed its people; a symbol of prosperity and fertility.

## Deities, treasures and the scholar's attributes

**God of Longevity.** Usually depicted smiling and friendly, sometimes carrying a vessel filled with the elixir of life. He has a high, domed forehead and holds a peach, the symbol of immortality.

**The Hundred Treasures** may be depicted in various combinations. They include:

- books — symbols of knowledge and one of the four accomplishments of the scholar;
- brushes — one of the four accomplishments of the scholar;
- chess — a symbol of knowledge;
- coral — longevity, advancement in office;
- flowers — beauty and harmony;
- hand drum — movement and ritual music;
- incense burner — veneration of ancestors;
- lute — family happiness, the suppression of lust;
- money — wealth;
- palm leaf — self-education;
- mouth organ (*sheng*) — a symbol of the phoenix;
- rhinoceros horn — happiness;
- sceptre — a symbol of the Buddha, magical powers, prosperity;
- scrolls — calligraphy, painting, truth;
- seal — strength and authority;
- ink stone — an attribute of the scholar;
- swords — wisdom, penetrating understanding, victory over evil, superhuman strength;
- teapot — tranquillity;
- umbrella — respect, purity, dignity and high rank;
- vase — a symbol of peace; a wide variety of plants and flowers in a vase signifies a broad field of symbols;
- wine cup — rank and the veneration of ancestors;
- wine vessel — a warning not to drink to excess.

**The Four Accomplishments of the Scholar.** No educated person in traditional China could do without the four writing implements — brush, paper, ink and ink stone — which were called the "four treasures of the scholar's study". In imperial China a well-educated scholar was expected to be skilled in four arts: chess, calligraphy, painting and music. Symbols of these skills often appear in decorative art as chess tables, brushes, silk scrolls and Chinese zithers.

**"Chess players"** — two hermits at a chessboard against a landscape, a favourite subject of works intended for the scholar's study.

## Buddhist symbols

**Prayer wheel** — a cylinder or roller on an axle containing mantras. In Tibetan Buddhism prayer wheels are turned in order to unite physical activity with spiritual content.

**Buddhist rosary** — a thin string of beads and elongated elements of equal size, belonging to Lamaism (Tibetan Buddhism). It is a cult object, an instrument for counting mantras recited, rituals performed and prostrations. In Buddhism, however, the rosary also serves as an object in which information connected with the fundamental philosophical and practical aspects of the Buddha's teaching is codified. Rosaries have been known since the third century.

**Vajra.** In Buddhism the *vajra* takes the form of a bundle of thunderbolts bound at the middle, with curved ends (single, double, triple and cruciform variants are known). From Sanskrit the word means both "thunderbolt" and "diamond". The *vajra* is a club or sceptre and symbolises firmness and indestructibility. It can be interpreted as one of the images of the world axis, fixing the influence of the upper world upon the lower.

**Taotie (ogre mask)** — the sacrificial animal; a warning against avarice, gluttony, sensuality and sloth. A stylised zoomorphic mask with ears, horns and large fangs but without a lower jaw. It appears on bronze vessels and in the ornament of ritual utensils of the Shang–Yin and Western Zhou periods.

**Swastika (*wan*)** — an ancient solar symbol embodying the cycle of the universe. On the one hand it relates to the religious and philosophical categories of Buddhism (the idea of infinity, the concept of truth); on the other it is perceived as a symbol of the Buddha's teaching.

**Arhat** — a saint of the Buddhist pantheon, a disciple and follower of the Buddha; one worthy of veneration, free from desire; the ideal of Southern Buddhism. In Buddhism the arhat is one who has attained a true understanding of the nature of the Transcendent and is ready to complete his life in this world after its final perfection. For this it is essential to be freed from the five "fetters": the desire for existence in form, the desire for existence beyond form, spiritual pride, self-love, and the last manifestations of ignorance and error.

## Plants and flowers

**The Three Friends of Winter** — pine, bamboo and plum, symbolising long life. Since all three can endure cold and adverse conditions, they were often depicted together. They symbolise longevity, perseverance and the wholeness of being and of the virtues of the ideal scholar, as well as the three principal religions of China — Buddhism, Daoism and Confucianism.

**Basket of flowers.** The contents of the basket represent riches.

**Lotus** — a symbol of incorruptible honesty and purity, and of the perfect man who, like the lotus, grows out of the mud without being sullied by it.

**Peach** — symbolises immortality and long life. The God of Longevity often sits upon a peach. Vases with nine peaches were a popular motif in the Qianlong reign.

**Peony** — an emblem of wealth. It is considered the king of flowers, symbolising spring, and a harbinger of good fortune.

**Bamboo** — signifies longevity. Never losing its leaves, bamboo is a symbol of old age, endurance and modesty. Bamboo and plum together represent husband and wife.

**Fungus (the sacred *lingzhi*)** — symbolises immortality and longevity. This strange plant looks like a tree with a gnarled trunk and has flat, three-lobed leaves. In Daoist mysticism it is regarded as the food of the immortals.

**Gourd** — symbolises mysticism and the science of magic; a talisman to drive away evil spirits.

**Pear** — purity, justice, wisdom and benevolence.

**Pine** — a symbol of steadfastness and longevity, and the favourite tree of Chinese painters. Pines grow taller than other trees and embody composure and constancy. Daoism values the pine's twisted trunk and admires the structure of its bark, which recalls the movement of vital forces. It is also a symbol of friends who must remain loyal in misfortune.

**Pomegranate** — has many seeds and symbolises the birth of many children. A **burst pomegranate** is a wish for numerous offspring.

**Willow** — symbolises spring and feminine beauty. In ancient times dwellings were sprinkled with willow twigs against evil spirits.

**Wild plum (prunus)** — a symbol of the New Year and the welcoming of spring. A plum branch was placed in a vase at New Year, symbolising the idea of harmony in the universe.

**Narcissus** — a symbol of the New Year. Narcissus bulbs are forced specially for the New Year in shallow vessels filled with water and large pebbles. It expresses a wish for longevity.

**Ten thousand flowers.** A composition depicting a great variety of flowers, called *millefleurs* ("a thousand flowers") in Europe. It is a pictorial rebus whose content is revealed through traditional symbolism: "all the flowers express their good wishes". The peony is read as a symbol of power and prosperity, the lotus as longevity, the lily as numerous male offspring, and so on.

**Chrysanthemum** — a symbol of autumn and at the same time a flower of joy and merriment. This meaning is reinforced when a butterfly (one of whose meanings is joy) is depicted beside it. "Chrysanthemums by a fence" is a subject connected with the poet Tao Yuanming, who gave up all his court posts, lived in a modest hut and grew chrysanthemums. He said that rather than work for the government he preferred to grow chrysanthemums, entertain his friends and drink. The flower thus became a symbol of an easy life in retirement.

**Banana (plantain).** Banana leaves symbolise self-discipline, one of the four precious qualities of the scholar.

**Orchid.** An orchid beside a rock alludes to the idea that the orchid is modest, yet its fragrance spreads everywhere.

## Animals, birds and insects

**Playing children** — a wish for male offspring.

**Fu, Lu, Shou (the three)** — designations of the three attributes of a good life: happiness, career (official emolument) and longevity.

**Butterfly** — a symbol of conjugal happiness, joy and summer.

**Lion** — in Buddhism, the guardian of temples and other sacred palaces; a symbol of valour, high social position and the material well-being of the family.

**Magpie** — a bird of joy and a good omen; it brings happiness. The magpie is the patron of the Qing dynasty, owing to a legend about a magpie that built its nest in front of the entrance to a cave where one of the founders of the dynasty was hiding from his pursuers.

**Horse** — a symbol of perseverance and speed. The legendary ability of the horse is that it can cover an enormous distance in one leap. A symbol of good fortune in battle.

**Mandarin duck** — an emblem of joy. A pair of mandarin ducks is a symbol of fidelity and harmony, since they stay together all their lives. They are often shown with lotus, usually swimming in a pond.

**Peacock** — symbolises dignity and beauty.

**Ox** — the animal that pulls the plough. It symbolises spring, since work on the land begins in spring.

**Three-legged toad**, which swallows the moon during an eclipse — a symbol of money.

**Deer (*lu*)** — a Daoist emblem of longevity. The deer is said to be the only animal able to find the sacred *lingzhi* fungi of immortality. It is also a symbol of wealth and longevity.

**Tortoise** — longevity, strength, endurance.

**Fish** — wealth. Fish depicted beside lotus flowers mean "may you live in abundance year after year". The goldfish signifies gold and abundance, which is why many Chinese keep goldfish in an aquarium or in a pond in their own garden. The golden carp is a wish for profit or advantage in business.

**Bat (*fu*)** — a symbol of longevity, prosperity and happiness, and one of the best symbols of good fortune. The Chinese word for "bat" sounds identical to the word for "happiness". In images the bat is often coloured red, the colour of joy.

**Five bats** — the "five blessings": long life, health, wealth, love and a natural death. A motif built of a great number of bats signifies endless happiness. A bat with a peach means "happiness and longevity"; a bat with a lotus, continuous happiness.

**Cicadas** — a symbol of immortality, resurrection, happiness and eternal youth, and of the curbing of greed and vice.

**Cockerel** — frightens away evil spirits. A red cockerel protects the house from fire; a white cockerel keeps demons in fear.

**Eagle** — a symbol of strength. A picture of an eagle perched in a pine forest is a good gift for an elderly person, wishing him the strength of an eagle and longevity. An eagle on a rock in the sea symbolises a hero who fights alone.

**Heron.** A picture of a heron with a lotus means "may your path always lead upward".

**Bird — rock — peony** — an auspicious rebus: "honour, prosperity, steadfastness".

**Flowers and birds** — the reflection of the beauty and harmony of the universe in images of beautiful flowers.

## Landscape and geometric motifs

**Meander ("cloud and thunder")** — a repeating geometric ornament symbolising life-giving rain and an abundance of the fruits of the earth for an agricultural people.

**Mountains** — the link between Earth and Heaven.

**The Five Sacred Peaks** lie along the five directions — north, south, east, west and centre — so as to connect Heaven and Earth. To communicate with the deities, emperors ordered important Daoist temples to be built on each peak. These five mountains represented all existing mountains and their earthly powers. They were regarded as a means of securing water, which was important for an agrarian society such as China. When a new dynasty was founded, the emperor had to visit the mountains to receive the heavenly mandate to rule the whole world.

**Rock garden.** Natural stones serve as the meditative centre of the Chinese garden and symbolise the circulation of the forces of the universe. They are also a symbol of longevity, firmness and durability.

## The Eight Immortals

**Ba Xian** ("the Eight Immortals") — the most popular group of heroes in Chinese Daoist mythology. They lived at different times and possessed various miraculous abilities (for example, the ability to walk on water). Each of the Ba Xian personifies one of the conditions of human existence: male, female, old age, youth, poverty, wealth, nobility and humbleness. Each of the immortals lived on his own, but sometimes they gathered together. Once they were invited by Xi Wangmu, the Queen Mother of the West. Having feasted to their hearts' content at her palace, they set off to visit the Lord of the East. The Eastern Sea lay on their way, but even the watery abyss held no danger for the immortals. Each set off across it on whatever he possessed — one on castanets, one on an iron crutch, one on a basket, one on a donkey — and all of these proved unsinkable.

**The Eight Treasures (*ba bao*)** — the attributes of the Eight Immortals:

1. **Double gourd** — contains all the magic medicines; symbolises the physician.
2. **Lotus** — represents the wise woman.
3. **Sword** — patron of scholars; rids the world of evil.
4. **Castanets** — patron of the theatre.
5. **Fan** — revives the souls of the dead and symbolises delicacy of feeling.
6. **Bamboo drum and sticks** — a symbol of longevity.
7. **Flute** — patron of musicians; music can make flowers grow.
8. **Flower baskets** — a symbol of deceptive pleasure.

## The Eight Buddhist Emblems

1. **Chakra, or flaming wheel.** The wheel of the law crushes all delusions and superstitions. It is also called the wheel of life, or the wheel of truth.
2. **Conch shell** — a symbol of royalty, dignity and high rank.
3. **Umbrella** — a symbol of spiritual authority and charity.
4. **Canopy or bell** — signifies respect, veneration and martial ardour; its sound drives away evil spirits.
5. **Lotus** — a symbol of fidelity, truth, fertility and purity, since its flowers emerge clean and beautiful from muddy water.
6. **Vase** — symbolises eternal harmony and the supreme intellectual victory over birth and death.
7. **Pair of fish** — a symbol of marriage, conjugal bliss, fertility and perseverance; an amulet against evil.
8. **Endless knot** — a symbol of abundance, longevity, infinity and eternity.

Any of these symbols is often combined with the lotus in forming a design. As a decorative motif, the eight Buddhist emblems first appeared on ceramics during the Yuan dynasty. They came from Tibetan Lamaist art, and their decorative elements were popular in the Ming and Qing dynasties.
