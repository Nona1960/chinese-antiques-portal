# Appraisal of Chinese Antique Porcelain

## The algorithm for appraising and diagnosing an antique porcelain object

The procedure is as follows:

1. Examination of the object.
2. Determination of the body material (porcelain, earthenware, stoneware).
3. Diagnosis of the period of manufacture.
4. Study of the set of marks and seals.
5. Study of stylistic features.
6. Study of the features of the technology.
7. Calculation of the base value.
8. Determination of the upward coefficients.
9. Assessment of wear.
10. Study of the market.
11. Determination of the market value of the object.

The valuation of antiques has its own particularities. When ancient objects are appraised, the assessment must begin with their appearance; then the quality of manufacture is evaluated, and the technologies used and the features of the decoration are analysed.

A considerable part of early ceramics consists of Tang-dynasty wares in the *sancai* (three-colour) technique, which can be valuable in their own right. The monetary value of such objects is rather debatable and depends chiefly on a collector's wish to acquire the piece and pay the price asked. The value of ancient objects of the Northern Dynasties (550–577) and of the Tang dynasty (618–907) is set by the current market, which consists of private collectors and museums. Prices may fluctuate widely as a result of a number of factors: the rarity of the item relative to other known items of the same kind; the length of time it has been on the market; the materials used in its manufacture; and the quality of the work.

One particularity must be borne in mind: Asian collectors look for objects of the later dynasties (Song, Ming, Qing), whereas Western collectors in recent years have also become interested in ancient objects and pieces from burials.

To determine the value of an ancient or antique object, the appraiser needs a serious knowledge of the culture and social customs of the relevant period.

## Period, place and mark

The basis of the appraisal of antique Chinese porcelain is the determination of the time and place of manufacture. If the object bears a set of marks, these are identified with the help of specialised reference books. In establishing age, the mark is the most important characteristic. On forgeries the marks often fail to match the genuine ones, because forgers paid no attention to them or simply lacked the necessary information. If there are no marks or inscriptions, diagnosis proceeds through the study of stylistic features.

Every porcelain object must be studied carefully from both the stylistic and the technical point of view. It has happened that an object bore no signs or marks at all, although it had been copied with complete precision. In such a case the object should be subjected to a thorough stylistic study. Style and form must correspond to the decoration. Contradictions are frequently found between the form of a vessel and its ornament; in figural decoration, inconsistencies in subject matter, in the dress of the figures, in the landscape and in the style of the scenes. The bases and handles of vessels also differed from period to period.

Let us examine these distinctive features using the example of the flattened handles with which vases were decorated from the mid-eighteenth century. The handles take the form of simplified, schematic dragon bodies, forming a distinctive curl at the bottom. Such fanciful handles are an important stylistic feature of vessels from the second half of the Qianlong reign.

If you have an object with handles and applied (sprigged) decoration, ask yourself: do they correspond to the stated period?

## Method of manufacture

Next, the method by which the object was made should be checked.

The methods used to manufacture modern mass-produced wares differ from the old technologies. Modern porcelain is made by pouring liquid porcelain slip into absorbent plaster moulds. The slip is left in the mould for several minutes until a layer has set against the walls. The excess, still-liquid clay is then poured out and the piece is left to dry. The hardened piece is removed from the mould and dried in the air. This method makes it easy to produce porcelain with a thin body.

Antique porcelain, like other ceramics, was made on the potter's wheel or, if the object was square or of complex shape, with the help of slabs shaped against wooden boards. The still-damp pieces were joined together with a small amount of clay of creamy consistency. Most tall, rounded antique Chinese porcelain vessels were, as a rule, assembled from two or three horizontal sections joined together to give the final form.

The final form was achieved by setting the piece on the wheel and trimming it with turning tools. The handles and neck of a vase were made separately, and the handles were attached afterwards. An important diagnostic sign of age is the presence of **slip joins** — traces of the luting together of two or more sections of the unfired ceramic body with liquid clay (slip). In genuine antique porcelain the joins of the vessel can be seen. Such a join is visible as a seam and can be felt especially well on the inside. It usually occurs on the shoulders of tall vases and at the widest part of the belly of a flask.

## Firing defects

The next step is the search for firing defects. Historically, the greatest problem for potters was finding the correct firing temperature. If the temperature is too high, the porcelain melts and deforms. To prevent deformation, kaolin was added to the clay. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, for economic reasons, the kaolin content of export wares and wares made for ordinary people was low, and deformed shapes are therefore often found.

On wares of the Transitional period (seventeenth century), the following defects often formed during firing: chips, pimples, bubbles, kiln grit, and a reddish or yellowish tint. The study of such defects helps to diagnose a porcelain object.

## Enamels

Not all antique porcelain objects have the same artistic value. Learn to tell the good from the beautiful, and the beautiful from the exceptional. The only way to acquire such experience is long study of the style concerned and the examination of as many enamel-decorated porcelain objects as possible.

One must understand the technology of applying enamels to a porcelain form. Enamel is a fused mixture of lead, tin and various oxides with borax, glass powder and quartz. The key component of vitreous enamel is a very friable form of glass called **frit**. In their natural state enamel pigments are a dry powder, usually mixed with oil, glue or ordinary water. In painting, the colours are applied with soft, sweeping brushstrokes. If a porcelain object is enamelled, check whether the enamel is in good condition, whether there are chips, and whether any areas of enamel have been replaced with lacquer. Check how cleanly and clearly the design is executed and whether the work is neat. Pay attention to the outlines of the design. Bear in mind that temporary outlines may have been used on wares to guide the decorators; these burn away in firing.

In China a number of glazes and enamels came into use only in the first third of the eighteenth century — for example, a chocolate-brown enamel with a lustrous sheen, which would later frame the reserves on wares with compositions in the *famille rose* palette. At the same time, pale enamels of rare shades became widespread: pistachio green, olive, ivory and pinkish. Accordingly, if the painting contains white, pink, violet or violet-blue enamels while the mark refers to a Ming reign, the piece is clearly an imitation, since these enamels appeared only at the turn of the eighteenth century.

At the same period, free painting in cobalt began to be combined with monochrome glazes (a technological achievement made possible by double firing). Glazes the colour of burnt sugar were incorporated into the decoration as bands emphasising the form of the vessel. Wares on which individual details are picked out in such a glaze are common. In Europe this glaze was known by various names: "Batavian brown", "cappuccino", "dead leaf". Wares decorated in this way became highly fashionable in Holland, where they were promoted by the French-born designer Daniel Marot (1661–1752); the term "Marot style" is therefore also applied to this colour scheme.

Wares in the **cloisonné style** (French *cloison*, "partition") form a separate group. This style of decorating ceramics or porcelain resembles cloisonné enamel on metal, in which raised ridges outline the decorated zones. Thin threads of clay were laid along the design, and the hollows they formed were filled with coloured glazes — violet, yellow, blue and turquoise. This style of decoration arose in the Yongzheng reign.

## Gilding

When appraising antique porcelain with gilding, appraisers must understand and distinguish the type of gilding. The oldest technique is called "hard gilding" or "fire gilding". Pure gold is mixed with liquid mercury, applied to the object and fired. On heating the mercury evaporates, and the gold remains on the surface as a matt coating that must then be burnished to give it brilliance and a bright finish. According to the letters of the Jesuit missionary in Jingdezhen of 1712 and 1722, gilding on Chinese porcelain was made by grinding gold leaf into powder and then mixing the powder with ordinary colourless enamel. Gilding on wares made before the middle of the eighteenth century therefore lacks a bright lustre.

Another gilding method was introduced in the West in the 1850s. This is "liquid gold", in which powdered gold is used in the form of a liquid suspension applied to the porcelain. When the piece is taken from the kiln the gold is already bright and needs no burnishing. The drawback of this method is that bright gold tends to rub off very easily.

## Crackle

A further diagnostic feature is the presence of characteristic **crackle** (French *craquelé*, "cracked"). Crackle is a pattern of fine cracks on the surface, created for decorative effect. The cracks may appear during cooling after the glaze firing, or after the muffle (enamel) firing, or a year later, or a hundred years later. Cracks in the glaze of a ceramic (porcelain) object arise from the difference between the coefficients of linear thermal expansion of the body and of the glaze covering it: they form when the "jacket" of the fired glaze layer is "too small" for the body. Special crackle glazes form cracks only in the surface of the glaze layer, without reaching the body. They cover many monochrome wares of the Song dynasty. The effect was enhanced by rubbing tea-coloured, red or black pigments into the cracks. Traditionally, Chinese connoisseurs distinguished large, bold cracks filled with black pigment, called "crab's claw", from a network of much finer cracks called "fish roe".

## Celadons

Wares known as **celadon** arouse great interest among collectors. This is a specific group of Chinese wares made of a refractory, porcellaneous ceramic body and decorated with feldspathic glazes coloured green or grey-green by iron oxide. They were produced in the workshops of Yaozhou in the north of the country and in the workshops of Longquan in the southern province of Zhejiang from the beginning of the Northern Song dynasty (960–1127) until the first half of the seventeenth century.

Celadons ranged in shade from pale green to dark grey. Celadon glazes belong to the family of transparent glazes with cracks that are often pronounced (and sometimes deliberately emphasised).

Celadon is the term for ceramics glazed in the green colour of jade in its various shades and tones, also known as *greenware*. It is characterised by simple, elegant forms, a "jade-like" glaze, high practical value and an easily recognisable style. Celadons can be obtained in various colours, including white, grey, blue and yellow, depending on several factors: (1) the thickness of the glaze layer applied; (2) the type of clay to which it is applied; (3) the exact composition of the glaze.

The official (imperial) kilns produced *guan* wares, distinguished by a more bluish tint of glaze, for which zinc was added to it. For many centuries, celadon wares of a sea-green colour were the most highly valued at the court of the Chinese emperor.

Imitations and forgeries of celadons are perhaps the largest and best group of all forgeries in terms of quality. They bear a very close resemblance to genuine pieces and have always been very popular. Already in the Guangxu period (1875–1908), Japanese dealers permanently based in old Beijing were collecting celadons for collectors in Japan.

## Yixing teapots

Collectors are increasingly interested in ceramic **Yixing teapots**, distinguished by simplicity and exceptional craftsmanship. In Yixing county (Jiangsu province), high-fired ceramics with a heavy, water-tight body were produced. They were not glazed but burnished and enriched with modelled elements. The Yixing kilns became particularly famous for their heat-resistant teapots, which from the late sixteenth century took the forms of fruits, flowers, tortoises and dragons. Their shades were given poetic names such as "cinnabar colour", "pomegranate skin", "pear skin" and so on. These teapots were highly prized by tea lovers and were used for brewing at "intellectual" tea gatherings, as well as water droppers for ink. In the Ming and Qing dynasties Yixing wares were signed by their potters.

A good Yixing teapot must have a very tight-fitting lid which, even though it has a hole, lets no water through when you place your finger over the hole.

## Temmoku blackwares

A separate collecting field is formed by **temmoku blackwares** — dark tea wares (bowls and cups) with a characteristic glaze pattern. They were produced in kilns in Fujian province and were at the height of their popularity in the Song dynasty. The wares were made of local iron-rich clay fired in an oxidising atmosphere at temperatures from 1300 °C. The glaze was made with wood ash (ash wood). At high temperatures, as a result of random phase-separation effects at the start of cooling, the glazes transform and create inimitable patterns.

In the Song period, complex glazes with unexpected, illusory effects were preferred to smooth ones: as the object is turned at different angles they appear now bluish, now golden-brown. Streaks in the form of fine stripes ("hare's fur"), a scattering of shimmering spots ("heavenly eye") or spreading "oil spots" are often seen on these wares.

Such tea wares are characterised by a thickening and pooling of glaze near the foot and by a dark-brown base. The thick body was hidden under the glaze, but it sometimes deliberately showed through where the glaze had been applied with studied carelessness, creating a contrast that emphasised the effect of incompleteness. Some of these old original tea bowls have survived in Japan, where they have been passed down from generation to generation and are preserved as national treasures.

The following characteristics help in looking for clear signs of age in temmoku and in detecting forgeries — though even they are not always a guarantee of authenticity:

1. **Sound when tapped.** If the tone is high, it is a forgery.
2. **Crackle.** Old glaze has fine cracks visible under a loupe. If there are none, the piece is most likely a forgery.
3. **Golden dust.** The presence of any sparkling golden dust under a 10× loupe suggests that the object is most likely genuine.
4. **Foot ring.** The foot ring should feel smooth when you turn it gently in your palm. A sharp foot is a bad sign.
5. **Glaze.** A thick, running layer of glaze is a sign of authenticity.

## Knowing the market

A specialist in Chinese antiques must know both regional and international markets. Price guides to antiques should be used only as guides, not as indicators of exact prices. Major auction houses such as Sotheby's and Christie's include Chinese porcelain in their specialised catalogues. Searching for comparable items in these catalogues is a good way to begin establishing current prices, but it must be borne in mind that auction houses publish illustrated catalogues only with realised prices.

Antique lovers living in Russia can be advised to visit regularly the various antique fairs held in Moscow and St Petersburg. The most information about current prices can be gathered at the antique fairs held regularly at the Central House of Artists on Krymsky Val in Moscow.

## Upward coefficients

After the base value has been determined on the basis of an analysis of analogues, it is recommended to apply upward coefficients and surcharges (as percentages) to the base value of the antique porcelain object. They are made up of the following factors:

| Factor | Surcharge |
|---|---|
| Presence of a set of marks | 15% |
| Presence of a legend | 55% |
| Documented history of the object (provenance) | 110% |
| Stylistic features | 10–15% |
| Features revealing the hand of a particular master | 15% |
| Originality of conception | 10% |
| Level of execution | 25% |
| Complexity of modelling | 30% |
| Technological innovation | 50–100% |
| Completeness of the object | 5% |
| Typicality (characteristic of its period) | 15% |
| Rarity (for its period) | 20% |
| Uniqueness | 50% |
| Rarity for museum collections | 15% |
| Increased antiques demand | 15–100% |
| Museum storage | 5–10% |

Upward coefficients applied to the base value according to the period of manufacture:

| Period | Coefficient |
|---|---|
| 20th century | 1.0 |
| 19th century | 1.0–1.5 |
| 18th century | 2.0–3.0 |
| 17th century | 3.5–5.0 |
| 16th century | 6.0–10.0 |
| Before the 15th century | 50.0–100 |
