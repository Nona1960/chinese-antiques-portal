# Attribution of Wares Painted in Underglaze Cobalt Blue

A separate category of antique Chinese porcelain consists of wares with underglaze painting in cobalt, copper or iron pigment (metallic oxides of iron, cobalt and copper). The colours are applied to the ceramic object before it is covered with glaze.

## The sources of cobalt, period by period

Recent research shows that the features of the pigment's colour and of the application of cobalt painting can help to determine the sources of the cobalt and the period of manufacture. The website *Gotheborg — Antique Chinese Porcelain* gives a brief classification of cobalt:

**1. Tang dynasty.** Cobalt with an increased content of copper and iron and a low content of manganese; a saturated, dark-blue colour. Several sources are supposed: Persia (Iran, Afghanistan), India, Egypt, western Asia Minor and deposits along the shores of the Mediterranean.

**2. Song dynasty.** Cobalt with an increased content of manganese and a low content of iron. After firing it acquires a greyish-blue colour with black specks. Its sources lay mainly in a province in the east of China.

**3. Yuan dynasty.** The cobalt differs greatly between the early and the late period. Imported cobalt from the Middle East was used to paint large wares. It is distinguished by a high lead content and a low manganese content. Correct firing gives a bright blue, sometimes with evident spots of iron ("rust"). A blue with a violet tinge is occasionally found.

**4. Ming dynasty.** Because of civil war, the import of cobalt stopped and the quality of underglaze blue painting became very low. Only local cobalt was used in porcelain production. Folk wares with painting show grey and dark shades. In the later period the cobalt painting became brighter. The blue painting, however, sometimes shows black-brown rust spots, called **"heaping and piling"**: the glaze surface shows dark, violet-black spots caused by a high concentration of pigment or by impurities in the cobalt. When the potters went over to domestic raw material, which was unrefined and contained many impurities of iron and manganese oxides, the design acquired a greyish bloom or numerous black dots. The effect is characteristic of porcelain painting of the Yongle period (1403–1424).

The multi-layered cobalt painting of the Chenghua (1465–1487) and Jiajing (1522–1566) reigns is considered the finest, when imported "Mohammedan" blue, brought from Iraq, was used.

At the beginning of the Wanli reign the official kilns went over to a blue cobalt with a violet sheen, while private kilns used mainly a local bluish-grey or blackish-grey cobalt.

At the end of the Wanli reign and in the Transitional period (to the end of the Ming), the official works used a light, clear blue cobalt with shades of grey. The folk kilns used many sources of cobalt; a bright violet is rarely found.

For lack of funds to buy cobalt pigment abroad, the potters turned to domestic pigment, which immediately affected the quality of the painting. A certain change in the tone of the cobalt, which acquired a greyish bloom or dark specks, gave rise to a fashion for wares with such colouring. These tones of painting came to be called "old cobalt" or "pre-Transitional cobalt" (1620–1670). The distinctive features of "old cobalt" are a blurred design and a greyish tone.

**5. Qing dynasty.** At the end of the seventeenth century, alongside additional processing of the cobalt, magnets began to be used to remove iron. The refining of local cobalt pigments became more perfect and led to the invention of the bright-blue *qinghua* cobalt in the Kangxi period of the Qing dynasty.

In the Yongzheng period, cobalt painting was used for the outlines of wares in the *doucai* style. A cobalt of pale tones and bluish-grey *pervenche* shades was used (*pervenche* is a shade of blue: greyish-blue, pale blue with a lilac tinge).

Cobalt-painted wares of the Qianlong period were made in imitation of the Ming, but unlike the latter they have a more precisely placed, elegant line. Qianlong cobalt painting is distinguished by a particularly high quality of execution and a bright, saturated colour. This is achieved above all by a distinctive technique: the image is outlined with the finest cobalt line, a delicate cobalt wash is laid within the outline, and on the pale ground of the wash a scattering of tiny, darker cobalt dots is applied. The dots are placed in compact, logically arranged groups. The cobalt scattering is usually combined with clear forms and emphasises the divisions of the vessel, accenting the neck, shoulder, body and foot, and breaking up into distinctive concentric bands.

As a rule, cobalt wares of good quality always bear a mark that can be fully trusted, since they were made at the imperial works.

In nineteenth-century porcelain painting, the dark cobalt dots that imitate the effect of "textured" pigment in underglaze cobalt painting — called "heaping and piling" in English-language literature — are distributed evenly over the whole surface of the paint layer. This creates an impression of a certain dryness and artificiality and can be taken into account in dating.

## Types of painting on Chinese porcelain

**Susancai** — soft three-colour glaze. The glazes are as a rule applied directly to the biscuit (unglazed) porcelain. The style appeared at the end of the Ming dynasty and was the most popular within the *famille verte* style during the Kangxi reign.

**Wucai.** A term for polychrome painting of Chinese porcelain in underglaze blue (cobalt-based) with a slight addition of overglaze colours. Some images may be outlined in dark red or violet, while leaves and shoots have no outline. The earliest examples of *wucai* painting date from the Xuande reign (1426–1435). Wares of the Jiajing (1522–1566) and Wanli (1573–1619) periods are regarded as the standard. Wanli wares in the *wucai* manner often depict four dragons, each painted in its own colour — green, red, yellow and blue — with spontaneity, expressiveness, deliberately careless brushwork, asymmetry, understatement and an increased role of the white ground.

**Wucai ("five colours of enamel").** Despite the name, the number of colours in *wucai* decoration is not strictly limited to five. Mainly three enamels are used (red, green and yellow). Chinese *wucai* porcelain dates from the Ming dynasty (1368–1644). Dark-blue *wucai* differs from other painting in its bold, energetic strokes of cobalt blue combined with overglaze red, brown, green and black colours. *Wucai* painting was one of the last major decorative techniques developed in the Ming dynasty.

**Doucai** ("joined" or "fitted" colours). It combines underglaze painting in blue (cobalt oxide) for the outlining of all details of the image with overglaze polychrome enamel painting to fill the coloured areas within the outline. This specific variety of polychrome painting of Chinese porcelain has been known since the Xuande reign (1426–1435) and spread in subsequent periods. It reached its highest flowering in the Chenghua period (1465–1487), whose examples came to be regarded as "classic". These are superbly finished objects, usually small, and often bear a four- or six-character reign mark.

**Yongzheng doucai.** In the 1720s–1730s this variety enjoyed a new flowering in connection with the restoration of the damaged kilns and the return to complex production technologies; it is now called "Yongzheng *doucai*". The porcelain body is exceptionally thin and white, the glaze smooth and glossy. The painting creates a clear ornamental rhythm, and the blue outline slightly mutes and harmonises the intense colours. But whereas in Ming compositions the outline is broken and careless, in the 1720s–1730s it is applied more carefully — smooth, finely honed like a jeweller's work — and executed in a pale underglaze cobalt.

**Jiacai** — a term for a two-colour scheme of painting of Chinese porcelain combining underglaze blue (cobalt-based) with underglaze (copper-based) or overglaze (iron-based) red.

**Yangcai.** At the end of the seventeenth century new types of painting appeared using low-firing enamels from Europe, called "foreign colours". Among them was a violet-blue that began to replace underglaze cobalt in polychrome compositions; its use cut out some of the firing operations. White enamel was widely used, with which "highlights" could be created, modelling volume. Varied shades of pink enamel were also created — from purple-crimson to a cool violet — by adding gold trichloride to the enamel.

At the end of the Kangxi period, while the palace workshop was producing wares in the *falangcai* style, the imperial kilns at Jingdezhen were also experimenting with the production of enamels for painting porcelain.

The finest *yangcai* works were produced at the imperial kiln under the direction of Tang Ying during the Yongzheng and Qianlong reigns. The enamels have a characteristic opaque or powdery appearance owing to the presence of opaque lead-arsenic white enamel. The shading effect that produces tonal transitions is achieved by varying the proportion of coloured enamels over this white enamel. A typical example is the pink petals of flowers.

The difference between *yangcai* and *falangcai* lies in the place where they were painted: *yangcai* was made at the imperial kilns of Jingdezhen, *falangcai* in the Imperial Workshop in Beijing.

**Fencai.** In the mid-eighteenth century, private kilns began to use pink enamels in porcelain painting; the enamelling technique was called *fencai*, meaning "powder colours". Orders from European buyers contributed to the development of this technology, which later became known as *famille rose*.

## Glossary of terms

The following are the concepts and definitions most frequently encountered, which may be useful in the attribution of antique painted Chinese porcelain.

**Appliqué** — a method of creating ornaments or other images by laying pieces of a different material on the main ground.

**Arabesque** — a rich and complex ornament based on a fanciful interlacing of geometric and stylised plant motifs, sometimes including an inscription.

**Bas-relief** — a variety of raised sculptural relief in which the image projects from the plane of the ground by no more than half its volume.

**Blanks** — fired and glazed but undecorated porcelain wares.

**"Boneless" painting** — a term for the outline-free method of painting, which creates a pictorial image without a linear contour, by means of patches of colour and colour washes.

**Border** — an elaborate type of continuous painting, rich in design and colour, usually used as a ground.

**Calligraphy** — the art of beautiful writing.

**Cartouche** — a graphic ornament in the form of a shield or half-unrolled scroll framed with scrolls, containing a coat of arms, monogram or emblem. In the 1740s–1750s cartouches with figures and narrative subjects became widespread.

**Cizhou** — painting in reserve on a black ground on wares made in the Cizhou workshops of Hebei province. The decoration may be painted, incised or carved. Cizhou wares were produced in northern China in the Song dynasty.

**Combined wares** — porcelain wares combining several techniques on one object; for example, wares painted in cobalt or polychrome colours in reserves on a monochrome ground such as "powder blue".

**Cracked ice** — the typical design of *meihua* plum blossom on a ground of cracking ice. A stencil is laid on the object to cover the reserved areas, and the space around it is filled with solid colour, sometimes even, sometimes with pooling that recalls ice on a river. In the reserved areas some details — stamens, the veins of flowers and so on — are touched in with a fine brush.

**Decalcomania (transfer)** — the application of an image to the surface by transferring it from a template; as a rule it was used for border ornaments, while the main motif was painted by hand.

**Decorative motifs** — compositions in the "flowers and birds" genre, narrative scenes, symbolic and auspicious compositions.

**Digital colour printing.** Today most forged wares are decorated by digital colour printing. On close examination of such wares one can see that the pattern is formed of a multitude of multicoloured dots. Modern wares with a digitally printed outline and the ornament then coloured in by hand are also sometimes found.

**Embossing** — a stamping process that makes the design project above the surface.

**Falangcai** — painting on imperial porcelain from 1723.

**Festoon** — a decorative element; an ornamental band with a downward-facing pattern of leaves, flowers, stepped teeth, isosceles triangles and so on.

**Fluting** — a vertical groove or horizontal grooves on vases.

**Frieze** — the middle part of an object in the form of a narrow band, usually decorated with a design.

**Garland** — a classical decorative motif in the form of a continuous band of strands (leaves, flowers, fruits, ears of grain) twisted or laid over one another.

**Geometric carving** — the most accessible kind of carving in terms of simplicity and technique (after pierced carving): wedge-cut hollows repeated in a particular composition, which may differ in size, depth and angle geometry.

**Gold brocade** — a technique of painting in gold on a red ground.

**Gongbi** — realistic painting, often called the "imperial style". It is painstaking, fine painting with the emphasis on great control in the use of very small brushstrokes. This style allows the artist to express the most intimate life of objects through the smallest details, without independent or expressive variations.

**Grisaille** (from the French for "grey") — monochrome painting in grey tones with many tonal transitions combined with a fine black line. On Chinese porcelain grisaille usually appears without shades of grey and is used to imitate engraving.

**Ground** — the basic colour or tone on which an image or text is placed; the part of an image that forms the background.

**Guilloché** (from the French *guilloche*, a pattern of wavy lines) — an ornament in the form of a dense network of wavy figured lines interlacing with one another.

**Judging Chinese painting.** Chinese freehand painting values above all the virtuosity of the line, paying less attention to perspective and shadow than Western painting. The word *fuzao* ("hasty") is often used to characterise poor Chinese painting: it means that the painter tries to be fast but has no control over the lines. Good painting as a rule has longer lines that flow smoothly in the intended directions, like water. Poor painting has shorter lines, abrupt and defective endings, and unevenly thick strokes that cover the drawn lines badly. A good way to train your eye is to place any painting beside a good one and look for the differences described above. After a while you will be able to spot them at once.

**Landscape.** In Chinese the written term for landscape is made up of the characters for "mountains" and "water". Since the time of Confucius the rule has been followed that as little colour as possible should be used in painting. Painters were not interested in a realistic rendering of the subject; they cared only for conveying the impression they had absorbed. There were several traditional viewpoints, from a low one, from which trees and other components of the landscape appear the most important part of the picture, to a high one, from which trees are seen at the foot of towering mountains. There are three main elements of the classical landscape: rocks, water and trees.

**Lattice** — an openwork criss-cross pattern.

**Literati painting (*xieyi*).** The philosophy of literati painting is to sketch freely and rapidly an idea or "stream of thought" — in other words, to express emotion in painting. Chinese brush painting derives from ancient Chinese calligraphy and took shape over centuries. The style signifies a free, spontaneous approach that tries to capture the idea of the subject. Literati painting gives the impression that everything has been done with an almost careless freedom. *Xieyi* paintings are executed quickly, but they require long experience.

**Mark (stamp)** — an impressed sign with a name, initials or monogram.

**Meander** — a geometric pattern composed of right angles forming a continuous line.

**Medallion** — an ornamental relief or image enclosed in a round or oval frame.

**Millefleurs** — a decoration of porcelain meaning "a thousand flowers". The style depicts scatterings of peonies, chrysanthemums, lotus flowers, daisies, lilies and foliage, with tonal nuances, in polychrome enamels on a gold ground in reserve. The design signifies "all the flowers bestow their blessings upon you". The style first appeared in 1730 and was very popular until about 1850. In its early development small vases were made, and the flowers on them were usually smaller and more densely set on the gilded ground than in the Guangxu reign (1875–1908). In 1910 a black ground finely painted with gold plant ornament appeared on such wares. The size of the wares increased and unusual forms appeared (tea containers). The style of flowers on a black ground was widely used for export goods, which additionally bore a red-brown "CHINA" stamp.

**Ornament** — a pattern based on the repetition and alternation of its constituent elements.

**Painted porcelain** — wares decorated in the technique of painting.

**Painting in pink manganese colour.** From the 1760s, painting in pink manganese colour was used increasingly on porcelain.

**Palmette** — a plant ornament in the form of the fan-shaped leaf of a palm tree, an acanthus flower or honeysuckle.

**Perspective** — the science of representing spatial objects on a plane or other surface in accordance with the apparent reductions in their size, changes in outline and relations of light and shade observed in nature.

**Printed decoration (transfer printing).** A method of decorating enamels or ceramics using an engraved copper or steel plate, from which a monochrome print is transferred to paper and then pressed onto the ceramic object. In the 1750s considerable success was achieved in decorating ceramic surfaces with overglaze prints, chiefly in black. The printed-design technique was perfected from the mid-1840s and was subsequently used widely for mass production. The engraved design is transferred to a plate with glue or gelatine and then printed onto the surface, leaving an outline. Such wares were sometimes then coloured by hand.

**Proportion** — the relation of the sizes of the component parts of a whole. It may be harmonious or disharmonious and is therefore an essential factor in the formation of the whole.

**Qianjiang** — a type of porcelain painting in which landscapes are outlined in ink and supplemented with strokes of pale burnt-sienna colour to indicate the lit areas of the landscape.

**Quatrefoil** — a figure resembling symmetrical four-lobed leaves or a flower.

**Relief** — carved, moulded or flat (impressed) decorative images on the surface of an object.

**Repeat** — the basic element of an ornament; the part of a pattern that is repeated many times.

**Reserve** — an unpainted area of a vessel; a zone for decoration.

**Rocaille** — the principal ornamental element of the Rococo style, recalling the curl of a shell.

**Scroll** — an ornament in the form of curls.

**Sepia** — dry brown-black enamel painting, which appeared on Chinese export porcelain from at least the 1740s to the 1760s and then went out of fashion. It was revived at the end of the Guangxu reign.

**Sgraffito** — a kind of ground pattern; a style of decoration in which the glaze is scratched away while still damp so as to reveal the colour of the layer beneath.

**Shanshui** (Chinese, "mountains — water") — a genre of Chinese landscape painting including images of mountains (usually panoramic), rocks, streams or lakes.

**Style without outline.** Inspired by the *xieyi* style. The painting has no outlines; the strokes are made either in ink or in coloured paint, but each stroke produces an object or part of one. The form of the subject is rendered entirely by bold brushstrokes, without preliminary marking or structuring. The painter uses light tones, overlaying them with another layer. A leaf, for example, may be painted with one or two strokes of the brush. Accents such as the veins of a leaf or the stamens of a flower are added later.

**"Etching" style of painting.** Under the influence of European engraving, an original technique arose in the 1750s of modelling a design by applying black iron pigment in a hatched manner imitating a black-and-white etching or a European pencil drawing.

**Texture** — the character of the surface of a work of art.

**Tier** — an element of horizontal division; a repeating part of a structure, the tiers being placed one above another.

**Tobacco leaf** — tobacco leaves or the multicoloured tropical leaves of the hibiscus.

**Trefoil** — a figure resembling symmetrical three-lobed leaves or a flower.

**Willow pattern** — a popular painted motif from Nanking on wares with underglaze cobalt. There are several variants, but all show a weeping willow.
