# Imperial Porcelain

Imperial porcelain comprises wares of the very highest quality, made by hand in the official (imperial) kilns or workshops. Such wares must live up to the meaning of the words "magnificent" and "unique". Within these notions one must single out quality, originality of conception, level of execution, complexity of modelling and technological innovation. All details had to be executed with absolute precision and without technical faults. Particular attention was paid to details, which had to be of a very high standard. Everything had to delight the eye.

There was a set of requirements whose fulfilment makes it possible to approach the assessment of an imperial porcelain object in the best way:

- quality and originality of the idea;
- harmony of composition;
- precision of geometric forms;
- correctness of pattern and symmetry of ornament;
- an original silhouette;
- beautiful texture;
- colour combinations of enamels and glazes.

The huge deposits of first-class raw materials around Jingdezhen ensured the uninterrupted existence and development of the production of the finest Chinese porcelain over many centuries. The main imperial orders, as well as orders for supplies to the foreign market, went to these works. In the Song era the emperor ordered Jingdezhen to be designated a special place for the production of porcelain supplied only to the imperial palace. By the beginning of the fifteenth century the number of imperial kilns had already reached fifty-eight.

The Ming emperors made high demands on the quality of the wares. To improve the moulding properties of the raw material, the porcelain paste used to make the famous very thin-walled Chinese porcelain was kept sealed in the ground for a hundred years.

## Marks on imperial porcelain

An object made in the imperial kilns has a high artistic and technological level. Its body is pure and white, free from impurities; its glaze is brilliant; its colours are applied (depending on the period) either meticulously or with artistic virtuosity. In such a case the mark may — only *may*! — testify to the authenticity of the object.

It must be borne in mind that imperial marks were written on porcelain by a small number of highly specialised painters who spent their whole lives writing nothing but marks on porcelain. These marks on various objects are well known and recognised.

Imperial wares with underglaze cobalt painting and reign marks are distinguished by high quality: their forms are clear and set off by beautiful cobalt painting, the body is slightly tinted, and on the cobalt wash there is a scattering of black dots.

## The palace workshops in Beijing

In 1680 about thirty workshops were set up within the walls of the Forbidden City in Beijing with the official task of making works of art for the pleasure of the emperor and his court. In these workshops experiments began with enamel, used to decorate glass snuff bottles. The main difficulty in making enamelled glass was that the melting point of the enamels was almost the same as that of the glass.

In 1777 these workshops began to experiment with the production of painted porcelain in ***falangcai***, "foreign colours". Plain porcelain vessels were ordered from the imperial works at Jingdezhen. The porcelain surface on which the enamels were laid was unglazed biscuit. After the object had been decorated with enamels, it was fired in a muffle kiln in the palace workshop. Evidently, during the experimental stage the potters were still unable to overcome the technical problem of the different rates of expansion and contraction of the enamels and the porcelain surface, and Kangxi enamels are therefore characterised by very fine cracks. Enamelling was done on copper, porcelain and glass in specialised workshops in the immediate vicinity of the imperial living quarters in the Forbidden City, under the close personal attention of the emperor. The creation of the workshops was initiated by European craftsmen working at the court of the Kangxi emperor, who encouraged the production of new enamel colours to be used in Beijing. This style of porcelain decoration belonged to the emperor alone; ordinary potters were not supposed to know the technology.

In the Kangxi period, porcelain wares had a coloured ground imitating cloisonné enamel on metal. The favourite colour was red or yellow. The base bears an overglaze enamelled Kangxi mark.

In the Yongzheng reign, in 1728, additional enamel colours began to be used in the imperial workshops in Beijing, and the production began of wares with "flowers and birds" compositions accompanied by a poetic inscription and red seals. Because the workshops of the Beijing palace were never constrained by the need for large-scale production, their style of painting and colouring is marked by individuality. All *falangcai* is therefore delightful, yet there are differences in quality even within this select group of wares. The quality of the brushwork comes close to that of ink paintings on paper or silk. The variety of enamel tones, used in particular for the plumage of birds, is remarkable, with unusual shades blended together. Texture is rendered by applying thicker enamel, for example for the legs and claws of birds.

Under Yongzheng there was an imperial standard of elegance and refinement in the making of porcelain. The emperor even appointed a particular group of painters to prepare drafts and sketches for painted-enamel wares.

The styles of painting generally show the influence of Western court painters, who worked in a naturalistic manner, conveying an impression of volume, depth and perspective through shading. The realistic representation of birds and the shading of flowers show the direct influence of such artists as the Italian Jesuit missionary in China and court painter **Giuseppe Castiglione** (1688–1766). His style was a unique blend of European painting with Chinese subjects and themes. It is supposed that he took part in designing the decoration of porcelain in the *falangcai* style.

The painting of Yongzheng *falangcai* porcelain combines a free and laconic composition with meticulous rendering of details: petals, veins, supple stems. Exquisitely concise compositions appear, including a flower with a bud, bent blades of grass with insects, or small flowers with a bee or two butterflies, always with careful truthfulness in the modelling of the subjects. Characteristic features of the *falangcai* tradition include a rather saturated pink enamel (which later became somewhat paler), two shades of green enamel, and tree trunks executed in violet manganese enamel. The flowers show the finest tonal transitions from crimson to pale pink and white. In the Qianlong reign, in 1740, human figures were added to the painting of *falangcai* wares.

Later, porcelain was also painted in the imperial workshops in Beijing. Works made in the so-called **Hall of Prudent Virtue** (*Shende Tang*), the principal residence of the Daoguang emperor (1821–1850), are known. Porcelain made for this hall bears a characteristic mark.

Even the last self-proclaimed emperor of China, **Yuan Shikai**, who reigned from 22 December 1915 to 22 March 1916, ordered the production of imperial porcelain bearing his own seal to be organised. Enamels from the imperial workshops were used for these wares, and the red seal "Jurentang" served as their mark.

## Some varieties and features of wares from the imperial kilns

**Medallion bowls.** These bowls were used in the palace. The traditional view is that the blanks were made at Jingdezhen, decorated with an underglaze blue mark, and then sent to the imperial workshops in Beijing to be painted in enamels. They were often decorated on the outside with four medallions enclosing figures in landscapes.

**Chrysanthemum dishes.** The name of plates shaped like a chrysanthemum flower. There is a documented account that in 1733 the Yongzheng emperor ordered the heads of the imperial kiln at Jingdezhen to make chrysanthemum dishes in twelve colours. Later the range of colours available for enamel on porcelain was extended by the introduction of new pigments from Europe and by experiments made in the palace workshops in Beijing and the porcelain workshops of Jingdezhen.

**The reward vase (*shangping*)** — a vase with a slightly flared mouth on a slender neck, a globular body and a foot. This style of vase first appeared in the Yongzheng reign (1723–1735). The Chinese name of the vase with a globular body and long neck may be translated as "the vase that brings happiness". Such vases became a traditional imperial ware. The main glaze colours were blue and white, interpreted as "pure and incorruptible". The emperor bestowed them on officials whom he wished to reward for "pure and incorruptible" conduct.

**The imitative tradition** — a priority line of official production. This is porcelain with monochrome decoration in underglaze cobalt, in a technique combining cobalt and copper painting, and with polychrome painting. The imitative tradition, which took shape mainly in court art, is represented by various categories of copies that look back to the achievements of the potters of the past. One distinguishes **copies** (exact analogues), **imitations** (free repetitions close to the original) and **stylisations** (interpretations reproducing only the style, manner of painting or character of decoration).

**All-over decoration of the vessel surface.** Among the new techniques of polychrome-enamel decoration that appeared in the Qianlong reign is the filling of the whole surface with bright coloured enamels, leaving reserves on whose white ground a free composition was created. Sometimes the enamel ground was supplemented with sgraffito incising.

**Sgraffito** — a kind of ground pattern; a style of decoration in which the glaze is scratched away while still damp so as to reveal the colour of the layer beneath. In the imperial kilns of the Qing dynasty some wares were made with an etched enamel ground so as to show the white porcelain body in the lines of the ornament. The most common ornament is one in which spirals extend from one another; this version is called "Chinese plants".

## What one needs to understand about the classification of imperial porcelain

The only customer of the imperial kilns was the emperor, and he demanded very high standards of quality. The porcelain made in these kilns passed a strict selection. Pieces that did not meet the standards of quality were broken and buried, because ordinary people were forbidden to use them.

To prove the supreme quality of an imperial object, one must analyse the complex techniques and secrets of the art of porcelain, identify any faults in the object, and study the mark.

Most collectors are mistaken when they speak of Ming porcelain as very fine and expensive. There is a great deal of porcelain that is dated to the Ming dynasty without belonging to it.
