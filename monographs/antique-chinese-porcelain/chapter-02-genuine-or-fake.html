Chapter 2

How to Distinguish Genuine Antique Porcelain from a Fake

Как отличить подлинный старинный фарфор от подделки

Antique porcelain objects are works of artistic and historical value that are more than 100 years old. The earliest porcelain and ceramic objects of historical importance are often referred to by specialists as ancient or early antique pieces. Their age may exceed approximately 300 years.

Even an approximate determination of the time and place of manufacture of an ancient porcelain object requires extensive specialist knowledge and experience. For this reason, even experts in this field may disagree on questions of origin, dating and attribution.

Distinctive Features of Ancient Porcelain and Ceramic Objects

Ancient porcelain and ceramic objects have their own specialised collectors’ market. Such works regularly appear at auctions in New York and Europe. These early handmade ceramic pieces are most often seen in museums, where they are protected behind glass from light, chemical exposure and physical contact.

One of the best-known types is three-colour lead-glazed pottery, also known as polychrome sancai ware, decorated with flowing patches of coloured glaze. Until the middle of the twentieth century, sancai pottery was known mainly from literary descriptions and later imitations produced in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries. It is now difficult to imagine that collectors initially paid little attention to these ancient works. The first authentic examples were discovered in 1949 in a tomb near Luoyang, Henan Province.

Today it is established that this type of ware began to be produced in the last quarter of the seventh century, while the peak of production occurred in the first half of the eighth century. Ceramic objects of this period directly reflected contact between Chinese culture and the cultures of other regions. Among vessels of the Tang period, one may find forms whose outline, neck structure or handles recall the Greek amphora. During the Tang dynasty, alongside green glaze, yellow and brown glazes appeared, as well as the particularly rare blue cobalt glaze. Typical signs of age include a fine crackle network on the glaze surface, often covered with a whitish deposit. The base of such vessels is usually flat and unglazed.

The production of three-colour lead-glazed ceramics finally ceased during the Northern Song period. This may have been connected with the destruction of sancai production centres during wars. It is also likely that the bright colourfulness of such ceramics came into conflict with the new aesthetic principles that became dominant in Chinese art of the tenth to thirteenth centuries.

In recent years, collectors have shown growing interest in ceramic objects of the Song dynasty period, 960–1279. The forms of such works are marked by simplicity and balance. Feldspathic glazes have restrained colours and delicate shades of grey, blue and green. The feet or bases of many objects are often left unglazed.

An important decorative feature of this period is crazing, a network of fine cracks covering monochrome wares. This effect, which probably first appeared as a firing defect, was later used deliberately for decorative purposes. Chinese masters learned to control the formation of crackle, which results from differences in the shrinkage coefficient between the ceramic body and the glaze. As the body cools, it contracts and breaks the glaze surface. Potters even achieved delayed crackling, in which cracks of different thicknesses appeared over a period of months. To emphasise the crackle pattern, black and brown pigments, sometimes called the colour of “roasted rice”, were rubbed into the cracks. This is especially characteristic of Ge ware, associated with special kilns in Longquan. In addition, Ge ware used powdered rather than liquid glazes, which explains the abundance of tiny bubbles within the glaze.

A distinctive technological feature of glaze application was its thickness and abundance. In some cases, the ceramic body was deliberately left partly visible to create an effect of “incompleteness”. This reflected Chan Buddhist ideas, according to which the process itself, the path toward perfection, was more important than a finished state. The viewer, contemplating the ceramic object during meditation, was invited to complete it inwardly.

During the Mongol rule of the Yuan dynasty in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, a major innovation was the appearance of wares decorated with blue cobalt underglaze painting. At that time, bright blue cobalt was imported from Persia. The glaze is white and slightly matte. The skill of Yuan painters reached remarkable perfection in brushwork, expressed through confident strokes and lines with precise curves and thickening.

The Ming dynasty is usually divided into the following periods:

  • early Ming, 1368–1435;
  • the Interregnum, 1436–1464;
  • middle Ming, 1465–1521;
  • late Ming, 1522–1627;
  • and the Transitional period, 1628–1644.

At the beginning of the Ming dynasty, porcelain became widely used. Large fish bowls, garden stools and headrests, often called porcelain pillows, were produced.

From the point of view of the development of porcelain art, the most important reigns were those of the Xuande emperor, 1426–1435; the Chenghua emperor, 1465–1487; the Wanli emperor, 1573–1620; and the Transitional period.

The Manchu conquest of China and the establishment of the Qing dynasty in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries strengthened feudal institutions and expanded the territory of the state. The growth of maritime trade, together with imperial interest in porcelain, led to a major rise in porcelain production.

During the Qing dynasty, porcelain production became increasingly concentrated in Jingdezhen. By order of the Kangxi emperor, the imperial factory, which had been destroyed during the anti-Manchu uprisings, was rebuilt in 1674–1678. The factory received enormous palace commissions, while private production also expanded thanks to overseas markets. The reigns of Kangxi, 1661–1722; Yongzheng, 1723–1735; and Qianlong, 1735–1796, were especially significant for the development of porcelain art.

During the Kangxi period, porcelain production developed in the styles known as famille verte, famille noire and famille jaune.

Famille verte is a term introduced in the nineteenth century by French scholars, including Albert Jacquemart, Le Blant and Émile Grandidier. They used it to identify a distinct group of Chinese porcelain of the Kangxi period decorated with polychrome overglaze enamels. The palette included transparent greens in two shades, yellow, blue, iron-red, violet and black. The name of this decorative technique derives from the different shades of green characteristic of the style.

The porcelain body of famille verte wares is notable for its whiteness and plasticity. From the late seventeenth century onward, shaped reserves, cartouches and medallions surrounded by finely developed ornament became common. A large number of literary subjects entered porcelain painting. Paintings and engravings based on literary themes, as well as popular theatrical prints, served as models. This development was also encouraged by the high technical level of copying famous paintings, which provided themes for porcelain decoration.

The painting has several characteristic features. A simple division of separate scenes gradually developed into a continuous background with sharply defined reserves or medallions containing another subject, often painted in a different colour range. On famille verte wares, the background ornament is highly varied: a very fine spiral brown-black pattern scattered with red or gold peonies or other flowers; geometric ornament, such as a colourful network of connected octagons and squares; or intersecting lines forming a diamond-shaped pattern.

The geometric network developed many variations. Framing the central subject, usually flowers or a figural scene, it is divided into many segments. The painting is characterised by considerable naturalism, even to the point of depicting imperfections, such as small holes made by snails on lotus leaves. At the same time, small plant motifs are carefully composed. An exception is iron-red pigment, which is used under the glaze for outlines emphasising structural elements and as a ground for gilding.

A technical sign of authenticity in famille verte wares is the so-called halo effect, or the presence of iridescence at the surface of the glaze and around the enamels. This effect is especially visible around blue enamel. The iridescence resembles oil stains on water and can be seen when light falls on the glaze at a particular angle.

Imitations of Kangxi wares began in the mid-eighteenth century. At the imperial factory, an entire series of vases was created on the theme of “in memory of Kangxi”.

The appearance within famille verte of a black ground covered with green glaze, against which flowers, rocks and birds stood out dramatically, led to the formation of a new group conventionally known as famille noire. In famille noire, the enamel acquires a strong lustrous sheen, which distinguishes it from classical Kangxi works. There is less natural white ground in the reserves; it is often replaced by the application of white body enamel.

A subgroup of famille verte distinguished by a yellow ground is referred to as famille jaune.

The Yongzheng period marked the beginning of the development of famille rose. This nineteenth-century French term refers to a later type of polychrome painting with exceptionally delicate tonal transitions of soft pink enamel. It appeared in China around the 1710s–1720s. The term was introduced by French scholars to describe Chinese porcelain decorated with overglaze polychrome enamels using opaque colours mixed with white enamel, combined with a specific pink pigment based on colloidal gold.

Compared with the transparent famille verte palette, this new opaque palette could be fired at a lower temperature, allowing a much wider range of colours. The palette became softer and more delicate.

The technology of producing pink enamel was borrowed from European enamellers and adapted by Chinese potters in the 1720s. Famille rose painting flourished during the Yongzheng period. In Chinese terminology, this palette is known as fencai, meaning “powdered colours”.

The colour was obtained by adding a small quantity of gold trichloride. The shade of the enamel depended on the firing temperature. In addition to pink, the palette included green, yellow, violet-blue enamels, as well as iron-red and black.

Works of art from this relatively short period are distinguished by exceptional refinement, a quality emphasised by many scholars of Chinese art. Michel Beurdeley wrote that vessels of this period possess supreme refinement: “high art, marked by elegance without sweetness”.

Porcelain of the Yongzheng period is characterised by elusive elegance of form and painting, as well as artistic fluency of line. The colours tend to be pale and subdued.

The imperial kilns produced exquisite monochrome wares, including glazes described as “tea-dust powder” and “green peacock feather”. Particularly noteworthy is a group of vases that repeated Kangxi-style reserves, while the ground was decorated with innovative continuous enamels in lime green, mirror black and an unusual greenish-blue. This group may be regarded as a kind of tribute to the decorative famille verte style developed during the Kangxi reign.

The Qianlong period represents the peak of Chinese porcelain technology.

Complex techniques were brought to perfection, including underglaze painting in cobalt and copper, refined monochrome glazes such as flambé, oxblood, apple-green, cream, celadon, and famille rose enamelling.

During the Qianlong reign, new technologies continued to develop. As a rule, decorative details were carefully filled within their outlines. More glaze and enamel colours began to appear as background fields. Decoration was applied not only to white glazed porcelain, but also to coloured grounds: yellow, blue, pink, coral-red and light green.

In the second half of the eighteenth century, such porcelain was highly decorative. New ornamental motifs included geometric networks framing the central subject, usually a figural scene or flowers.

Black linear drawing was used not only to frame the decoration, but also for central images, reflecting European influence.

In the second quarter of the nineteenth century, porcelain production began to decline.

During the Guangxu period and the early Republic, considerable work was done to restore lost recipes for pigments and firing techniques.

The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries witnessed a steady revival of historical styles and a taste for realistic reproductions of antique vessels using improved technologies. Republican-period porcelain is often of excellent quality in both body and painting, and may reproduce the stylistic features of specific historical periods with great precision.

Since the founding of the People’s Republic of China in 1949, ceramic artists were guided by the slogan proclaimed by Mao Zedong, Chairman of the Central People’s Government: “Let the past serve the present.” During this period, great attention was paid to the development of artificial ageing technologies. In 1950, a school was opened in Beijing on the basis of the Research Institute of Decorative and Applied Arts, where porcelain makers were trained to reproduce traditional techniques. As a result, the number of professionally executed imitations and fakes increased further. Masters were interested not only in canonical works from the finest periods of “old” China, but also in objects specific to the late Qing period. Modern ceramic production began to develop rapidly after 1949 with the introduction of more efficient kilns. In the same years, overglaze transfer printing also came into use.

How the Age of a Porcelain Object Can Be Proven

Museums use thermoluminescence testing to identify signs of antiquity. This scientific method can determine the age of fired ceramics and porcelain, often over a range of several hundred years.

Thermoluminescence dating is based on the ability of certain materials, such as glass, clay, ceramics, feldspar, diamonds and calcites, to accumulate the energy of ionising radiation over time and then release it as light when heated. The older the sample, the more light emissions are recorded.

If the sample was at some point exposed to strong heating or prolonged sunlight, the original accumulated signal may be erased, and the dating must be counted from that event.

In fact, thermoluminescence dating does not determine the exact date of manufacture. It determines the date of the last heating to a high temperature. This could have been the original firing, but it could also have been a later fire or prolonged exposure to direct sunlight.

In recent years, an increasing number of scientific studies have focused on identifying signs of ageing in antique porcelain through microscopic analysis. Such research requires a modern high-power microscope, usually with magnification of 200–500 times, as well as other advanced scientific instruments. Specialists examine etching lines, glaze corrosion, glaze defects, surface deposits and other features.

Research may also include chemical analysis of materials and changes in molecular structure. This makes it possible, in a sense, to reveal the “ancient DNA” of ceramics. At the same time, traditional methods of identification have their own value and should not be dismissed. The best approach combines traditional attribution methods with professional scientific equipment. The methodology should include both macroscopic analysis — mark, style, technology and decoration — and microscopic features, or micro-signs.

When attributing porcelain, it is important to remember that an ancient appearance can be deceptive. Corrosion and patina may be created artificially.

Some fakes and imitations of antique porcelain date back to the eighteenth century, and certain examples may be traced even earlier. Museum specialists warn: one should assume that anything can be copied or forged, and that it is indeed copied or forged, whether with or without the intention to deceive.

When diagnosing a porcelain object, it is important to identify traces of artificial ageing. Visual examination with a 10x magnifying glass can reveal surface treatment intended to conceal undesirable signs that indicate a later origin. Excessively strong glaze shine may be dulled by mechanical abrasion or chemical treatment with acid. A body that is too white, especially visible on the unglazed edge of the foot ring, may be rubbed with dark pigment to imitate older wares.

Is the object genuine antique porcelain or a reproduction? This question can be extremely difficult to answer, as many modern Chinese companies produce reproductions of antique porcelain. Imitation wares are often deliberately fired under irregular gas and temperature conditions. Porcelain bodies contaminated with organic inclusions may be used to create defects in the body and glaze.

Artificially induced defects may include even spotting, deformation, obvious cracks, bubbles, dullness, pinholes and bare patches in the glaze. For example, in cobalt-painted wares, the pigment may be deliberately contaminated with manganese and iron impurities to give it a cloudy greyish tone.

Various technical methods have been used since ancient times to “age” porcelain objects.

The main protection against fakes is knowledge of the history of production of specific types of Chinese ceramics and the technological features of their manufacture. More specifically, one must understand:

  • which materials were used in particular historical periods;
  • how those materials were used and combined in a given ceramic type;
  • what kinds of finishing were used;
  • which methods of decoration were preferred at the time, such as painting, engraving or openwork, and how they were applied.

However, if a reproduction is made using old tools, glazes and enamels, distinguishing it from an authentic object can be almost impossible even for an expert. Old original moulds are also frequently used to create new copies.

A beginner in the field can avoid many mistakes by becoming familiar with modern fakes. There have always been, and still are, numerous workshops and individual masters specialising in forged Chinese porcelain.

It is therefore useful to obtain information about such workshops and craftsmen, and to understand the methods and technical possibilities they use to make objects “in the antique style”. Special attention should be paid to the decoration technique. Forgers attach particular importance to decoration, because it is usually the decoration that first attracts the buyer’s eye. The sharper and more aggressively treated the details appear, the more cautious one should be in assessing the object. To create a convincing antique appearance, many forgers produce artificial corrosion on the surface of their fakes.

When studying an antique porcelain object of historical and artistic value, attention should be paid to the method of forming. It should be remembered that although most artistically valuable nineteenth-century objects were made by hand, slip casting became increasingly widespread toward the end of the century.

When identifying a fake, look on the reverse side for porosity — small pits and bubbles on the surface. These may indicate that the object was cast, which is a common method in mass-produced reproductions. A cast reproduction often has a very glossy glaze and excessively bright colours. It usually lacks the finely worked finishing or engraving characteristic of genuine old porcelain, because the modern ornament is cast rather than hand-executed.

The method of forming and decoration allows one to determine whether all the nuances of artistic taste, style and manufacturing technique correspond to the period in which the work is claimed or assumed to have been made.

In ancient vessels, particular attention should be paid to whether the base corresponds to the historical period. During the Tang dynasty and earlier periods, a completely flat base without any noticeable projections was widespread. In later periods, such a base is found mainly on small jars, jugs and plates.

It is also important to identify traces left when the object was cut from the potter’s wheel. On the base of a vessel, triangular cutting marks may be barely visible through the transparent glaze. These marks are caused by the irregular movement of an old primitive potter’s wheel used to level the base of the porcelain object. If the base is unglazed, these marks are easier to see. They are typical of objects made from the first half of the seventeenth century to the middle of the eighteenth century.

These base characteristics coincide with the period of expanding porcelain production. However, it must be remembered that such marks may also appear on later objects. They are not unique, but usually indicate rapid manufacture and the use of a blunt cutting tool to separate the porcelain blank.

A magnifying glass is recommended for studying small air bubbles trapped in the glaze. In old pieces, these bubbles should be of different sizes and shapes and distributed unevenly. This is connected with the uneven heating typical of old wood-fired kilns. Modern glazes tend to have more evenly distributed bubbles.

On late Ming porcelain and, less often, on wares from private kilns made before the mid-seventeenth century, one may often see an opened bubble or blister in the glaze. Such bubbles reveal the unglazed surface of the porcelain body. This effect is most often observed on the neck and projecting parts of porcelain objects. Technically, the defect was caused when potters touched the edges of the unfired porcelain. From around the mid-seventeenth century, however, this technical problem was largely solved. To conceal the defect, brown rims began to be applied to the outer parts of porcelain.

A reddish base may be another sign of age. This is explained by the presence of iron in the porcelain body in the form of oxides, carbonates, sulphates and silicates. During firing, iron oxide is released and colours the porcelain red. The intensity depends on the iron content of the ceramic body. A porcelain mass contaminated with iron impurities may become yellowish-brown when fired with quartz, due to the release of iron oxides. Such a base is characteristic of early Ming wares and Transitional-period pieces. A dark brown base may occur if private kilns used for firing were built of bricks with a high iron content.

A grey or brown colour of the body appears when carbon does not burn out before the glaze melts. The same technological failure explains the presence of tiny crater-like pinholes. This is often a diagnostic feature of cobalt-painted wares of the sixteenth and early eighteenth centuries.

It is well known that different materials were used to decorate porcelain in different periods: underglaze pigments, overglaze pigments and enamels. It should also be remembered that particular stylistic periods preferred specific materials and can often be recognised by the chosen material and its method of application, in combination with other signs.

The safest and most reliable identification method is examination under strong magnification. For example, one reliable sign of age is the so-called orange peel effect on the glaze. Also known as chicken skin, this surface texture resembles orange peel or chicken skin. It occurs on certain wares made during the Song dynasty. It is also found on many black-and-white wares of the Ming dynasty. It appears especially often on monochrome sacrificial red, coral and blue glazes from the imperial kilns of the eighteenth century. This effect is not seen on all types of porcelain and may be a useful sign of authenticity.

To confirm age, it is important to look for a halo effect on the surface of the porcelain object. Such halos are caused by the use of lead as a flux, particularly in blue enamel, where lead reduced the melting temperature and made it possible to fire blue enamel together with other enamels. This phenomenon is especially visible in early Kangxi-period blue enamel. Iridescent stains are most clearly seen when viewed at a certain angle to the light. This effect is regarded as a sign of authenticity in Kangxi and later wares.

Another important sign of age is iridescence. An iridescent shine produced by a coating of metal oxides is often found on the surface of blue, brown and green enamels on porcelain from the early eighteenth century onward. This effect is difficult to copy correctly and is generally considered a good indication of age.

There is also another method for diagnosing the age of painted porcelain. In 2003, Erik Nilsson examined cobalt lines on different types of wares under a microscope at 100x magnification and identified the so-called hollow-line effect. This refers to the tendency of cobalt particles to concentrate along the outer edges of a blue line. The effect is characteristic of wares with underglaze cobalt from the late Guangxu and early Republican period, especially the first decade of the twentieth century.

Eighteenth-century wares showed normal underglaze blue lines without hollow lines and had fewer bubbles in the glaze over the blue painting. It has been suggested that in later porcelain this effect may have been caused by the fact that biscuit-fired porcelain was fired before cobalt painting. Biscuit firing was introduced into the Chinese porcelain industry in the first decade of the twentieth century and then became common practice.

A collector should test theoretical knowledge through practical observation of a wide and securely dated group of objects, preferably in museums. Familiarity with museum collections makes it much easier to recognise crude mass-produced fakes, since signs of handwork, such as traces of joining or hand painting, are usually absent in such imitations. Whenever possible, objects should be studied in person, because book reproductions may be misleading.

The rising demand for antique Chinese porcelain has led to rapid growth in the forgery industry, and the artificial ageing of modern objects has become almost a separate “art” in itself. It is important to remember that modern porcelain fakes often tend to look overly obvious. Their main weakness is a lack of restraint: they often lack elegance, subtle tones, proportional balance and refined colour relationships.
