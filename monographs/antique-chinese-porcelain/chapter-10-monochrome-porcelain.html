Chapter 10

Varieties of Antique Chinese Monochrome Porcelain

Разновидности старинного китайского монохромного фарфора

Author: Nona Dmitrievna Dronova

Monochrome porcelain is antique Chinese porcelain decorated with single-coloured glazes (from the Greek monokhromos, “of one colour”).

Originally, monochrome glazes were used to decorate altar vessels — the set of sacrificial ritual utensils. A decree of the Ming emperor of 1369 prescribed the use, in temples of state significance, of ritual utensils of an established pattern, according to which monochrome porcelain was used for particular groups of altar vessels: blue for the Altar of Heaven (Tiantan), yellow for the Altar of Earth (Ditan), red for the Altar of the Sun (Chaoritan) and white for the Altar of the Moon (Xiyuetan).

Under the regulations established in the Ming dynasty, which remained in force in the Qing empire, porcelain objects of certain categories were decorated with monochrome glazes of sacred colours. This ensured the high level of their execution throughout the subsequent history of traditional China until the fall of the monarchy in 1911.

The potters of the imperial kilns were obliged to experiment with new techniques and styles. They created quite remarkable glazes of various colours and shades. The iridescent blue, violet or brown splashes and spots characteristic of these monochromes were produced by the transmutation of colloidal solutions of copper, iron or other metallic materials in the surface of the glaze under different firing regimes. The potters controlled the firing process in the kiln with virtuosity, having mastered both oxidising and reducing firing, which produce different shades of glazes and colours. The difficulty lies in catching the right temperature and the right amount of air or smoke at particular moments.

The potters achieved particularly great success in making monochromes during the reigns of the Yongzheng (1723–1735) and Qianlong (1736–1795) emperors.

Red monochromes

Particularly notable is the red monochrome glaze with the finest crackle, distinguished by its variety of shades, which is called “sacrificial red”. This glaze is bright red, even crimson, owing to the presence of copper fired in a reducing atmosphere. Copper-red pigment is the most difficult to control in firing: the right conditions must be maintained throughout the firing process to achieve rich crimson tones. Copper-red glazes on porcelain began to be perfected in China during the Yongle reign (1403–1424). Originally these wares were used as imperial ritual vessels connected with the sacrifice to the Sun. At the end of the Ming dynasty copper-red glazes became less common.

Monochrome red was particularly popular from 1723 to 1795. The glaze is even and opaque, and as a rule has an “orange peel” surface. It became standard as a ground for imperial wares.

Red glazes are distinguished by their shades and by the pattern of their spots. To the highest grade belong wares with glazes called “great red robe” or “cardinal red” (bright red and “flawless”); to the second grade, the glazes “red bean”, “apple red”, “beauty’s blush” and glazes with spots of various shades. The third category comprises glazes with tonal transitions, called “baby’s face” or “peach blossom”. The lowest categories include glazes named “elm bark”, “donkey’s liver” and “horse’s lung”.

The classification has its own particularities. For example, if a red glaze is brightly and evenly coloured, it is called “great red robe”; a slightly mottled glaze is called “drunken beauty”; and if the colour tends towards a reddish pink (with dots), it is called “peach blossom”.

To a special, rare category belong wares with the glaze called “green lichen” or “mottled bean”. This is a purple-brown glaze based on copper oxides with inclusions of greenish specks. The basis of the glaze is copper, and the small green spots on the pinkish-red ground form where the green copper oxide has not been fully reduced to red. The green indicates points of accidental oxidation during firing, which is extremely rare. Firing of this kind was very difficult. These objects were produced only in small quantities for the emperor’s private orders. They were never taller than 20 cm, and their main purpose was to adorn the scholar’s desk. During firing the base colour, which looks like a ripe cherry, changes, giving many different shades. The appearance of non-round green specks (light shades of green, like green moss on the ground) is considered particularly rare.

Somewhat later, by deliberately changing the methods of high-temperature firing, potters began to obtain unusual shades passing on one and the same object from reddish-lilac to silvery blue. In Europe such wares are said to have a flambé glaze (French, “flamed”). This specific variety of copper-red glaze (from high-temperature reducing firing), with the addition of manganese forming silvery-violet runs, was first obtained in the Kangxi period. The colour results from firing in which a copper-oxide glaze, which would normally give green, is reduced to pure copper by carbon deliberately introduced into the kiln, and shimmers with every shade of red, with violet and blue.

Blue monochromes

Blue monochromes come in various shades, depending mainly on the quality and quantity of the cobalt used. They are fired, like porcelain with blue painting, at high temperature. They are classified by shade (light blue, dark blue, lavender and grey-blue).

There are several types of monochrome blue glaze, depending on how the glaze is prepared and applied. There are three main methods of application: dipping, painting and blown (“powder”) cobalt.

The following are distinguished:

Mazarin dark blue. A deep, saturated blue, found at the end of the seventeenth and the beginning of the eighteenth century on export porcelain, often with gilding. The name may derive from Cardinal Mazarin, chief minister of France (1601–1662). The same blue was used as a ground colour in painting with reserves.

Powder blue (French bleu poudré). A technique of decorating Chinese porcelain by spraying (blowing) cobalt pigment onto the unfired body through a bamboo tube, followed by glazing and firing. It was invented in the Kangxi period. The difference between the varieties of blue glaze lies in the size of the cobalt particles. In the “powder blue” variety the wares show specks, visible as powder, which create a mottled pattern.

“Precious-stone blue” (baoshilan). Found on wares made in the imperial kilns. It is characterised by a velvety, deep, even blue tone. The colour is pure, the surface unctuous and thick, and an “orange peel” texture is noticeable.

Other monochromes

In the 1720s–1730s, complex firing techniques giving an unpredictable final result were revived. Bright green and pale lime-green colours appeared, as did wares with a blue glaze with crimson spots or runs, and vessels with matt glazes of bluish and peacock-green colour.

The following are distinguished:

Blanc de Chine — a type of white porcelain ware made in the Ming dynasty.

Clair de lune (French, “moonlight”) — a glaze of delicate bluish-greenish colour with a low cobalt content (about 1%). It first appeared in the Kangxi period and was produced at the imperial kilns of Jingdezhen for a limited number of forms. The Chinese call this shade of pale blue “sky after rain”, or use the French expression clair de lune. Vases with this glaze were made as gifts for members of the imperial family.

Lavender — a blue-pink monochrome glaze, developed in the Qing dynasty and used only on imperial wares.

Mirror black — a black monochrome glaze, brilliant as a mirror. It is rare and laborious, requiring high-temperature firing with a cobalt-iron ore. It was obtained in the Kangxi period. Black wares were often painted in gold.

Turquoise, or kingfisher blue — a glaze of bright turquoise colour. Various shades and tones of this glaze were used to cover the interiors of vases and their bases.

Matt blue — a matt glaze of blue colour, used on imperial wares.

Peacock green — a glaze of matt, deep, bright green with a tinge of blue, used on imperial wares.

Tea dust — an olive-green, two-layered, speckled glaze. It is obtained by blowing the finest particles of green glaze onto a yellowish-brown or dark-green glaze, and is fired at high temperature. The unctuous surface of the glaze tends towards a yellowish tone and is evenly covered with small black and grey specks. The forms are stately, simple and elegant. The base usually bears an incised four-character Yongzheng mark in two columns. Shades called “eel yellow”, “snake green” and “crab green” are distinguished.

Robin’s egg — a mottled, opaque turquoise glaze with irregular crimson and violet spots, developed in 1723–1735.

Partridge eggs — a mottled white glaze with black-brown spots, developed in 1723–1735.
