Chapter 4

Features of the Attribution of Antique Chinese Porcelain

Особенности атрибуции старинного китайского фарфора

Author: Nona Dmitrievna Dronova

The attribution algorithm

  1. Determining the country of production.
  2. Determining rarity for collections.
  3. Classification and description of the object.
  4. Analysis of technological features.
  5. Stylistic analysis and study of the mark.
  6. Critical analysis and identification of inconsistencies with the stated period of manufacture.
  7. Dating and attribution of the object.
  8. Artistic merit.
  9. Appraisal and attribution.
  10. Rarity.

Analysis in the appraisal of antique porcelain is the mental and recorded division of an object or a collection into its individual components in the course of its study.

1. Determining the country of production. Way of life, local traditions and techniques of manufacture and painting, the varieties and particular properties of the raw materials, and traditional forms and details all help to determine where a ceramic object was made.

2. Determining rarity for collections. This is connected with classifying the purpose of the porcelain object. Practically the whole diversity of antique Chinese porcelain can be divided into three kinds: (1) ordinary wares, as a rule anonymous, which interpret the aesthetics of porcelain only approximately, at the level of a generic, collective image (mass production with a “standard” form); (2) export porcelain; (3) “landmark” wares, which define the line of development of the art of porcelain and which, in the individuality of their imagery, approach works of fine art.

On first acquaintance with an object, one must decide whether it belongs to the ordinary wares made for the people. This type includes Chinese commercial porcelain made for trade, for export and for local markets. Most Chinese porcelain is “ordinary” porcelain and is unlikely to interest a collector.

Export porcelain is also easy to single out by a number of features (motifs, painting, forms).

For a porcelain object that may be of value to a collection, a comprehensive analysis of its stylistic features and artistic merit must be carried out.

In the study of antique Chinese porcelain, the search for literary and illustrative sources, together with their analysis, classification and recording, is very important. The study of sources develops an interest in stylistic analysis, sharpens attention and one’s sense of orientation when taking part in auctions, and teaches one to scan quickly and select what meets a high standard.

The assessment of the “formal” aesthetic characteristics of an object — harmony, consistency of colour and quality of execution — makes it possible to establish its rarity and exceptional character.

3. Description and classification of the object

Porcelain objects are classified by form, size, presence of a glaze layer, purpose, completeness, and by the types and levels of complexity of their artistic decoration.

The description of a porcelain object covers:

  • form (simple or complex);
  • description of the form;
  • dimensions;
  • applied (moulded) decoration;
  • handles;
  • covers;
  • completeness (single piece or set);
  • gilt-bronze (ormolu) mounts.

Forms are distinguished as cylindrical, globular, spheroidal, ovoid, apple-shaped, cubic, quadrangular, hexagonal and others.

There are standard names for the various forms of antique Chinese porcelain:

  • Vase — a vessel of elegant form with painted or moulded decoration, made of clay, porcelain or other materials.
  • Sleeve vase — a tubular vase without handle or spout.
  • Bottle — a vase with a globular body, a long neck and a narrow mouth.
  • Double-gourd — a gourd-shaped oriental bottle, which may be double or triple.
  • Dish — a flat, plate-like vessel; with raised sides it is called a bowl or basin.
  • Bowl — a low vessel with a wide top.
  • Ewer — a vessel for liquid with a handle and a spout for pouring.
  • Flask — a flat or oval bottle.
  • Jar — a round, deep vessel, often with a handle and a cover.
  • Beaker vase — a vase with a flaring mouth and a swelling central section.

There are also specific names for Chinese porcelain objects:

  • Censer — one of the sacred temple vessels, used for burning incense on especially solemn occasions.
  • Garlic-head vase — a bottle-shaped vessel with a mouth in the form of a head of garlic.
  • Gall-bladder vase — a vase with a pear-shaped body and a very narrow, long neck.
  • Fangping — a four-sided vase.
  • Cong — a four-sided vessel with a round opening, derived from funerary objects symbolising the union of heaven and earth.
  • Meiping — a vase with a pear-shaped body and a very narrow, short neck, intended for a single branch of flowering plum.
  • Hu — a pear-shaped vessel with a short cylindrical neck.
  • Tazza — a temple bowl on a high foot. Its body is painted with stylised lotus leaves, stems and flowers, into which Tibetan characters may be woven; the high foot carries stylised petals, a pattern of bats and Lamaist garlands of pearls.
  • Rolwagen — a tall cylindrical vessel with a short neck, dated to the 1640s–1650s.
  • Tea caddy — a container for tea.
  • Jardinière — a decorative pot intended to hold small potted plants or cut flowers.
  • Guan — a rounded jar with a wide mouth and a cover, intended for storing tea and for New Year offerings.
  • Wan — a bowl without handles on a low foot ring.
  • Amphora — an elongated vessel with handles on both sides.
  • Archaistic vessel — a porcelain object imitating archaic forms. To give a more “authentic sense of antiquity”, it was often covered with a “rust-coloured” glaze with a metallic sheen.
  • Scholar’s objects — works of art traditionally used by the Chinese scholar in his studio. Most of them relate to writing and painting: small pots, water droppers for adding water to dry ink cakes, and small vases and boxes of various sizes and shapes needed for calligraphy.

The description of a vase includes the baluster form, the neck (the part between the mouth and the shoulder), the mouth (the upper opening of a round vessel), the foot ring, the rim (the outer edge of the mouth), the body, the applied decoration and the shoulder.

By size, ceramic objects are classified as small or large. Round objects are measured for height, rim diameter, maximum diameter and base.

The presence of moulded decoration on a vessel is recorded — for example animal heads, rings or fruit. In describing handles, their form and their decorative or functional nature are established. If there is a cover, its features are described.

By completeness, single pieces are distinguished from sets. Objects belonging to a set share a unity of decoration, construction and form. Porcelain garnitures consist of three or five pieces: usually three covered ovoid vases (or one) and two beaker vases. They were placed on mantelpieces or on the floor on special stands.

Gilt-bronze mounts — applied ornaments made of bronze and serving as decorative and structural elements — are also described.

4. Analysis of technological features

Visual diagnosis of antique porcelain makes it possible to identify the properties of the material: the porcelain body, the glazes, the enamels, the uniformity or non-uniformity of the object, and the size and uniformity of the bubbles in the glaze.

In assessing the quality of an antique porcelain object, one must check for forming defects: uneven wall thickness, tool marks, and foreign matter in the porcelain body.

The signs of age in antique Chinese porcelain are indicated by the technological features of its manufacture. These include:

  1. the colour and tone of the porcelain body;
  2. the characteristics of the glaze;
  3. the characteristics of the base;
  4. the technique of decoration (painted or monochrome porcelain);
  5. the characteristics of the enamels;
  6. the characteristics of the painting;
  7. the signs of handwork (joining lines, traces of the potter’s wheel on the base).

In analysing and diagnosing an antique ceramic object, it must be remembered that porcelain is a type of ceramic impermeable to water and gas. Porcelain can be distinguished from other ceramics by the clear, sustained ring or high, pure note it gives when lightly tapped with a wooden stick; the tone may vary with the form and thickness of the object. In thin sections porcelain is translucent; it is hard and glassy.

Porcelain and stoneware differ fundamentally in the composition of the body and in their production process. An obligatory component of the porcelain body is “porcelain stone” (a rock of volcanic origin consisting of feldspar combined with white mica). The manufacture of stoneware involved the addition of quartz-bearing materials, most often sand. Stoneware production did not require such careful purification of the components as porcelain production, and its body is therefore usually greenish, pale cream or greyish-white, noticeably different from the snow-white body of high-quality porcelain (porcelain of low quality sometimes shows slight colour impurities).

The next fundamental difference between stoneware and porcelain lies in the firing temperature. The firing temperature of stoneware varies, depending on the time and place of manufacture, from 1050 to 1250 °C. Porcelain must be fired at no less than 1350 °C; only then does a qualitative transformation of the original physical structure of the body take place, so that, thanks to the silica contained in the kaolin clay, it becomes glassy, translucent and completely impermeable to water. In attribution it is important to determine the thinness and translucency of the body.

Visual examination allows the material — porcelain, faience or stoneware — to be identified by the following features:

  • the colour and tone of the body;
  • texture and uniformity;
  • hardness;
  • translucency;
  • the sound when tapped;
  • the purity of the material.

Careful study of the glazes is an indispensable part of diagnosis.

According to whether a glaze layer is present, wares are divided into glazed and unglazed. Glaze is a coating on ceramic objects that gives the surface its lustre. It consists of ground kaolin, feldspar, quartz, clay and water, sometimes with other materials such as colorants and fluxes added to lower the melting point. The glaze must be fired together with the ceramic body, either in a single firing or separately.

The transparent glazes that cover porcelain consist of pure quartz sand combined with kaolin, feldspar and limestone mixed with water. The limestone is usually burnt together with grass, which provides ash. Coloured glazes acquire their particular colour and tone from the presence of various metal oxides.

The lustre of a glaze depends on its composition and on the firing regime. A sharp loss of lustre and the appearance of matt areas on parts of the surface are significant observations. The structure of the glaze is examined under a 10× loupe.

A special subdued lustre is obtained by applying the glaze in several layers of different transparency.

The refractory glaze is applied as a suspension by dipping, pouring or spraying. After glazing, the glaze is wiped from the foot or the upper edge of the porcelain object to prevent it from fusing to the support during firing, or to other pieces when objects are fired in pairs.

Once the bodies have dried, the glaze suspension is poured into a large container, and each object is dipped into it in turn for a few seconds; the excess liquid is removed so that the finished object has no drops or runs.

Antique ceramic objects often bear the traces of kiln setters. Setters are objects used to support a ceramic piece in the kiln during firing, in order to minimise warping or contact with the glaze. They are designed to have the smallest possible area of contact with the glazed object. The interruptions in the glaze they cause are called setter marks or firing marks.

In the attribution of antique Chinese porcelain, great attention is paid to the surface texture of the object. It may be matt or glossy, smooth or in relief. Every texture carries its own aesthetic and serves to give the decoration expressiveness. Studying glazes and enamels of different textures, and the contours of the painting, makes it possible to date a porcelain object more precisely.

The description of the glaze includes:

  • the colour or tone of the glaze;
  • its thickness;
  • its transparency;
  • its fineness;
  • its uniformity;
  • the presence of flaking;
  • the size and uniformity of the bubbles in the glaze;
  • patina;
  • cracks;
  • unglazed “biscuit” areas;
  • deliberately created crackle;
  • the presence of iridescence;
  • the presence and colour of glaze on the foot ring.

The analysis of technical and technological characteristics is of fundamental importance for dating. To establish the time of manufacture, one must be able to find features connected with the technologies and places of manufacture of a given period. For example, a very white porcelain body combined with lower translucency than that of eighteenth-century wares may indicate the second half of the nineteenth or the early twentieth century. The glaze of late Qing wares has a greyish tinge, compared with the colourless glaze characteristic of eighteenth-century wares.

Another diagnostic feature is the brown rim. Historically it is a defining feature of the years 1644–1661. The glazes used for the brown rim were the simplest, based on “yellow earth” — in other words, mud. Depending on the content of iron and other impurities, the tone and colour of the rim varied over the years and even within a single batch of porcelain.

From about the 1630s, a brown rim appears on the mouth of the object, imitating a silver or copper band. Such rims are rarely found on porcelain of the Yuan dynasty.

Biscuit porcelain — matt and unglazed — should be considered as a separate group. It is sometimes said to be called “biscuit” because it is fired twice. In porcelain production, a first (biscuit) firing is carried out, followed by a glaze firing. Biscuit porcelain is also fired twice, but the second time without glaze. An unglazed surface may also be combined with thin layers of transparent glaze. Painting in large designs on the unglazed fired body is often found in the early eighteenth century in the famille verte style; after firing, it gave deeper tones than on glazed porcelain. To this type belongs a special kind of Kangxi porcelain decorated in white, yellow, green and aubergine.

When analysing an object, the base must be studied carefully:

  • glazed or unglazed;
  • the presence of crackle;
  • tool marks;
  • the presence of decoration;
  • the colour of the base;
  • the quality of finishing of the base;
  • the presence of other coatings;
  • the character of the edge around the base;
  • plain or decorated base;
  • the presence of a mark (in cobalt, enamel or iron red).

By decorative technique, the varieties of Chinese porcelain fall into two main groups: painted and monochrome. Painted porcelain is divided into two independent subgroups: objects whose principal decoration is overglaze polychrome painting, and objects with underglaze painting in cobalt and copper. Within each group or subgroup, objects are arranged chronologically.

Porcelain is painted in two ways: under the glaze and over the glaze.

In underglaze painting, which is especially common on Chinese porcelain, metal oxides able to withstand great heat (cobalt, copper, hematite) are applied directly to the body and then fired together with the glaze. The colours are applied to unglazed porcelain; the object is then covered with transparent glaze and fired at high temperature, up to 1350 °C. The design must be executed carefully and precisely, since paint absorbed into the body cannot be removed. Defects of underglaze painting include separation of the paint from the body (when it is applied too thickly or onto a dusty surface), crawling of the glaze at the start of melting, and blistering of the colours (caused by insufficiently calcined pigments).

During firing the colour fuses into the glaze, sinking beneath its thin layer. After a good firing the colours are glossy and free from any roughness. It is important that the colour should not spread from under the pen or brush. The design is applied to a well-dried and clean body; otherwise the solution penetrates to the reverse side.

The oldest overglaze technique is painting in enamel colours, whose palette is rather limited. The variously coloured overglaze enamels are mixtures of pigments (oxides of iron, cobalt, copper and others) with fluxes (lead or lead–boron silicates). During firing the fluxes soften and the colour fuses onto the glaze.

Changes in and fading of colours may result from too high a firing temperature. In that case individual colorants decompose, changing colour and dissolving in the molten flux to form weakly coloured or colourless silicate glasses. The effect of excessive firing temperature is especially noticeable on turquoise, purple and pink colours containing iron and gold: at higher temperatures turquoise becomes grey-blue, purple and pink acquire a bluish tinge, and gold loses its lustre, becomes dull and is easily rubbed off. This defect may also be caused by the reducing action of flue gases entering the muffle through cracks. Flaking of colour during firing results from applying it too thickly. Runs of colour result from thinning it with more turpentine or oil than necessary.

Dryness of colour in the design results from too low a firing temperature, too thin an application or too little flux in the colour. Hairline cracks — fine fractures in the surface of the design — cause soiling and shedding of the colour from the body; they result from too thick an application or an incorrect proportion of flux, and occur most often in low-melting colours.

When the temperature is raised too quickly, organic matter burns out intensively, releasing a large quantity of gas that cannot escape fast enough and blisters the colour.

Crawling of colour appears as separate bubbles in the design: in firing, the colour seems to boil, forming foam-like bubbles that burst when touched. The causes of this defect are an excess of organic matter in the colours, a rapid rise in temperature, poor washing and insufficient drying of the objects, poor-quality transfers and improper storage (over-drying).

Under-firing of the colours occurs when the firing regime is not observed: the colours are dull and rub off easily.

Smudges and scratches on the surface of the design result from careless application of the colours or careless washing.

Contamination of the design occurs when grog dust from poorly fired or uncleaned kiln shelves falls onto the objects during firing.

Enamel is a glassy mass formed by partial or complete melting, with metal additives, applied to a metal base. There is a great variety of enamels, differing both in the composition of the enamel mass and in the technology of preparing the base.

Enamels are distinguished by colour and transparency:

  • matt or glossy;
  • transparent or opaque;
  • with or without crazing;
  • by the manner of application.

Recommendations for assessing the artistic merit of a porcelain object

Porcelain objects are classified by the types and levels of complexity of their artistic decoration. The price of an antique porcelain object always takes into account the quality and complexity of its enamelling, engraving and painting.

The techniques of decorating porcelain — that is, the types of its artistic treatment — include enamelling, engraving and painting in enamel colours.

It is important to note the presence and quality of relief decoration. Raised or sunken decoration was especially widespread in the Song dynasty. Decoration may be applied by engraving, by piercing or by raised relief. Raised relief is produced in forming by modelling; sunken relief by incising, drilling or impressing the surface. Vessels may also be decorated with a second layer of contrasting colour, which is incised or cut away through the slip. Engraving and carving are classified as simple or complex: simple work comprises simplified ornaments and single motifs; complex work comprises varied compositions and the imitation of other materials.

A characteristic of antique Chinese porcelain is the so-called “rice-grain” porcelain. The name refers to a technique, not to actual grains of rice. In this type of decoration, holes are pierced through the fairly thick walls of the raw, unfired porcelain and then filled with translucent glaze. When everything has dried, the walls of the vessel are thinned by hand.

In the Yongle reign of the Ming dynasty, fine rice-grain porcelain was made in the imperial kilns. After the Qianlong period such wares were made in both imperial and private kilns. Antique rice-grain porcelain occasionally has metal rims.

A porcelain object is analysed by a number of separate but closely related characteristics:

  1. Artistic design of the object (originality, emotional character, historical analysis, artistic idea, elements of the decorative scheme, techniques, technology and methods of manufacture).
  2. Technical execution (skill of execution, proportions of the construction, absence of defects).
  3. Condition (defects, extent of damage, completeness, replacement of lost elements, etc.). If there is no visible damage, one should write: “No noticeable damage.”

The beauty of a porcelain object is one of the fundamental aesthetic categories, marking the qualitative perfection of the object that corresponds to the accepted standard for antiques. Aesthetic perception evaluates not only the form but also its relationship to the essence of the object. A person’s contact with the aspects of the object’s content that matter to them determines the depth and power of the aesthetic experience — the stronger, the more they associate their own personality with the finished product of creative work.

There can be no absolute criterion of beauty that is not related to the typological characteristics of the object.

A number of factors help to assess an antique Chinese porcelain object as well as possible. In attribution, one must substantiate and specify:

  • the essence of the creative idea of the object and its meaning;
  • the materials and technology of manufacture;
  • the novelty of the idea or technology;
  • the correspondence of the style of the object to its historical period;
  • the quality of execution.

There are classical criteria for assessing the artistic merit of porcelain:

  • Proportion — usually understood as the relationship of the parts to the whole and to one another.
  • Unity — allows the whole object to be taken in at a single glance and perceived as an indivisible whole.
  • Harmony of decoration — the arrangement and relative size of the elements of the painting.
  • Colour scheme — the unity and integrity of a colour harmony that does not irritate the eye.
  • Careful choice of horizontals and verticals — which draws attention to the details.

In determining the artistic value of a porcelain object, one must take into account the diagnosis of its method of manufacture and classify the work by categories of complexity.

Categories of complexity in enamelling porcelain

First category. Application of single-colour enamel to objects of simple form to obtain an even, mirror-like surface. Filling patterns on objects with up to three colours of enamel. Subsequent (post-firing) painterly retouching in a single colour.

Second category. Filling with up to three colours of enamel on objects of complex shape. Painterly retouching of the pattern after firing in up to three colours.

Third category. Filling with up to five colours of enamel, or application of enamel to objects of complex shape. Application of enamel to large flat surfaces. Filling reserved patterns with enamels of three colours with different melting temperatures.

Fourth category. Manufacture of complex, highly artistic enamelled objects. Application of enamels rendering a relief image through colour and light and shade by means of glazing (layering).

Categories of complexity in painted enamels

First category. Application of simple outline designs — ornamental patterns, flowers — to an enamelled surface.

Second category. Painting after models in six to eight colours on an enamelled surface: plant forms, landscapes, complex ornamental compositions.

Third category. Application of multicoloured pictorial images. Painting on enamel with repeated firings after models of complex compositions with human figures and animals.

Fourth category. Portrait painting, depiction of architecture and of human figures in complex foreshortening. Designs in a range of more than three colours with shading.

The algorithm for determining the quality of the artistic treatment of a porcelain object:

  • identify the type of artistic treatment;
  • determine the category of complexity of manufacture;
  • select a comparable object (analogue) for calculating the value of the art porcelain.

Quality examination, appraisal and attribution of porcelain

Quality examination is the expert assessment of an object according to established criteria that determine its estimated value. First the method and quality of production are established (modern moulding or handwork); then the technique is described (painted porcelain, monochrome, celadon, enamels).

Unlike the many kinds of porcelain objects perceived mainly as articles of everyday use, works of art in porcelain require a different approach: they present the porcelain object as a phenomenon of culture.

The basic category of porcelain objects should be the single object. A single monument is any handmade piece that meets the relevant criteria of value. Collections are assemblages of single monuments that possess the qualities of a valuable historical and cultural object only as a whole and that form specialised collections of various kinds.

In establishing the rarity of porcelain objects, chronological, socio-cultural value and quantitative criteria are applied.

The chronological criterion is the “age” of the porcelain object, determined by the length of time between its date of creation and the present.

The socio-cultural value criterion refers to distinctive spiritual and material properties, usually expressed as:

  • uniqueness — distinguishing the object as one of a kind, with individual features of historical, cultural and scientific significance;
  • priority — characterising the object as of fundamental importance for the development of technology, history and culture;
  • collection value — showing that the object belongs to a collection that is itself an important historical and cultural object.

The signs of the quantitative criterion are limited distribution (small production runs, limited access) and the rarity of the porcelain objects, determined by the relatively small number of surviving examples.

Rarity may be established by individual criteria or by a combination of them.

Marks on Chinese porcelain

Antique porcelain has the advantage that it was provided with marks which, for the most part, accurately reflect the time of manufacture. Originally, only imperial porcelain bore marks.

Marked imperial porcelain must show a high standard of quality and the finest design, and the mark must be correctly written. If an object bears the name of a reign (Kangxi, Qianlong) but is clearly the work of an unskilled hand, or has firing faults producing colour distortions and the like, its mark may well be false.

Of course, there are still marks that have not been deciphered or published. Wares made in private kilns may bear so-called “hall” marks. Many porcelain objects are not marked at all; they were made mainly in private kilns. Doubts should be raised by certain features of the writing of a mark: an irregular double circle, uneven line thickness, and drops of cobalt formed when writing the characters, which are uncharacteristic of mid-Qing porcelain.

Imperial reign marks, like all Chinese texts, are read from top to bottom and from right to left. The first character is therefore the one in the upper right-hand corner. Marks may also be written in one, two or three columns or rows.

Naturally, the mark must first be read correctly — while bearing in mind that many marks do not correspond to the period of the reign. The “tradition” of applying false marks is connected not only with the fashion for Chinese antiquity, both in China itself and in the West, but also with the decree of 1677 that prohibited private manufactories from using the reign mark of the ruling emperor. On the products of private kilns, therefore, one may find no mark at all, or an earlier one; or, as was customary in the Kangxi period, the base may bear a circle, an artemisia leaf, an orchid flower, a hare, a double lozenge, a lingzhi fungus and so on. Two underglaze blue circles are characteristic of the years 1677–1780. The absence of an inscription, or a mark consisting only of a circle, meant that the object was not made at the imperial manufactory.

The characters are written under or over the glaze; on unglazed porcelain they are sometimes impressed into the body. Sometimes the mark takes the form of a seal with stylised characters. From the mid-eighteenth century, a bright or pale pistachio-green enamel came to be applied to the inside of a vase and to the base, on which a mark written in cobalt or in overglaze iron red was placed in reserve. These are most often marks of the Qianlong or Jiaqing (1796–1820) reigns. If a mark in this technique names an earlier reign, it is therefore not genuine.

From the second half of the nineteenth century, marks were placed in decorative medallions surrounded by dragons or plant ornament. On modern porcelain, the most common mark is “Qianlong” in overglaze iron red.
