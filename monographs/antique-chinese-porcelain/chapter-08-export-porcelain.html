Chapter 8

Export Porcelain

Экспортный фарфор

Author: Nona Dmitrievna Dronova

Chinese export porcelain is defined as porcelain made for use outside China. Export porcelain of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries is found on the market quite often, and for that reason there are practically no forgeries of it.

Today the trade in Asian export art is taking these objects back to their homeland. Ceramics, furniture and decorative art made in the Far East between the early fifteenth and the mid-nineteenth century specifically for shipment to the West now increasingly attract the attention of collectors from the countries of origin.

In recent years export porcelain has been classified on the antiques market as “Chinese East India Company art”.

The East India Companies and the porcelain trade

What does the term “East India Company” mean? From the end of the sixteenth century maritime trade expanded. “East India Company” is the name of a number of trading companies of European countries in the colonial era. Chinese porcelain was in great demand at auctions in Europe, despite the high prices at which it sold. Yet the objects so popular in Europe were made specifically for export and were of much lower quality than the porcelain made for the imperial court.

Chinese porcelain was transported for the most part by Chinese merchants, whose junks sailed to the Philippines, the Indonesian archipelago and the coasts of Vietnam, Thailand and Malaysia. The enormous quantities of porcelain needed to satisfy the colossal demand for it, once it had become fashionable in Europe, were carried from southern China to Batavia in Chinese junks. From time to time Dutchmen or other Europeans might privately join a Chinese crew, but certainly none of the East India Company’s ships sailed to China in the early eighteenth century to buy porcelain at Canton. The whole risk of this process fell on the Chinese side; the Dutch did not pay the transport costs, and competition kept prices low. In 1729 the Company’s ships began to sail directly from the Netherlands to Canton and back.

Batavia (today’s Jakarta) was an important market and a centre for the distribution of porcelain to Holland and to other markets supplied by ship, such as the Islamic markets of India, Indonesia and Iran. The Dutch East India Company had its administrative and trading departments in this city, which drew trade from all over Asia to it. Batavia served partly as a base for further intra-Asian trade and as the point of departure for ships returning to the Netherlands.

From 1757 Canton (now Guangzhou, the economic centre of southern China) remained the only port open to foreign trade. In the eighteenth century ships of most European states came there. Porcelain occupied a considerable place among the goods exported, and the port shops were overflowing with it. Foreign buyers, however, were not satisfied with ready-made wares of a purely Chinese character and gradually began to place orders for particular forms and decoration that were not traditional for Chinese craftsmen.

Jingdezhen and Canton

At this time the Jingdezhen works remained the main supplier to the court of the Chinese emperors, but they also gradually became the main source of export porcelain. Merchants placed their very large orders through Canton. Fired porcelain blanks were sent from Jingdezhen to the workshops of Canton, where they were then painted in overglaze enamels. This was caused by the need to keep in direct contact with the customer and the mass buyer, and at the same time it led to a wide penetration of engravings and drawings into the Chinese workshops, which Chinese painters copied very carefully onto porcelain.

It was common practice to order an assortment of goods with a special design through a Chinese captain, who passed the order to the porcelain workshops at Jingdezhen, and then to wait for the objects to be delivered to Batavia. Jingdezhen, for centuries the centre of porcelain production, consisted of hundreds, if not thousands, of small private workshops where porcelain was made and painted. Workshops producing porcelain for export had to work so that the goods looked attractive to foreign clients. They often made special pieces to order and did not produce goods for the local market.

It appears that there was extreme specialisation, one workshop making only teapots for export and another only cups and saucers. The painting done in the workshops where the porcelain was made, or in special workshops where it was decorated, was likewise specialised. The products of these workshops were fired in large kilns, space in which was bought from their owners.

The producers and owners did not distribute the finished product themselves but used licensed brokers, who made deals with Chinese merchants who distributed the orders. It may be supposed that, being small and specialised, these export workshops were not the first to follow fashionable trends in porcelain painting, as the larger and more important factories were. Quite possibly they went on working as they had for years, only gradually adapting to new fashions and new themes in painting, and only when their products had become truly outdated and provoked complaints from merchants and brokers.

Adaptation to foreign taste

The forms and decoration of porcelain were adapted to foreign models. The first European subjects were religious, most often the figure or likeness of Christ, but secular subjects also occurred. The custom of ordering porcelain from China bearing the coats of arms of noble families became widespread. The copying of European engravings and drawings, and the depiction of Europeans from life, was not mechanical; the models were reworked.

When North America began to send ships to trade independently with China in the early 1800s, export wares were produced with a limited number of standard patterns. Popular designs were painted in underglaze cobalt under the name “Nanking”, with pagodas and river landscapes. Enamels with patterns of flowers, birds, butterflies and mandarins in panels were called “Rose Medallion” and “Mandarin Medallion”.

The wares of private workshops, which even earlier had been coarser in both material and execution, now became still more careless: a coarse body and a greyish glaze. Roughness and grains of sand are constantly found on the base, and the base of export cobalt wares was not always even glazed. The division of labour also extended widely to painting. The more skilled painters executed the main design, while the less skilled specialised in particular details. There is no doubt, too, that the painting process was divided among the craftsmen by colour — one worked in red, another in blue, and so on.

Western export porcelain is easy to recognise by its specific forms and decoration. Images of mandarins, flowers and butterflies are characteristic.

For Turkey, Persia and the Islamic markets, enormous trays and tall water jugs were made. For the countries of the Middle East, wares were produced with Chinese painting but of specific forms (perfume sprinklers, incense burners) and with Arabic or Persian inscriptions. For Central Asia, mainly bowls and plates with distinctive painting were made: large peonies, chrysanthemums and fruits unlike those usual on Chinese porcelain. Porcelain for Siam bears images of Buddhist deities. Porcelain made for the Japanese market was intended mainly for the Japanese tea ceremony: typically small objects, deliberately made to look distorted.

Varieties of export porcelain

Martaban jars. Among the many kinds of Chinese ceramics that were exported are ovoid jars sometimes called “martaban”. The name comes from the transit port of Martaban in Burma, a usual stop on the trade route of the ships that carried these ceramics. These large wares have bold decoration in bright enamels, mainly olive-green, golden-brown, brown or almost black.

Canton porcelain (“Canton” pattern) — the European name for export porcelain made at Jingdezhen but painted in Canton in the first and second quarters of the nineteenth century. The design consists of a large central panel with characteristic painting. One distinguishes the “Rose Medallion” (characterised by four alternating panels around a central roundel; the ornament includes peonies and groups of birds, butterflies and people in a house) and the “Mandarin Medallion” — a term introduced for export porcelain on which figures of mandarins (Chinese officials) are depicted in panels, surrounded by an abundance of flowers and ornament covering the whole object. The term “Canton enamel” is also distinguished, for painting with butterflies and flowers only.

Chinese Arita (or Chinese Imari). The first Chinese Imari was produced in 1722–1725 and remained popular until 1760. The term “Arita” comes from the name of the place in Japan where the kilns were located whose products, from 1659, were actively shipped to Europe from the port of Imari by the East India Company. By the 1680s, when the situation in China had stabilised and contacts with Europe had resumed, Chinese craftsmen, first in Guangdong province and then at Jingdezhen, began to reproduce wares with the characteristic blue-and-red painting, supplementing it with gold and with pink, yellow and green enamels. Compared with the Japanese wares, the Chinese ones have a clearer, more symmetrical composition.

Swatow — export ware of the Wanli period (1573–1620), produced at the end of the Ming dynasty. The name comes from Shantou, a coastal city in the south-eastern province of Guangdong. The painting is characterised by a predominance of iron red, turquoise and black.

Mushikui. Chinese blue-and-white porcelain of the seventeenth century exported to Japan. These are rather coarse pots for use in the tea ceremony. They are covered with a glaze with numerous pits and fritting at the rim, recalling “paper eaten by worms”.

Tenkei. These wares have a naive charm. Their designs show distorted proportions and broken scale, so that a flower or a butterfly may be larger than a human figure, or a huge crab may stand beside a tiny man.

Kraak porcelain. A type of Chinese export ware of the Wanli period for export to Holland and Japan. The name probably derives from the Portuguese carraca (carrack), the type of ship in which these wares were usually carried. It was painted in cobalt; the division of the surface into elaborate panels is characteristic. This type of porcelain was the first Chinese ware to be imported into Europe in mass quantities. Only the wealthiest citizens could afford the first consignments of kraak, which is why it is often seen in paintings of the Dutch Golden Age.

Lowestoft. An old and obsolete name for Chinese export porcelain decorated with coats of arms, eagles, hunting scenes, maritime emblems and mythological subjects.

Shipwreck porcelain. Finds from sunken ships.

Peranakan — a synonym for Nyonya ware. This exuberant, rich and colourful porcelain is a variety of polychrome export ware from China. This beautiful porcelain was used on festive occasions such as weddings, birthdays, anniversaries and the Chinese New Year.

“Warriors and battles”. Wares with military subjects, characteristic of 1900–1940. They have a typical crackle on a grey-beige glaze and a dark-brown glaze around the neck. The battle scenes include horses, unfurled banners and warriors in characteristic dress. The base of such wares bears a brown stamped mark.

What one needs to know when diagnosing export porcelain

1. Marks on export porcelain. Most antique Chinese export porcelain bears no marks at all. On export wares for South-East Asia from 1860 to the 1920s one often sees a smudged red square “seal”. Marks are practically never found on export wares made in the kilns of the southern provinces (no marks could be found on kraak, Swatow or Chinese Arita wares, or on large “palace” vases — that is, vases intended for European palaces).

From the second half of the nineteenth century, marks were placed on export wares too — a reflection, of course, of the demands of the Western customer, who paid attention to signatures.

The most common type of such wares are vases or large dishes with a thick body covered with crackled glaze. Individual parts are picked out with bands of iron-brown colour, sometimes with a lustrous sheen and slight relief, as though imitating metal mounts. This type of ware usually bears a mark incised within a brown square, most often with the reign title of Chenghua (fifteenth century), Kangxi or Qianlong. In such cases the porcelain researcher or collector must first of all compare the mark with the general appearance of the object and, above all, with the quality of the work. Most antique Western-export Chinese porcelain is unmarked.

Marks were nevertheless sometimes placed on porcelain wares. Chinese export porcelain can be divided by importing country:

  • the West — Europe and the USA (no marks, or marks are rare);
  • the Middle East and India (marks are rare);
  • Japan (marks are frequent);
  • South-East Asia (marks are frequent).

2. Blue-and-white export porcelain generally dates from the end of the Ming dynasty and the first few decades of the Qing. It is often found in the West at quite affordable prices. Many wares of the Transitional period differ substantially from the traditional Chinese ceramics produced before that time.

3. Hong Kong. The greatest number of forgeries can be found in Hong Kong. As a centre of trade it became one of many places where porcelain blanks were painted to European order. In the 1960s the porcelain factories and trade in Hong Kong reached their peak.

4. Modern copies of kraak. Recently, modern Chinese copies of kraak porcelain have appeared on the antiques market. There is an easy and simple way to detect them. The glaze is thick, soft and dull rather than glassy and brilliant, and the firing faults (grains of sand, blisters under the glaze, colour changes, swellings, warping of the form) are absent. On genuine examples the rims are usually thin and show a production defect of the period which antique dealers call “mouse nibbles” (mushikui). Some genuine kraak dishes, however, have perfect rims.

5. The McKinley Tariff Act. In 1890 William McKinley, a Republican member of the US House of Representatives, introduced a tariff bill that became known as the McKinley Tariff. Under it, foreign goods were subject to import duties. This affected the marking of Chinese export porcelain. The original text of the 1890 Act states: “On and after March first, eighteen hundred and ninety-one, all articles of foreign manufacture … shall be plainly marked, stamped, branded, or labeled in legible English words, so as to indicate the country of their origin; and unless so marked … shall not be admitted to entry.” As a result, Chinese porcelain was marked with the word “China”.

6. Cultural relics. Everything made in China before 1949 is considered a cultural relic and may not be exported from the country without a government seal and/or an officially issued permit. It should be borne in mind that nothing dating from the Qianlong reign or earlier (that is, before 1796) can be legally purchased in an antiques shop in China. There is a jianding seal that is placed on certified export porcelain; it serves as a sign of official permission to export.

According to official Chinese legislation on the protection of cultural relics, exported goods (including objects either handed down from earlier generations or found) must meet the following criteria.

Relics are divided into grades. In 1987 the Ministry of Culture published the circular “Grading and Levels of Cultural Relics”, which gives guidance on determining the grade of rarity. Grade one is a very rare relic, a symbol of Chinese culture. Grade two comprises objects of great historical or scientific value that are nevertheless widespread. Grade three comprises ordinary antiquities. In 1992 the State Administration of Cultural Heritage issued detailed rules for the assessment of “important relics”.

This essentially administrative process of determining the grade of rarity of relics may have consequences for criminal penalties.
