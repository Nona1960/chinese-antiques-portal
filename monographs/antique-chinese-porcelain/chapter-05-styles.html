Chapter 5

Styles in Chinese Antique Porcelain

Стили в китайских антикварных фарфоровых изделиях

Author: Nona Dmitrievna Dronova

The character and forms of porcelain objects reflect the life and development of human society, and the ideology, culture and everyday life of a particular epoch. Herein lies their historical significance. Over time, ceramic objects acquire characteristic differences. Many factors influence the emergence and development of a style.

Factors shaping a style

1. Class distinctions. With the development of class relations, porcelain objects acquired ever new meanings. They reflect not only features of culture but also differences of social position. It is known that there were times when the use of particular porcelain objects by the various estates was fixed by law.

Thus the image of the five-clawed dragon in the mid-Qing period (eighteenth century) retained its meaning as a symbol of imperial power — a meaning adopted as early as the end of the Yongle reign (1403–1424) — and indicated that objects bearing this motif were intended for the Court. Such objects were produced until the early twentieth century and served as a kind of normative marking of the owner’s status. Vessels decorated with an incised dragon under a yellow glaze of characteristic warm tone, known as “imperial yellow”, could be used only by the emperor, the empress or the empress dowager. All members of the imperial house used dragons of a particular colour as a symbol of social position and rank.

In the decoration of Chinese official dress in the Qing period, every element was strictly regulated, including the number of claws. The emperor could, for example, “grant” a fifth claw to an official for his merits. Accordingly, in the painting of porcelain, five-clawed dragons were obligatory on imperial wares, whereas on private production one finds mainly four-clawed dragons.

In China, membership of a particular estate was emphasised by the colour of the dragons depicted on porcelain. The dragon is perhaps the most popular subject in painting, in relief modelling or in incised decoration.

Under the Qing dynasty, the ruler ordered porcelain bowls to be made specially for the palace, decorated with dragons in different colour combinations according to the rank of the user:

  • yellow dragons inside and out — for the emperor, the empress and the empress dowager;
  • dragons on a yellow ground, white ground inside — for officials of the first rank;
  • green dragons on a yellow ground, white ground inside — for consorts of the second and third rank;
  • yellow dragons on a blue ground, white ground inside — for consorts of the fourth rank;
  • an aubergine-coloured dragon and green dragons on a yellow ground — for members of the imperial family and consorts of the fifth rank;
  • red dragons with polychrome painting — for consorts of the sixth rank;
  • polychrome painting — for consorts of the seventh rank.

2. The influence of personality. An epoch that creates a style is characterised not only by the scale of artistic activity but also by the success and talent of individual artists. Outstanding individuals played a great role.

One such historical figure was Tang Ying (1682–1756), superintendent of the imperial porcelain works at Jingdezhen, a post he held for more than two decades. Under his direction porcelain at Jingdezhen reached an unprecedented level. It was Tang Ying who created the “luxurious style” — the stylistic individuality of the imperial porcelain of the Qianlong era. In decorating the objects of that time, artists often practised an all-over (“carpet”) principle of decoration, combining various techniques and devices. The “luxurious style” was distinguished by a complexity and detailing of form, and a density of decorative motifs, greater than in any preceding period.

3. Social status. Style is a kind of constructive principle on which a culture is built. It includes such notions as “style of life” and “style of behaviour”. An example is provided by porcelain objects “for the scholar’s desk”. The making of such objects belongs to an aesthetic connected with the category of wen (literary culture). The literati were scholars with an academic education, often holding high administrative posts. They were expected not only to be well versed in literature and poetry but also to be painters and calligraphers, and they often painted for their own pleasure. These artists regarded themselves as ideal observers of the workings of the moral law in nature, since they alone were free both from the control of the academy and from the need to earn a living.

Because the bureaucratic aristocracy played a great role in China, and a high post could be obtained (even by a person of humble origin) only through the system of competitive examinations, enormous importance was attached to mastering the complexities of the characters and to the study of the Confucian classics. Objects connected with writing and calligraphy were treated with reverence. They entered auspicious symbolism as “noble” objects owned by “educated sons”. Objects “for the scholar’s desk” included brushes in brush pots, water vessels, table screens, ink stones, bundles of books, rolled painted scrolls, musical instruments, chess sets and so on.

4. Economic ties. In the 1750s–1760s, with the growth of trade with Europe, a style of export porcelain painting arose known as the “Canton style”. Until 1843, Canton (Guangzhou) remained China’s only link with the outside world. Depictions of Europeans, undoubtedly made from life, are not uncommon. The somewhat caricatured rendering of the Europeans who came to Canton is distinguished by liveliness and accuracy of characterisation, combined with a certain folk-print quality in the painting.

5. Cultural traditions. An example of their expression in porcelain is a distinct and characteristic group of vessels imitating archaic forms and bearing the taotie mask typical of Shang bronze vessels of the second millennium BC.

Stylistic analysis

A scientific approach to building knowledge and concepts of the styles of antique Chinese porcelain can be drawn from dictionaries, reference literature, textbooks and scholarly works. Such a study of stylistic features requires knowledge and use of special words — terms.

Styles in the art of Chinese porcelain do not have clear boundaries; they are in continual mixture and opposition, forming over a long time and passing smoothly one into another. Many styles existed simultaneously, so “pure” styles practically never occur. Nor are there “good” or “bad” styles: each style of the past played its positive role in the development of the art of Chinese porcelain.

The mixing of different styles in one object is called eclecticism (from the Greek eklektikos, “selecting”). Such mixing is a consequence of the interpenetration of different cultures, and this process cannot be judged in simple terms.

The most important criterion of attribution is stylistic analysis, based on an extremely complex study of Chinese culture, which results in a “sense of the epoch” when an object is examined.

Methods of gathering information fall into two groups: elementary (observation, reading literature, visiting museums and exhibitions, free conversation, testing, the study of documents) and synthetic (comparison of sources, analogy). By complementing one method with another, the best and most effective result can be obtained in collecting porcelain objects.

Critical analysis and the identification of inconsistencies with the stated period

Traditional expert examination is a critical analysis of the data of primary attribution (which provides provisional reference points: material, decorative technique, supposed date of the object under study, possible place of production and so on). It makes it possible to reveal contradictions among these data, or their inconsistency with one another, with a traditional scheme, with historical facts, and so on.

Porcelain objects are designated by the name of the ruling dynasty and, within it, by the name of a particular emperor. The most important dynasties for the development of porcelain are the Tang (618–907), Song (960–1279), Yuan (1279–1368), Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1912). Each epoch in turn is divided into early, middle and late periods, and by the reigns of the members of the ruling house, designated by their reign titles.

Style unites the regularities of form-making with specific technological methods of porcelain production on the basis of some general principle. A style is defined not by forms but by the very manner of form-making — by the relations between the individual elements of a form. Each historical period used characteristic forms. Let us consider some of them.

The Tang dynasty

In the Tang dynasty, ceramic objects were known by the general name tang sancai. Sancai (“three colours”) is decoration in green, brown and yellow glazes, with touches of bright green and amber, on pale yellowish bodies; on heating the glazes “ran”, forming distinctive and beautiful colour patterns. Blue and purple-brown wares are rarer.

A variety of figurines were made (people of different social status, age and sex, officials, soldiers, tomb-guardian monsters, the zodiac signs of a person’s birth year, and so on).

The appearance of this ceramic is connected with the fact that the principal materials of manufacture in Jingdezhen and its surroundings were refractory clay and white kaolin, to which a secondary dark-yellow kaolin that had already been fired was added. The decorative techniques were incising and carved or moulded relief designs.

Western antiquarians and dealers sometimes call Tang wares “egg and spinach” because of their use of green, yellow and white glazes. From the mid-eighth century the production of this ceramic slowed. The style later continued to develop under the Liao dynasty.

The Song dynasty

Heavy forms gave way to lighter and more airy ones, and the bright polychrome palette to muted, single-colour tones.

Cultural traditions were refined: in particular, schools of painting and ceramics developed, and the tradition of the tea ceremony, which originated in the Tang era, reached its perfection. Song ceramics are therefore strikingly different, with simpler yet elegant forms that convey the refinement of spiritual development and the self-cultivation of one’s inner qi. The forms of porcelain objects were distinguished by simplicity and calm. Their decoration used feldspathic glazes that gave muted colouring and a richness of the most delicate shades of purple, blue and greenish.

Porcelain objects were named after the five famous kilns of the time: Ru, Guan, Ge, Jun and Ding.

At the same time, the appearance of new firing techniques and the use of materials other than white and brown kaolin led to the rise of new pottery centres. Among the best known are the following:

  • Longquan, Zhejiang province. Here a pale-green “jade-like” porcelain was produced, called qingci in China and celadon in Europe.
  • Yixing, Jiangsu province — a centre for the production of unique ceramics from a rare purple clay.
  • Jingdezhen — the centre where unique “imperial” porcelain was produced, supplied exclusively for the needs of the imperial court.
  • Cizhou — this stoneware centre had formed as early as the sixth century, but by the mid-eleventh century a great number of varied wares were being made there. Their distinctive feature was unusual decorative techniques: sgraffito, three-colour overglaze painting, white slip and incising. The main motifs are floral and plant ornaments and compositions. Particularly interesting are wares decorated with leaves: a natural leaf was stuck to the unfired object, covered with glaze and fired.

In the history of Song ceramics, experts count a considerable number of different styles and techniques with their own unique features. Here are some of them.

1. Celadon ware, or qingci. The centre of production of elegant pale-green porcelain was the city of Longquan in the south of the country. Here, already in the Five Dynasties period, a special glaze with a high iron content, usually green in various shades, began to be used. During firing the potters deliberately raised the kiln temperature sharply, as a result of which defects in the form of fine cracks appeared on the glaze surface. This decorative technique is now called crackle. It reached its full development in the Northern Song. Potters created inimitable patterns on the ceramic surface, making it resemble precious jade. Since in qingci ceramics it was the texture and colour of the glaze that mattered most, the forms themselves were fairly simple.

2. Long Guan, or southern celadon. This type of ceramic, popular in the first half of the twelfth century, first appeared at the beginning of the Northern Song period. It was made by a technology similar to that of traditional qingci but had two key features. First, because of the high iron content of the clay, the white body darkened in firing, becoming reddish-brown or red. Second, the glaze was not green but blue-green — it was called “the blue-green haze of distant hills”.

3. Yingqing — “shadowy celadon”, the ancestor of the “finest porcelain”. At the end of the Song dynasty potters were only beginning to master yingqing, which is characterised by a very fine texture and an incredibly clear, melodious sound. The glaze was a very thin layer, and its icy-green colour recalled a ghostly haze.

4. Jingdezhen ciqi — porcelain that in the Song period began to be made at Jingdezhen from white kaolin. The first examples were pure white and decorated with incising. Practically all of this porcelain was supplied to the imperial palace.

5. Ji’an — a unique and now lost glazing technology. Glaze of this type is also called “hare’s fur” or “partridge feathers” because of its special texture of rust-coloured spots and streaks on a dark ground. This was achieved by adding ferruginous haematite to the raw material; in firing, its flakes acquired a golden colour. The glaze was brought to a temperature at which the crystals began gradually to dissolve, but not completely — they had been deliberately crushed to a coarse grind. As a result, after the temperature was raised, rivulets of orange, light-brown and golden colour formed on the surface, resembling in structure the fur of a hare or the feathers on a partridge’s wings.

6. Yaozhou greenware — a typical pottery style of the Northern Song. It originated in the Tang dynasty and flourished in the Northern Song. It is also known as the “northern sea-green”. The ware is characterised by a fairly translucent olive-greenish glaze and a light-grey body. These were dark-green glazed vessels decorated with carving depicting dragons, phoenixes or floral patterns.

In the Jun kilns (Henan province), remarkable Jun wares based on copper oxides were fired. The so-called flowing glazes are particularly notable. The distinctive streaks are formed by pieces of glaze laid over the main glaze and mixing with it during firing: one colour penetrates another, and on the delicate blue ground purple streaks, patches and spots with violet haloes can be seen. Potters often created a particular design, such as a fish or leaves.

7. In the city of Jingdezhen, Jiangxi province, official kilns were founded in the Song dynasty producing the famous greenish-blue porcelain with crackle. Collectors call such wares qingbai, which in Chinese literally means “clear blue-white”.

8. The Ge style is represented mainly by two types of ware. The first has a warm yellowish glaze with conspicuous dark cracks crossed by reddish lines; these are called “golden thread” or “iron thread”. The second type of Ge ware has a practically opaque, greyish matt glaze and dark crackle.

The Yuan dynasty

Porcelain with a white surface, allowing painting to be used for decorative purposes, came to the fore. Since in the Yuan era Chinese potters placed their main emphasis on underglaze painting, it was naturally distinguished by genre, colour scheme and a number of other features.

Jingdezhen, one of the largest pottery centres of the time, continued to supply porcelain to the imperial court. Besides Jingdezhen, which specialised in thin-walled white porcelain, there was another fairly large centre, Yuxi in Zhejiang province, where white porcelain wares in the tobi seiji style, decorated with iron-brown spots, were produced.

The most popular varieties of underglaze painting were the following:

  • Qinghua — one of the best-known genres of the period. White porcelain was painted in cobalt, usually with plant or other subjects. Such painting is characterised by very fine and sensitive lines, the highest technique of execution and the use exclusively of cobalt pigment, which can vary from light blue to extremely saturated. Graphic painting was usually done with a very fine line. The subject “flying ducks” is often encountered.
  • Fenghua ci — a less common technique of monochrome painting with a copper-based pigment. Alongside cobalt, underglaze copper red was also used, but it was still imperfect and partly ran under the glaze. Throughout the fourteenth century it was often brownish and strongly lustrous.

The Ming dynasty

In this period there was a transition from monochrome to polychrome painting.

From the fifteenth century the technique of double firing appeared: first cobalt is applied and the object is fired with its glaze at high temperature; then green, yellow, iron red (rough to the touch rather than smooth and oily) and aubergine (a manganese-oxide purple) are applied over the glaze. Iron brown and iron black were used for outlines. The second firing was carried out at a lower temperature, since lead was added to the colours to lower their melting point.

In the Xuande reign (1426–1435) the doucai style (“contending” or “competing” colours) appeared, using the same palette and double-firing technique, but the design was always bordered by a fine pale cobalt line forming a delicate bluish halo. Blue-painted porcelain was perfected still further. At first imported cobalt was used; then a local cobalt was found which displaced the more expensive imported one. Underglaze copper red with a brick-coloured tint was sometimes used together with cobalt. This pigment later went out of use because its supplies were exhausted, and an overglaze iron-oxide red, brighter and simpler to use, was adopted instead.

Collectors and connoisseurs single out wares of the Chenghua reign (1465–1487) as particularly exquisite. The raw material was very pure, practically free of iron impurities, so the body was distinguished by its whiteness and high translucency. The glaze was improved by reducing the amount of flux, giving a smooth, glossy surface with small, evenly distributed bubbles. The cobalt is pure, of a delicate pale-blue tone. Gilding appears on some objects with polychrome painting. The style of Chenghua cobalt wares is characterised by the combination of large and smaller motifs within one composition.

The style of porcelain of the Wanli reign (1573–1620) was influenced by the state of the kilns and the technological features of production, by a taste for antiquity — in particular for the forms of ancient bronze vessels — and by the growing influence of painting. Hence its spontaneity, expressiveness, deliberately careless brushwork, asymmetry, understatement and the increased role of the white ground. Wares appeared in which cobalt painting is set off by celadon (greyish-greenish) glaze. In both ceramics and porcelain, glazed works without painting became widespread.

Alongside the flowering of monochromes, the line of painted wares continued to develop in this period. Wares in the wucai manner often depict four dragons in medallions or on each side of the vessel, each painted in its own colour — green, red, yellow and blue.

Porcelain of the Transitional period (1620–1683)

This comprises the last two reigns of the Ming dynasty and the beginning of the Qing dynasty, including early Kangxi (up to 1683).

Some objects of this period were made with great artistry. A bright underglaze blue was used for painting, sometimes combined with a very difficult underglaze red made from copper oxide.

At this time some good-quality wares were made for scholars and officials, and some were produced for export to Japan and Europe through the Dutch East India Company.

Transitional blue-and-white porcelain is painted in a cobalt of deep blue; dealers describe this colour as “violets in milk”.

The pictures were executed by a creative hand. Many of the best objects are decorated with distinctive landscapes: sheer cliff walls, clouds, isolated conical mountains.

Wares of this period are characterised by a greyish-bluish glaze and careless drawings full of expression. Buddhist motifs with written characters stylised into ornament became increasingly widespread in painting. In the ornamental design, emphasis on various bands is noticeable, often expressed in a conventional drawing of water.

The stylistic features of “early Transitional porcelain” are characterised by such motifs and devices as branches with dots imitating leaves, sharp pine needles, dark rocks with tonal gradations, and the introduction into the composition of a fence that divides the space into asymmetrical fragments. Also very characteristic is the device of the V-shaped tuft of grass. Human figures have elongated, unnatural body proportions; the garments of the figures are covered with varied but rather primitive geometric ornament. Clouds and water surrounding the figures are merely indicated by simplified strokes.

Typical motifs of the Tianqi reign (1620–1627) are holy fools or Buddhist figures in unnatural poses and fluttering garments among heaped rocks or undulating clouds, often against a conventional ground on which the landscape is only lightly indicated by blurred patches or strokes denoting vegetation. Images of scholars conversing against the same conventional landscape are also common. In the modelling of the figures, unexpected complex foreshortenings and elongated, displaced body proportions are striking. Among the details, the drawing of the nose as a clearly outlined triangle attracts attention. This last device is so characteristic that it can be a real aid in attribution.

Characteristic elements of composition are asymmetrically depicted pines with hanging threads of moss, overhanging rocks with a branch growing downwards, and the combination in one painting of the “flowers and birds” genre with landscape.

The second period of “Transitional porcelain” (1630–1662) is characterised by a number of stylistic changes. Among the wares of this time are pear-shaped bottles, wide vessels with a lid sunk into the body, and cylindrical vessels (rolwagen) dating from 1640–1650.

Multi-figure compositions became widespread, always including clouds billowing among mountains; with the appearance of the coral-and-green palette, clouds were often executed in iron red.

In the Shunzhi reign (1644–1662), scenes of women in a garden or palace interior became very popular. Among the most widespread motifs are flowers and birds, and landscape compositions, sometimes supplemented by lines of verse. Ceremonial scenes and hermit monks are frequently depicted. Haima sea-horses among waves, treated in an ornamental manner, also occur.

Tree crowns and grass are rendered as follows: clear short strokes surrounded by pale blurred patches. The cobalt spreads beyond the outline, lying freely and conveying various shades. Figures are elongated with small heads, and the noses are outlined in the characteristic triangle.

The third stage of the Transitional period covers the 1660s–1680s. It is characterised by ribbed forms, a greyish-bluish glaze and careless drawings full of expression. Buddhist motifs with written characters stylised into ornament became increasingly widespread in painting.

From the beginning of the eighteenth century, cobalt painting using a reserve became widespread: a stencil was placed on the surface of the vessel to cover the reserved areas, and the space around it was filled with solid colour, sometimes even, sometimes with pooling that imitated “ice on the river”. In the reserved areas some details were touched in with a fine brush, executed in the reserve technique in iron red with a slight addition of enamels (for example, the eyes or fins of fish might be painted in green or white enamel).

Unglazed bases are a characteristic feature of this period, but not a rule.

The use of acanthus ornament, probably derived from European sources, is notable.

At the end of the Ming period, porcelain borrowed many devices from ceramic technology, above all the system of decoration with coloured glazes. Celadon, beige or copper-red glaze might be applied to porcelain, sometimes combined with cobalt painting.

The Qing dynasty

Unlike earlier epochs, especially the Tang and Song, Qing porcelain is distinguished not by region but by period. The famous wares of the famille verte or famille rose are not characteristic of any particular region — they were produced throughout the country. The same applies to other decorative and production techniques.

The Kangxi style (1662–1722)

In this period the production of porcelain of the very highest quality was established. Kangxi blue-and-white porcelain reached the peak of perfection at the end of the seventeenth century, thanks to the very good quality of the cobalt-blue pigments. The bases of porcelain objects of this period are carefully finished and polished.

The most traditional technique remains painting in cobalt, in which, alongside the old Ming devices, new ones arose, often by combination with other kinds of decoration. Cobalt-decorated wares can be divided into the following categories: free painting in cobalt on a white ground; painting in reserve; free painting combined with the reserve technique; “powder-blue” (blown) cobalt; decoration combining painting with powder-blue cobalt; and cobalt drawing combined with glazes and polychrome enamels.

Alongside the predominant underglaze cobalt painting, painting in overglaze enamels appeared. In European sources these were called famille verte, famille noire and famille jaune.

It is also important to note that the enamel colours were applied both over the glaze and directly on the biscuit. On the iron red covering petals, numerous black dots are usually applied, dense at the base and then thinning out. The “frog spawn” ornament (a green ground with black dots) is widespread.

New varieties of glaze were obtained, designated in European literature as “ox-blood”, “peach bloom”, “powder blue” and “flambé”.

Porcelain vessels were often embellished with bronze mounts emphasising the necks of vases, lids and bases.

Yongzheng (1723–1735)

The emperor took an interest in the Imperial Porcelain Works, and the finest, most artistic pieces were produced during his reign. The porcelain was very thin and often imitated the style of the Ming emperor Chenghua.

On the whole, the Yongzheng reign continued the so-called “painterly” line in the development of porcelain decoration, both in subject matter and in technique. The famille rose palette began to play a decisive role; in other types of painting there was a tendency towards muted colouring, with the most subtle working-out of tonal variations and attempts to render light and shade. In the depiction of flowers one sees the finest tonal transitions from crimson to pale pink and white. A characteristic sign is pink enamel pooling in small raised beads.

In visual attribution, attention should be paid to the special, refined restraint of colour and a certain mannered elegance in the appearance and decoration of the wares. For a whole series of wares painted in polychrome enamels, characteristic dating features are the filling of the interior of the vessel with bright pistachio-green enamel, and inscriptions in characters on a free white ground with red artist’s seals, borrowed from painted scrolls.

The presence of the reign mark, which was often placed on wares for the imperial court, helps in the correct attribution of Yongzheng wares.

In 1730 the millefleurs technique (French, “a thousand flowers”) appeared for the first time. In its early development a gilded ground, as a rule, serves as the background for floral ornament covering the whole object. In this period handles in the form of elephant heads are widespread on vessels and vases. Cobalt painting is distinguished by light-blue tones with bluish-greyish pervenche (periwinkle) shades.

Qianlong (1735–1796)

The decoration of wares became more complex, marked by the combination of the most varied devices.

The most popular technique is painting in reserve with the whole surface filled with complex ornament. The “white on white” technique was introduced, in which the design, most often on a white or celadon glaze, is traced in white enamel resembling lacework. Porcelain was often covered with gold painting.

Imperial wares are characterised by “brocade” ornament, in which the objects are decorated with fine needle-point incising in the paste reproducing brocade or imitating textile patterns.

The second half of the Qianlong reign is characterised by increasingly complex forms which nevertheless keep a clear outline.

The main attribution feature of late Qianlong may be the characteristic flat handles in the form of stylised writhing dragons (on the neck or shoulder). At the bottom the handles seem to break away from the body and form a curl. They are usually covered with enamel of a contrasting colour. Such fanciful handles are an important stylistic feature of vessels of the second half of the Qianlong reign.

Cobalt painting is distinguished by a particularly high quality of execution. Painters returned to a bright, saturated cobalt, achieving a special velvety resonance. This was achieved above all by a distinctive technique: the image is outlined with the finest cobalt line, a delicate cobalt wash is laid within the outline, and on the pale ground of this wash a scattering of tiny, darker cobalt dots is applied. The cobalt scattering is usually combined with clear forms and emphasises the divisions of the vessel, accenting the neck, shoulder, body and foot, and breaking up into distinctive concentric bands. As a rule, good-quality cobalt wares always bear a mark that can be fully trusted, since they were made at the imperial works.

Monochrome-glazed wares are supplemented not only by a fluted body but also by applied relief and by a crackled greyish-cream glaze on the base. In the absence of a mark, it is precisely the character of the glaze on a heavy body that points to the Qianlong period.

Painting in the famille rose palette became very widespread during Tang Ying’s tenure, and from the second half of the Qianlong reign it became, as it were, a component of varied polychrome painting.

All-over filling of the surface with ornament is characteristic. Ornament alternates with painting of various genres, frames the reserves and is arranged in bands that fix the continuous division of the vessel. It was placed on the shoulder, at the base, along the mouth of the vessel and so on. The white ground practically disappears from porcelain objects. Sometimes the enamel ground is supplemented by incising (sgraffito). Painting in pink manganese colour is sometimes used.

The late Qing

The nineteenth century is defined as the time of the late rule of the Manchu Qing dynasty, or the late Qing period, comprising the reigns of Jiaqing (1796–1820), Daoguang (1821–1850), Xianfeng (1851–1861), Tongzhi (1862–1874) and Guangxu (1875–1908).

The following stages in the development of porcelain are distinguished:

  • 1796–1855 — the period of eclecticism;
  • 1855 — the destruction of the Jingdezhen kilns;
  • 1855–1864 — stagnation;
  • 1864–1911 — restoration.

Jiaqing

Stagnation set in in ceramics and porcelain. The bulk of production consisted of replicas imitating the wares of the Song, Ming and early Qing, in 75% of cases of rather low quality.

In an attempt to show originality, the artists of this period experimented with combinations of different devices, for example combining cobalt painting with crackled glaze. Rather interesting is the dark-red “ox-blood” glaze, used in combination with bright colours. Even so, overly loud and bright tones could not replace the refined simplicity and elegance for which eighteenth-century porcelain was famous.

Daoguang

Even in the famous workshops of Jingdezhen the quality of manufacture declined: the tones are too pale, lines are often asymmetrical, and the subjects garish.

The painting of porcelain shows dryness and considerable simplification of ornament. Its design is based on harsh colour contrasts and a more limited palette. The colours are dull, without half-tones. The early nineteenth century is characterised by great dryness of line, incomplete filling of colour within the outlines and small white gaps.

Some imperial workshops nevertheless continued to produce porcelain of very high quality.

Xianfeng

In 1855 rebels destroyed the imperial workshops at Jingdezhen and killed most of the craftsmen. The long interruption that followed in the work of the country’s leading ceramic centre, which “fed” national porcelain production, can confidently be defined as a phase of stagnation, which ended only in the next reign, Tongzhi.

Tongzhi

In 1862 official production at Jingdezhen was fully restored.

A characteristic sign of the porcelain of the restoration period (second half of the nineteenth century) is a specific pastel palette which, compared with the rich colouring of eighteenth-century painted porcelain, gives an impression of a certain blurriness, owing to the use of mixed colours, the introduction of new whitened shades and changes in painting technology. Whereas in the eighteenth century enamel colours were as a rule applied over a base layer of white pigment (arsenic-based), later painters abandoned this underlayer and painted directly on the glaze; as a result the colours lost their density and their hues their saturation.

The “boneless” technique of painting is frequently encountered. On porcelain in this style the outlines of the images are left in reserve, revealing the colour of the body or the base ground and separating the main patches of colour. This is explained by a feature of the technology: the preliminary outline was applied in ordinary water-based ink, which burned away in firing, leaving an empty trace.

Guangxu

While in the Tongzhi reign potters still experienced technological difficulties, as shown by the numerous cases of glaze flaking, in the Guangxu reign they achieved excellent quality of body, glaze and colours.

At the same time the circle of private customers widened and the role of government dictate weakened, especially in the selection of pictorial motifs. Many traditional prohibitions lost their force, including the regulation of the depiction of imperial symbols — the yellow colour and five-clawed dragons. Contrary to traditional regulation, imperial symbolism appeared even on export porcelain. In 1920 the largest export customer for Chinese porcelain was the United States.

From the late 1880s the artistic style of decoration showed itself in the use of enamels of muted colours characteristic of traditional paintings on silk. Porcelain objects with characteristic borrowings of forms and ornaments from bronzes are also found. The ornament clearly shows splayed taotie masks.

The colour scheme changed: colour became even, with calmer tonal gradations and no “strikes”; the bright contrast of pure colours gave way to a soft range of diluted or muted shades.

In the Guangxu reign, a black ground finely painted with gold floral ornament appeared on millefleurs wares. The size of objects increased and unusual forms appeared (tea containers).

The Republican period

In 1911 a revolution took place and the Republic of China was founded. From 1910 new kinds of imported enamels, shuicai (literally “water enamels”), distinguished by their purity and delicacy, came into use. This continued until the 1930s.
