Chapter 1

On Collecting Chinese Porcelain

Особенности коллекционирования китайского фарфора

Author: Nona Dmitrievna Dronova

What makes collecting Chinese porcelain so captivating?

My motivations — or what inspires me:

  1. The prospect of a sound investment. All over the world one comes across a great many genuine pieces that have been wrongly identified as imitations. In 2011, for example, an imperial Chinese vase was at first undervalued by one of the largest auction houses. It was expected to sell at Sotheby’s for 800 dollars, but its price later soared to an astronomical 18 million dollars. The catalogue had dated it as “probably Republic period”, that is, to the early twentieth century.

    This beautiful famille rose vase set off a bidding war among specialists who were convinced that the auction house had made an error of judgement and that the vase was in fact an imperial piece made during the reign of the Qianlong Emperor (1736–1795).

    Porcelain of the Song and Yuan dynasties can also be found at flea markets in Southeast Asia. For the past thousand years the region was a vast market for Chinese merchants, and because of its geographical proximity to China, antique porcelain still turns up there.

  2. A love of travel — the opportunity and the desire to visit the world’s great museums and exhibitions. Among them are the State Hermitage (St Petersburg); the Porcelain Collection in the Zwinger (Dresden); the Palace Museum (Gugong) in Beijing; the National Palace Museum in Taipei; the museum of Jingdezhen, the leading centre of Chinese porcelain production and the site of the imperial manufactory; the Tianjin Municipal Museum; the Nanjing Museum; the Guangdong Museum of Art; the art museums of Shanghai and Hong Kong; the Tianminlou collection; the Museum of Oriental Ceramics in Osaka (Japan); the Topkapı Palace Museum in Istanbul (Turkey); the Ardabil Shrine in Iran; the National Museum of Singapore; the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Percival David Collection and the British Museum (London); the Louvre, the Musée des Arts Décoratifs and the Musée Guimet (Paris); the Palace of Fontainebleau; the Royal Museums of Art and History (Brussels); the Rijksmuseum (Amsterdam); the Metropolitan Museum of Art (New York); the Freer Gallery of Art (Washington); and the Denver Art Museum.

  3. Curiosity. I have studied the private collections of J. Paul Getty, J. Pierpont Morgan, Weishaupt (Berlin), S. Kwan (Hong Kong), Sir William Burrell, George Eumorfopoulos, Sir Richard Wallace, Prince Esper Ukhtomsky, Alfred Baur, V. S. Kalabushkin and D. M. Melnikov, and came to appreciate the intellect and high culture of these people.

  4. A cultured way to spend one’s time. I studied the literature and immersed myself in the methods of research on Chinese porcelain. I analysed the catalogues and sale results of the major auction houses. Searching for comparable objects in the literature, studying museum collections and following the results of the major auctions all help one make the right decision when buying a piece.

  5. A spirit of adventure. It develops the imagination and offers a kind of return to the past. Consider, for example, the International Exhibition of Chinese Art, held for the first time in London in 1935–1936. One can only imagine the difficulties involved and the diplomatic talent that was needed to organise such a display.

  6. Compassion. The almost physical pain of thinking about the looting of the Yuanmingyuan (the Garden of Perfect Brightness) and the theft of its magnificent collection of imperial porcelain during the Opium Wars, and about the eunuchs who sold priceless imperial objects when the Qing empire collapsed.

  7. Pride and patriotism. Pride in the porcelain collections of the State Museum of Oriental Art (Moscow) and the State Hermitage (St Petersburg).

  8. Gratitude to my parents for giving me the education that allows me to truly appreciate antique Chinese porcelain.

Conclusion

The transformation of a person into a collector passes through several stages:

  1. A person buys a beautiful and interesting object “by chance”.
  2. They begin to gather information about it from the internet, magazines, films and so on, but do not yet know enough about the piece.
  3. The amateur discovers that they have bought a rare object at a modest price.
  4. They are now motivated to look for information about other objects of the same kind.
  5. They consider whether it makes sense to buy something else unusual.
  6. The interested amateur buys another piece.
  7. Now almost a collector, they decide to buy several more objects and think about it constantly.
  8. The collector loves their collection.
  9. Their life acquires a new meaning!
