Chapter 1

General Information on Nephrite and Jadeite

Общие сведения о нефрите и жадеите

Author: Nona Dmitrievna Dronova

What makes antique jade carvings so attractive?

Ancient carvings in nephrite are distinguished by unique artistic and decorative qualities and form one of the most sought-after fields in the collecting of decorative and applied art. Their appeal is rooted in the traditional aesthetic preferences of the vast population of Southeast and East Asia, and above all of China.

What should one know about nephrite as a gemstone?

Today the name nephrite is given to a rock composed of extremely fine fibres of tremolite–actinolite amphibole with a characteristic felted, interwoven structure. The exceptional strength of nephrite — up to 12,000 kg/cm² under uniaxial compression — is a consequence of this structure. Its specific gravity is 2.8–3.1 g/cm³, and its hardness on the Mohs scale is 5.0–6.5.

Nephrite used for jewellery and carving is a dense, tough, cryptocrystalline aggregate of common minerals of the hornblende group — pale tremolite and green actinolite — with the general formula Ca₂(Mg,Fe)₅[Si₄O₁₁]₂(OH)₂.

It is a stone of great toughness, wide range of colour and good translucency.

By texture, nephrites are divided into three groups: uniform, mottled, and mottled with disseminated inclusions. Each group includes decorative varieties that differ in colour and tone.

What is particular about the structure of nephrite?

Nephrite has a unique property that sets it apart from all other stones. It is a kind of “stone felt”: a matted, fibrous mass of extremely fine crystals that gives it its extraordinary toughness and strength. It consists of thin tremolite fibres gathered into bundles, which interweave closely to form a felted aggregate. Each bundle consists of 10–30 sub-parallel fibres measuring from 0.005 × 0.01 to 0.01 × 0.2 mm. The structures most often found in nephrite are fibroblastic (felted), felted-parallel-fibrous, and porphyroblastic sheaf-like with a fibroblastic groundmass.

In nephrites of different colours, tremolite, actinolite and hornblende are clearly identified as the principal constituents. The predominance of one of them, the varying degree of crystallisation and the development of secondary minerals account for the many colours and the wide variety of decorative qualities of nephrite, even where its working properties are similar. In Eastern Siberia, nephrite is mined in green — from dark green to light apple green — as well as white, grey, honey-coloured, greenish-brown (“marsh”) and black. Polymineralic, mottled nephrites in green tones are the most common; uniform nephrites of mono- or bimineralic composition are rarer.

Green nephrite is a high-grade material for jewellery and carving. It has strong green colours, from light to dark green, and is translucent in pieces up to 1.5 cm thick. Among green nephrites, Chinese carvers distinguish leaf green, lettuce green, apple green and spinach green. Green nephrites have a microfibrous structure, in which grains of an ore mineral 2–3 mm across are clearly visible; their content sometimes reaches 15 per cent. A small number of such grains does not reduce the artistic and decorative value of the stone.

In green nephrites, actinolite and hornblende play an important role alongside tremolite. The “marsh” and “tobacco” varieties contain considerable amounts of chlorite and flaky iron hydroxides.

The colour of black nephrite is caused by finely dispersed graphite. The white and whitish spots that often occur may be formed in some cases by tremolite and in others by talc or opal. An important role in the composition of nephrite is played by ore minerals (magnetite, chromian spinel), which form micro- and macro-inclusions and give rise to the mottled, spotted varieties.

White nephrite is snow-white, more often with a faint bluish, yellowish, greenish or greyish tinge. Compared with other varieties of the stone, it has the most even and finely fibrous structure.

Finely spotted nephrite is a low-grade carving material with dark, white, light grey and bright green spots.

The group of uniform nephrites is in most cases characterised by even colour and high translucency. Macroscopically, uniform nephrites are white, green in various shades, grey, black or honey-coloured.

The trade name “tobacco nephrite” is given to a mottled variety coloured greyish greenish-brown. In plates up to 1 cm thick this nephrite is translucent, and the unevenness of its colour, caused by concentrations of one mineral or another, then becomes clearly visible.

Yellow nephrite is a practically monomineralic rock with a characteristic fibroblastic structure, produced by the growth of elongated prismatic crystals (usually of amphibole) that form a matted mass of fine fibres.

Honey nephrite is yellowish-brown of varying depth and is translucent in plates up to 1 cm thick. Its structure is practically uniform; only in places is a weak zoning of colour observed.

The microstructure of yellow and honey nephrite consists of acicular (needle-like) crystals and their intergrowths — complexes of hundreds and thousands of elementary needle crystals joined together, each about 0.1–0.5 µm across. In honey nephrite, extremely thin films of iron hydroxides develop along individual microcracks and along the grain boundaries of the needle crystals; it is these films that produce the yellow colour.

The black colour of nephrite is caused by finely dispersed, dust-like graphite, which emphasises the delicately wavy, feathery and complex structure of the stone. Uniformly coloured black nephrite is rare in nature. Macroscopically it is a massive black rock that takes a good polish.

How do apocarbonate and apo-ultramafic nephrites differ?

Nature produces two types of nephrite: apocarbonate nephrites, formed from carbonate (dolomite) rocks, and apo-ultramafic nephrites, formed from ultramafic rocks (serpentinites). Both are composed of tremolite and differ in their content of accessory and secondary minerals.

The distinctive features of apocarbonate nephrites are their very rare light colours — from lettuce and olive green and pale blue to white — and the complete absence of dark and coloured minerals.

Apocarbonate nephrites are richer in magnesium and fluorine and poorer in iron. Their contents of lithium, beryllium and caesium also differ from those of apo-ultramafic nephrites.

Apocarbonate nephrites are much less widespread in nature than the apo-serpentinite industrial-genetic type. Their deposits are currently known only in China, in the foothills of the Kunlun range; in Australia, on the Eyre Peninsula; and in Russia, in the basin of the middle reaches of the Vitim River.

The range of mineral inclusions in apocarbonate nephrites is poorer; diopside, apatite, zoisite, calcite and talc have been found in them. The iron content of apocarbonate nephrites is 0.23–1.30 per cent, compared with 3.11–8.56 per cent in apo-ultramafic nephrites.

How does nephrite form in nature?

Geologists regard nephrite as a typical contact mineral, formed among dissimilar rocks through their chemical reaction with the ubiquitous aqueous solutions of the Earth’s crust. Sometimes nephrite forms in serpentinites when deep-seated olivine–pyroxene rocks are altered. But the finest pale grey nephrite grows at the boundary between magnesian marbles and granites.

The products of reactions between deep-seated brines of different composition arising in different rocks are called skarns. Under the action of alkaline solutions rich in calcium and magnesium, soluble silica is released from granite and “binds” the alkaline-earth elements into salts of silicic acids — the amphiboles.

For amphiboles to be deposited in the form of thin, hair-like crystals, a saturated solution and rapid crystallisation are evidently required. Such conditions arise rarely. Crystal growth needs space filled with a fluid containing calcium, magnesium and silicon. The optimal situation arises when xenoliths of marble in granite are dissolved by acidic granitic fluids and the resulting cavities are filled with an alkaline solution. Masses of nephrite then form in the cavities through the chemical reaction of magnesium, calcium and silicon. But the conditions of crystallisation must be very particular.

Nephrite forms at depths of 20–30 km under pressures of 40–60 kbar. The solutions there are rich in salts, supercritical and acidic, and contain carbon dioxide and fluorine (nephrites contain up to 0.6 per cent fluorine). A peculiarity of nephrite is that it is enriched in the light isotopes of water — which means that it grows in a solution enriched in the light isotopes of oxygen. The presence of light isotopes in nephrite is usually taken as evidence of ancient buried rainwater, which is always rich in light isotopes. Yet the minerals of the deep Earth are normally enriched in the heavy isotopes of oxygen and hydrogen, and deep fluids are extremely mobile. We believe it unlikely that the isotopically light water of ancient rains could have preserved its isotopic composition for billions of years.

Light isotopes always accumulate (fractionate) in the vapour–gas phase and then remain in rain or snow. Fractionation is also possible in the superheated fluids of the Earth’s depths through retrograde boiling, if zones of locally reduced pressure appear. Vapour can then form at depths of tens of kilometres. This is especially marked in clay-rich rocks.

The authors who discovered light water isotopes in the heavy minerals of crystalline schists attribute the anomalous isotopic composition of these ancient minerals (corundum, hematite, ilmenite and others) to the composition of Archaean–Proterozoic rainfall. Professor A. M. Portnov argues that the principal cause is different: the shrinkage of clay-rich strata as they are transformed into heavy minerals creates local zones of reduced pressure, in which retrograde boiling occurs and light isotopes become concentrated in the heavy minerals crystallising from the mineraliser-saturated vapour. A similar phenomenon occurs during the formation of nephrite, except that here retrograde boiling is caused by the dissolution of magnesian carbonates.

This paradoxical accumulation of light isotopes in nephrite makes it possible to explain its enigmatic felted microstructure. According to Portnov, the formation of nephrite is accompanied by a reduction in the volume of the carbonate rocks as they are dissolved by acidic solutions in a vapour saturated with silica and carbonate. The carbonates, unstable in the acidic granitic fluid, dissolve and create open space with reduced pressure and a concentration of light isotopes.

Retrograde boiling of the superheated magnesium–calcium–silicate solution creates a vapour–gas phase enriched in the light isotopes of oxygen and hydrogen. It is therefore in the gas phase that the nephrite “hairs” grow. Under these unusual conditions, hair-like crystals grow from the supersaturated mineralising vapour and are then compressed by pressure into a tough stone “felt”.

Where are the main deposits of nephrite?

Deposits of nephrite are known in Canada, the United States, New Zealand, Australia, China, Poland, Germany, Italy, Mexico, Zimbabwe, Russia, Central Asia and southern Kazakhstan.

Primary (in situ) deposits of nephrite, which are of metasomatic origin, fall into two types: deposits in alpine-type ultramafic rocks (Ospinskoye, Ulan-Khodinskoye) and deposits in dolomitic marbles (Vitimskoye, Buromskoye). In chemical composition, nephrites from ultramafic rocks differ from those of dolomitic marbles in their higher content of iron oxide (1.5 per cent and more), chromium and nickel (by two orders of magnitude), and their lower content of fluorine.

Nephrites of the first group are dense, massive, in places schistose, cryptocrystalline rocks in various shades of green with clearly visible ore inclusions. Black varieties also occur here. Among the stones of the first group there are many varieties of sub-standard colour, the so-called “tobacco” (greenish-brown) nephrite.

In nephrites associated with dolomitic marbles, ore inclusions are practically absent. These are dense, massive rocks, translucent in places and sometimes with a matt transparency, which differ from the nephrites of ultramafic rocks in their lighter colours: apple green, lettuce green, honey yellow, yellowish white and bluish. Such nephrites are an excellent material for the jewellery industry. However, later oxidation processes often give their pure light tones a brownish tinge, which considerably lowers the quality of the stone.

The exceptional richness of the nephrite deposits of Eastern Turkestan, lying along the northern and southern slopes of the Kunlun range, was already noted by A. E. Fersman.

For many centuries nephrite from Eastern Turkestan played an important role in the cultural development of China. The main centre of nephrite extraction was the city of Khotan in Eastern Turkestan. From the primary deposits in the upper reaches of the Yarkand River in the Pamirs, up to five tonnes of nephrite were sent every year to the Chinese emperor — until, the story goes, his son, who slept on a bed made of nephrite from Yarkand, fell ill. The emperor then forbade the quarrying of the green stone in the gorges of the Yarkand. From that time onward, nephrite could be gathered only from the river: slaves standing waist-deep in the water had to catch the stones rolling along the riverbed and throw them onto the bank. Nephrite was searched for with bare feet, since its pebbles are so slippery that it is simply impossible to stand on them. After its place of origin, it was often called Khotan or Kunlun nephrite.

Where are the famous nephrite placers of China?

The famous alluvial placers on the northern slopes of the Kunlun range are of commercial importance. The main nephrite placers of China lie in the valleys of the Yarkand and Khotan rivers on the northern slopes of the range. These deposits, together with the primary ones, lie within a belt extending roughly east–west for 600 km with a width of 100 km. Geologists suggest the existence of a “nephrite belt” that also embraces the north-eastern coastal regions of the country (Liaoning and Hebei provinces), the east (the Shandong Peninsula), the south-east (Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces) and the middle reaches of the Yangtze (Hubei and Hunan provinces). Through these provinces run series of deep faults in the Earth’s crust, which penetrated the carbonate sediments together with the granites and raised the interior of the Earth into the gigantic Kunlun range.

Was there a Jade Road in the history of China?

The delivery of ever greater quantities of nephrite from Eastern Turkestan required the development of permanent trade links. In the opinion of Professor Yang Boda, a Jade Road existed long before the opening of the Great Silk Road and followed approximately the same routes.

Jade culture is an inseparable part of Chinese culture. Khotan nephrite has long been famous throughout China: people knew it as early as ten thousand years ago. The Jade Road existed long before the Silk Road.

It is said that Khotan nephrite, on its way to the central regions of China, always passed through a place called Yumenguan — the Jade Gate. Even before the Han dynasty, the export of Khotan nephrite to central China was already considerable.

The main centre of nephrite extraction was the city of Khotan in Eastern Turkestan, which supplied musk and nephrite in abundance. An ancient legend, quoted by the historian of Khotan Abel-Rémusat, tells of it as follows: “The sacred river of yu flows past the city from the peaks of the Kun, or Kunlun, and at their foot it divides into three streams: one is the stream of white yu, the second of green, the third of black. Every year, when the fifth or sixth moon comes, the rivers overflow their banks and carry down from the mountains much yu, which is gathered when the water subsides. The people are forbidden to approach the banks of the river until the ruler of Khotan has come himself to make his choice.” He writes that jade is like the beauty of a young girl, and that if, at the second moon, a special radiance begins to spread over the trees and grasses, this means that yu has appeared in the river. That is why the Chinese called the city of Khotan Yutian.

New impetus to the study of the northern routes of the “Jade Road” may evidently come from the mineralogical study of samples from all the territories mentioned.

How does Turkestan nephrite differ?

Turkestan nephrite differs noticeably in colour from the nephrite of the Baikal region: it has a greyish tinge.

The high-quality grades of Khotan nephrite are white like fat, yellow like chestnut, red like a cockscomb and black like lacquer. The finest of all is considered to be the stone translucent like the fat of a fat-tailed sheep.

As to form, the most precious is nephrite in pebbles — fragments of rock rounded to varying degrees, from 1 to 15 cm in diameter. Larger rounded pieces are called cobbles and boulders. The angular fragments of nephrite are rounded by countless collisions with one another as they roll along the riverbed.

From which deposits was nephrite obtained for jewellery in pre-revolutionary Russia?

Until the 1850s, nephrite was imported into Russia from China. At that time the Peterhof Lapidary Works bought dark green nephrite at a thousand roubles per pood (about 16 kg), and the price doubled for better-quality stone.

In the mid-1850s, the geologist G. M. Permikin found boulders of nephrite in the Eastern Sayan, and later the first primary deposit was discovered. Objects made from this nephrite at the Peterhof Lapidary Works were shown at the international exhibitions in London in 1862 and Paris in 1867.

How and where was nephrite mined in the USSR?

In the USSR, especially in the post-war years, the Soyuzkvartssamotsvety production association discovered new deposits in the Eastern and Western Sayan, in Buryatia, in the north of Krasnoyarsk Territory and in the Polar Urals. In the Vitim district, a genetic type of deposit new to the USSR was discovered, associated with carbonate rocks and characterised by the extremely rare white colour of its nephrite.

What colours of nephrite are mined in Russia?

The deposits of our country yield mainly green nephrite (Eastern Sayan); white with a smoky tinge is rarer. The colour is usually uneven — cloudy, less often banded, mottled or uniform. Polished plates reveal a distinctive pattern that depends on differences in the intensity of colour and on the uneven distribution of inclusions of magnetite and chromian spinel. Many stones are translucent. Their surfaces may show iron staining caused by the oxidation of ferrous to ferric iron.

How is nephrite mined?

Sometimes nephrite is extracted directly from the riverbed. In certain places the river is artificially dammed so that its water flows faster. Workers standing waist-deep in the water stir up the bottom sediment with long poles and rakes. The clay and sand, being lighter, are carried away by the current, while the heavier stones remain on the bottom. Sands extracted from pits or from the river are further concentrated by washing: workers fill special baskets with the loose mineral-bearing material and shake them in washing pits filled with water.

What should one know about jadeite?

In the late eighteenth century China began to import jadeite from Burma (now Myanmar). The vivid green colour of Burmese jadeite brought about a major shift in the traditional market for carved stone. From that time on, imported imperial jadeite came to be even more highly esteemed than the original Chinese nephrite, and Chinese craftsmen began to make sacrificial vessels, ornaments and many other objects from it.

Jadeite is transparent to translucent, with a clearly visible vitreous lustre. Its hardness is between 6.0 and 7.0 on the Mohs scale. The colours of jadeite are more saturated than those of nephrite and include green, white, red, black and a delicate lavender.

For a long time jadeite was not distinguished from nephrite by its external features. A. E. Fersman wrote: “Here we encounter the rare phenomenon of a remarkable similarity between two minerals of different chemical composition. Jadeite is a monoclinic pyroxene, whereas nephrite is a monoclinic amphibole. Like nephrite, jadeite has a cryptocrystalline, finely fibrous structure of microscopic fibrous crystals interwoven with one another.”

Jadeite is characterised by a fine- and very fine-grained sheaf-like (broom-like) structure. The granular texture of jadeite can be seen with the naked eye or under a 10× loupe, and this makes it possible to distinguish it from nephrite.

Today practically the only supplier of jadeite to the world market is Myanmar, where the main deposits are concentrated. They have been worked for many hundreds of years and are now under state control. Interest in jadeite grows from year to year; it is sold in the United States, Canada, Indonesia, Spain, China, Japan and the Philippines and reaches the markets of Western Europe. This growing interest is explained by the fact that jadeite has become an inseparable part of Chinese culture.

How can nephrite be distinguished from jadeite by visual examination?

“Jade” is a collective term used to describe two separate minerals, nephrite and jadeite. On the market, both are called jade.

Nephrite carries deeper historical associations, while jadeite is known for its more popular emerald-green colours.

From the mineralogical point of view, true nephrite and the similar jadeite are compounds of silica with magnesium in the first case and with sodium in the second. Both stones have a greasy lustre and a wide range of colours — from milky white to grey-green, green, dark green or the bright green of spring foliage. The main differences between them are as follows:

  • Hardness: nephrite 6–6.5; jadeite 6.5–7. Nephrite is slightly softer than jadeite and has a greasy lustre.
  • Specific gravity: nephrite about 3.02; jadeite 3.3–3.5. Both can easily be distinguished from most imitations, which are usually lighter.
  • Colour: jadeite shows more variation in colour than nephrite; yellow, lavender, pink and emerald green are characteristic of it.
  • Rarity and value: the finest nephrites are white and slightly grey; the finest jadeite is translucent emerald-green jadeite from Myanmar, also known as “imperial jade”, the most valued and sought-after of these gemstones.
  • Chemistry: jadeite is a sodium aluminium silicate; nephrite is a calcium magnesium silicate.
  • Structure: nephrite has a loosely interwoven fibrous structure; jadeite has a very dense granular one.

What information about ancient nephrite deposits can be found in old books and archival documents?

In his notes Trails to Inmost Asia, George N. Roerich writes: “…deposits of magnificent nephrite are apparently scattered over an enormous stretch of the northern and southern slopes of the Kunlun, from the Raskem Darya (a tributary of the Yarkand River) in the west to the mines of the Chinese province of Gansu in the east. The stone was the object of an extensive trade, and from these centres of Eastern Turkestan it found its way into China, India, Russian Turkestan and Persia. The main centre of extraction and working of nephrite was the district of Yarkand and Khotan, from which caravans of camels carried valuable stone objects to Peking every year. Carl Ritter, on the basis of descriptions from the late eighteenth century, paints a picture of pebbles being gathered from the riverbed by workers who unerringly identified the valuable nephrite boulders on the bed of the Khotan River ‘by the slipperiness under their feet’. The richest district is that of the Karakash River (Gulbashen), where at the beginning of the nineteenth century a rich primary deposit of grey-green and pure green nephrite, lying among crystalline schists, was described. The deposits were intensively worked until 1864 and yielded an enormous quantity of very valuable material.” (Translated from the Russian edition.)

The following passages are quoted from Academician A. E. Fersman’s book Gems of Russia:

“…To the same region belong the deposits of the Raskem Darya, south of Yarkand, visited at the risk of his life by Grombchevsky in 1888 (Mushketov, 1899); fine dark green nephrite was found in them, whereas in the locality nearest to Yarkand, on the Tunga River, greenish jadeite occurs. Extraction here in the eighteenth century was so great that up to 300 poods of nephrite in pieces of various sizes were reportedly sent every year from Yarkand to the palace in Peking. These deposits lie in the Eastern Pamirs and are the westernmost occurrence of nephrite in Asia. Further east, according to the observations of K. Bogdanovich, they extend along the northern and southern slopes of the Kunlun as far as the province of Gansu. Here, in Gansu, are also the workshops for working nephrite. Another centre for working the green stone lies further south, in the Chinese province of Yunnan, which probably represents a second region where nephrite occurs. This region is a major trading centre to which valuable jadeite flows from northern Burma, and from which the stone or objects made from it are sent on to Canton. In Yunnan, soft agalmatolite from a number of deposits on the coast opposite Formosa is used to imitate jadeite. All references to nephrite deposits in Yunnan are apparently mistaken and should be attributed to Burma.”

“…Thus a continuous chain is established for the movement of nephrite, as rough stone or as finished objects, from the Khotan–Yarkand district through Kashgar or Badakhshan to Russian Turkestan, and through Gansu to the great trading centres of eastern China — Peking, Canton and Chefoo.”

“…Mongolia and Manchuria were supplied partly with stone from the Kunlun and apparently with nephrite pebbles from the Irkutsk region, from which the Soyots sold nephrite and agalmatolite from the Onot, Belaya and Biboy rivers to Chinese officials.”

“…Considerable changes in the use of this stone came in the 1860s. At that time the extraction of nephrite and jadeite in the spurs of the Kunlun ceased. The present state of the nephrite industry of China is entirely unknown to us, and in all probability its influence on the stone markets of Europe and America is very slight.”

“…Present production of nephrite is very small. The trade was undermined during the rule of Ma Fuxing, who ordered the experienced jade carvers to be taken from Khotan to Kashgar.”

“The consul in Kashgar reported to me that at present the extraction of nephrite in the Khotan (principally) and Yarkand districts is a free trade of the inhabitants, on condition, however, that the stones they find are shown to the Chinese authorities before being sold to anyone, and that the authorities buy the best specimens for themselves. The highest grades are considered to be those obtained from the Karakash River in the Khotan district, namely stones of a milky translucent colour with yellow specks and veining; the price of such a stone, weighing about 3 pounds, reaches 40 yambu (about 5,000 roubles), while stones of a more common colour but of high grade cost in Kashgar no less than two yambu, that is, about 260 paper roubles.”

“…In the markets of Kuldja, nephrite of the highest grades is not found at all.” (Archive of the Imperial Appanages Department of Russia.)

“…In 1889 the Peterhof Lapidary Works undertook, through Poklevsky-Kozell, the purchase of nephrite in Khotan and Yarkand; the engineer Komarov succeeded in obtaining 213 specimens of stone of various colours from the Karakash and Yurungkash rivers and from the upper reaches of the former near Mount Merdjai. The whole expedition cost the treasury about 17,000 roubles.”
